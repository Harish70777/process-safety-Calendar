## What Happened

Four Formosa employees were replacing an empty one-ton chlorine container with a full one at the facility's Ethylene Glycol unit, where chlorine gas was used as a biocide in the cooling water treatment system. As a worker disconnected what was believed to be the empty container from the process equipment, chlorine gas escaped — the container was not actually empty at all, still holding 1,250 pounds of chlorine, 62.5 percent of its original inventory. Because the container was believed empty, the worker wasn't wearing respiratory protection. Three attempts to stop the release failed before an emergency responder was able to close the container's vapor valve, roughly 50 minutes after the release began. The worker who disconnected the container was flown to the hospital with respiratory difficulties from chlorine exposure. Approximately 10 pounds of chlorine gas escaped.

## Root Causes

- **No way to verify the container was actually empty:** there was no instrumentation, such as a scale, allowing workers to confirm a chlorine container's actual contents before disconnecting it — the belief it was empty was simply wrong, and nothing in the process caught that.
- **Standard practice didn't require respiratory protection during container changes:** because containers were assumed empty at this stage, protective equipment wasn't required — a reasonable-seeming practice given the assumption, but one with no verification behind it.
- **A more hazardous chemical was used where a safer alternative existed:** CSB noted that using bleach instead of chlorine gas for this cooling water application could have prevented the incident's hazard potential entirely, regardless of the container-weight issue.

## Lessons Learned

1. "Believed empty" is not the same as "verified empty" — any process handling containers of hazardous gas needs a positive verification method (like a scale) rather than relying on assumption or expected sequence of use.
2. Respiratory protection requirements should be based on the actual possible hazard of a task, not solely on the expected state of equipment — building in protection as a default for chlorine container work, regardless of assumed contents, would have meant this exposure didn't happen even with the verification gap present.
3. Inherently safer design — substituting a lower-hazard chemical (like bleach) for a task that doesn't strictly require the more dangerous option (chlorine gas) — removes an entire category of risk rather than managing around it. Formosa's own post-incident corrective actions specifically included pursuing exactly this kind of substitution.
4. A container-changing task performed routinely can develop assumptions that go unchallenged for a long time — this incident is a reminder to periodically ask "how do we actually know this is true" about routine operational beliefs, not just "how have we always done this."
