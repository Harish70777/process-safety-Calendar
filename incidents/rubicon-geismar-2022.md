## What Happened

A rupture disc on process equipment at Rubicon's facility, a joint venture between Huntsman and Lanxess, activated prematurely, releasing 201 pounds of hot methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI) at roughly 400 degrees Fahrenheit. The disc was designed to activate at 30 pounds per square inch, but process data showed it actually opened at about 13 psi. The equipment it protected normally operated under vacuum; at the time, it was running at a somewhat elevated pressure due to an unrelated problem in the system that maintained those vacuum conditions, but that pressure was still within the equipment's safe operating limits. When the rupture disc opened anyway, the hot mixture of liquid and vapor MDI discharged into vertically-oriented piping that ended only about eight inches above a solid concrete floor. Because the system had been operating within safe limits, workers hadn't been restricted from the area near the discharge piping. Two contract maintenance workers installing a pump nearby were sprayed when the high-velocity mixture hit the floor and dispersed, and both were hospitalized with thermal burns.

## Root Causes

- **The rupture disc activated well below its design pressure:** at roughly 13 psi against a 30 psi design activation point, the device opened in a scenario it wasn't expected to trigger for, releasing material under conditions the facility hadn't specifically prepared for.
- **The relief discharge point directed hot material toward a hazard, not away from one:** piping ending just eight inches above a solid floor gave a high-velocity two-phase release nowhere to go but to spray outward across the surrounding area.
- **A known hazard from the facility's own hazard analysis wasn't mitigated:** Rubicon's process hazard analysis had already identified personnel exposure to hot MDI as a potential consequence of this rupture disc activating. The company hadn't acted on that identified risk before this incident occurred.
- **Workers were present because the system appeared to be operating safely:** since pressure was within normal safe operating limits, there was no restriction on non-essential personnel working near the discharge piping, even though the consequence of an unexpected relief event had already been identified as hazardous.

## Lessons Learned

1. A pressure relief device that activates below its design threshold indicates either a device problem or an inadequately understood process condition. Either way, it's a signal worth investigating rather than simply accepting as anomalous.
2. Relief and rupture disc discharge piping needs to route hazardous material to a genuinely safe location. Discharging just above a hard floor, where a two-phase mixture can spray outward on impact, defeats the purpose of directing the material away from people in the first place.
3. A hazard already identified in your own process hazard analysis needs a documented mitigation, not just documentation that it exists. Rubicon's PHA correctly flagged this exact exposure risk, but flagging it wasn't the same as controlling it.
4. Restricting non-essential personnel from an area shouldn't depend solely on whether current operating conditions are "within safe limits." If a hazard analysis has identified what happens when something like a relief device unexpectedly activates, that finding should inform access restrictions regardless of routine operating status.
