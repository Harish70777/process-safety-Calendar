## What Happened

An aboveground storage tank holding liquid urea ammonium nitrate (UAN) fertilizer catastrophically failed, splitting open vertically and releasing roughly 2.1 million gallons of fertilizer almost instantaneously. A welder and his helper working on the opposite side of the tank at the time were caught in the collapse and immersed in the released liquid fertilizer, sustaining serious injuries; employees from a neighboring business rescued both of them. The released fertilizer overtopped the tank's secondary containment berm, flooded a nearby road and blocked egress for more than 100 employees at an adjacent business, and reached portions of a nearby residential neighborhood, requiring soil remediation. At least 200,000 gallons of the spilled fertilizer were never accounted for, with some reaching the nearby Elizabeth River. Following the incident, CSB issued an urgent recommendation for immediate risk reduction on three other tanks at the same facility — one located only about 250 feet from the residential neighborhood — prompting the company to significantly reduce maximum liquid levels in its remaining tanks while a full evaluation was completed.

## Root Causes

- **Welding defects in the tank's original construction:** CSB's investigation pointed to flaws in the tank's welded construction as the structural root of the failure — a defect present from construction that went undetected through the tank's operating life.
- **No state regulation specifically governing large fertilizer storage tank design, construction, maintenance, or inspection:** at the time, Virginia had no regulatory framework requiring oversight of tanks like this one, despite the clear public safety consequence a catastrophic failure could carry — CSB explicitly recommended the Commonwealth establish or authorize such regulation as a result of this incident.
- **Secondary containment inadequate for the volume released:** the containment berm surrounding the tank was overtopped by the scale of the release, meaning the engineered safeguard meant to contain exactly this kind of failure was insufficient to the actual failure magnitude.
- **A broader pattern across the industry:** CSB's investigation identified sixteen other similar tank failures at nine different facilities across multiple states between 1995 and 2008 — this wasn't an isolated construction defect but part of a recurring, industry-wide pattern of large storage tank failures.

## Lessons Learned

1. Original construction quality — particularly welding integrity on large storage tanks — needs verification methods capable of catching latent defects, not just visual inspection after the fact; a flaw present since construction can remain hidden for years before manifesting as catastrophic failure.
2. Secondary containment sizing needs to account for the full credible worst-case release volume, not just routine leak scenarios — this incident's containment berm was overwhelmed by the actual failure it was meant to catch.
3. A regulatory gap in tank design, construction, and inspection oversight is a real risk factor at the jurisdiction level — this incident's most durable outcome was CSB explicitly calling for the state to close that gap, since voluntary industry practice alone hadn't prevented this or the sixteen other similar failures found elsewhere.
4. Facility siting matters even for storage-only operations: a tank containing millions of gallons of hazardous material sited near a residential neighborhood carries real consequence potential that deserves proactive risk reduction — exactly what CSB's urgent recommendation on the facility's remaining tanks was meant to address before another failure occurred.
