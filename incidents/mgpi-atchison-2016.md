## What Happened

A tanker truck from a chemical distribution company arrived at the MGPI Processing plant — which produces distilled spirits and specialty wheat proteins — to make a routine delivery of sulfuric acid. A facility operator escorted the driver to the loading area and unlocked the gate along with the sulfuric acid fill line, but did not notice that the adjacent sodium hypochlorite fill line was also already unlocked. The two fill lines looked similar, were not clearly marked to distinguish them, and sat only about 18 inches apart. The driver connected the sulfuric acid delivery hose to the sodium hypochlorite line by mistake, and thousands of gallons of sulfuric acid flowed into the facility's sodium hypochlorite tank. The two chemicals reacted, producing a dense toxic cloud containing chlorine gas that spread beyond the facility. Four MGPI employees, the delivery driver, and more than 140 members of the surrounding community sought medical attention, with several hospitalized; local authorities ordered roughly 11,000 residents to shelter in place while the plume dispersed.

## Root Causes

- **Visually similar, inadequately marked fill lines positioned close together:** the sulfuric acid and sodium hypochlorite connection points looked alike and sat only about 18 inches apart, creating a real risk of exactly the kind of mix-up that occurred.
- **Both lines were left unlocked simultaneously:** the facility's lock-based control meant to ensure only the intended line could be accessed failed in practice — the operator unlocked the correct line but didn't notice the other was already unlocked too.
- **No remote or automatic shutdown capability:** once the incorrect connection was made and chemicals began mixing, there was no engineered system to quickly stop the transfer before a large quantity of material had already combined.
- **Chemical unloading procedures did not adequately prevent this failure mode:** CSB's investigation found the facility's procedures for this high-risk delivery operation had shortcomings in how they verified correct connections before transfer began.

## Lessons Learned

1. Fill lines and connection points for incompatible chemicals need physically distinct designs (different fitting types, colors, clear permanent labeling) — visual similarity between connections handling reactive chemicals is a design flaw waiting to cause a mix-up, not a minor inconvenience.
2. A locking system only provides protection if it reliably prevents access to the wrong line — verify locking procedures actually achieve "only the correct line can be connected," not just "the correct line has been unlocked."
3. High-consequence, routine-seeming operations like tanker deliveries deserve the same procedural rigor as complex process operations — this incident is a clear case of a "simple" delivery task carrying catastrophic potential that wasn't matched by the safeguards in place.
4. Remote or automatic shutdown capability for chemical transfer operations can be the difference between a caught mistake and thousands of gallons of incompatible chemicals combining — build in a way to stop a transfer quickly once something looks wrong, not just prevention at the connection stage.
