## What Happened

A junior propane service technician employed by Appalachian Heating arrived at the Little General convenience store in Ghent, West Virginia, to transfer propane service from an existing tank to a newly installed replacement tank. The existing 500-gallon tank had been installed in 1994 directly against the store's exterior back wall, in violation of both West Virginia and OSHA regulations requiring greater separation. When the technician removed a plug from the existing tank's liquid withdrawal valve, propane unexpectedly and uncontrollably released; the valve, later testing confirmed, had malfunctioned. The technician, who had only 45 days of limited on-the-job training and was working unsupervised, called his offsite supervisor for guidance rather than immediately evacuating the area. Propane gas drifted into the store through the building's restroom ventilation system. The Ghent Volunteer Fire Department was dispatched to a reported gas leak; a fire captain, a firefighter, and two emergency medical technicians arrived at the scene and, along with the propane technicians, remained near the tank rather than ordering an evacuation. Four store employees stayed inside after posting a sign reading "Store closed due to gas leak." Nearly thirty minutes passed between the start of the release and ignition. At 10:53 a.m., the fire captain told the firefighter, "Make sure everybody's out, okay?" Before the firefighter could act, the accumulated propane ignited from an undetermined source and the store exploded, leveling the building and destroying a responding ambulance. Two propane technicians, the volunteer fire captain, and an emergency medical technician were killed. The four store employees survived with serious injuries; two additional responders were also injured. A fifth person among the critically injured later died from complications three years afterward.

## Root Causes

- **The propane tank was sited in violation of required clearance:** installed directly against the store's exterior wall since 1994, the tank sat far closer to the building than West Virginia and OSHA regulations require, giving released gas a direct path into the structure.
- **A malfunctioning valve caused an uncontrolled release:** CSB's testing of the liquid withdrawal valve found it was the likely source of the large propane release, failing in a way the technician had no way to anticipate.
- **The technician had inadequate training and was working unsupervised:** with only 45 days of limited on-the-job experience, he lacked the training to recognize the severity of the situation or to know that immediate evacuation, rather than a phone call for guidance, was the appropriate response.
- **Emergency responders were not trained to recognize the need for immediate evacuation:** West Virginia only required a one-time introductory hazardous materials course for firefighters, with no mandated refresher training; the responding fire captain had received hazmat training only once, nearly a decade earlier.
- **No one present ordered an evacuation during the nearly thirty minutes before ignition:** technicians and responders alike remained near the leaking tank rather than clearing the area, a decision CSB attributed directly to insufficient training on recognizing when a propane release demands immediate evacuation.

## Lessons Learned

1. Facility siting requirements for hazardous equipment, like the minimum clearance between a propane tank and a building, exist specifically to limit worst-case consequences. A tank installed against an exterior wall gave this release a direct path into an occupied building.
2. Junior technicians working with hazardous materials need genuine supervision, not just brief initial training, especially for tasks where an unexpected equipment failure could create an immediate life-safety emergency.
3. The default response to any significant, ongoing hazardous material release should be immediate evacuation, not an attempt to first understand or manage the situation from within the hazard zone. This incident's clearest lesson is that thirty minutes were available and used for the wrong response.
4. Emergency responder training on hazardous materials needs to be recurring, not a single introductory course completed years earlier. A fire captain's decade-old hazmat training left him without current, actionable knowledge exactly when it mattered most.
5. When technicians, responders, and civilians are all present at a developing hazardous materials incident, someone needs clear authority and instruction to order an evacuation early, rather than everyone individually assessing risk in the moment without a defined trigger for when to leave.
