## What Happened

At approximately 11:50 a.m. on February 14, 1991, a storage facility for fireworks operated by MS Vuurwerk BV exploded in Culemborg, Netherlands, near the border with the municipality of Vianen. Investigation found the explosion most likely originated in one storage room and was immediately followed by a second explosion in three adjacent rooms. Two people, the owner's daughter and son-in-law, were killed, and 20 to 30 others were injured. Roofs were lifted, doors dislodged, and windows shattered across the surrounding area; roughly 30 buildings were damaged or destroyed, with property damage estimated at approximately 4 million guilders. The facility was buffered by the Diefdijk levee and surrounded by open, largely uninhabited landscape, which prevented further loss of life and additional property destruction that a more populated setting would have suffered. The exact ignition source of the fire that preceded the explosion was never conclusively determined. However, TNO's investigation found the stored fireworks behaved very differently than expected for their official 1.3 hazard classification, exploding rather than burning as that classification would predict, and found that storage weight limits, capped at 200 kilograms per workshop, may have been a relevant factor in the explosion's unexpected severity. Following the disaster, TNO developed recommendations for stricter fireworks production and storage safety precautions, but a working group formed to revise the relevant regulatory directive was never given funding or staffing by the responsible ministries, and the recommendations were never implemented before the far larger Enschede fireworks disaster nine years later.

## Root Causes

- **The specific ignition source was never determined:** despite thorough forensic investigation, investigators could not conclusively identify what started the initial fire that led to the explosion.
- **The stored fireworks behaved inconsistently with their official hazard classification:** TNO's investigation found the fireworks exploded rather than behaving as their 1.3 hazard category would predict, indicating the classification system didn't accurately capture the material's actual risk under these storage conditions.
- **Storage weight limits may have contributed to the explosion's severity:** the facility's storage rooms were subject to specific weight caps, and TNO's analysis suggested exceeding or approaching these limits may have been a contributing factor to how the explosion unfolded.
- **Resulting safety recommendations were never funded or implemented:** a regulatory working group formed specifically to revise fireworks safety directives following this disaster received no funding or personnel from the responsible government ministries, leaving the identified gaps unaddressed for years.

## Lessons Learned

1. Hazard classification systems for explosive or pyrotechnic materials need periodic validation against how the material actually behaves in real storage and fire conditions, not just theoretical or laboratory-derived category assignments, given how differently these fireworks performed compared to their official classification.
2. Storage weight and quantity limits for hazardous materials need active enforcement and verification, not just documented limits that may or may not reflect actual conditions on site.
3. Safety recommendations generated by a thorough technical investigation are only as valuable as the institutional follow-through behind them. A working group with no funding or staffing cannot implement change, regardless of how sound its underlying recommendations are.
4. This disaster stands as one of the clearest, most consequential examples in the historical record of an unaddressed warning: the exact safety gaps identified here directly preceded and, according to later analysis, directly enabled the far more catastrophic 2000 Enschede fireworks disaster also documented in this database.
