## What Happened

In the refinery's sulfuric acid alkylation unit, an operator was working to remove an inoperable gearbox from a manually operated plug valve on a line carrying flammable isobutane, a routine task, since the refinery's standard practice was to replace rather than repair these gearboxes when they failed. Removing the gearbox on this particular valve design required unscrewing horizontal screws that attached it to a mounting bracket, but the operator instead removed four vertical screws that actually secured the valve's pressure-retaining top-cap to the valve body itself. When the operator subsequently tried to open the valve with a pipe wrench, the improperly secured assembly came apart and released isobutane, which rapidly formed a vapor cloud. The cloud reached a welding machine operating about 70 feet away within roughly 30 seconds and ignited into a large fireball, severely burning four workers, one ExxonMobil employee and three contractors, who were unable to escape the vapor cloud before it ignited. Emergency responders isolated the release and extinguished the fire about 25 minutes after ignition. CSB later found that only about 15 of the roughly 500 manually operated plug valves in the unit, some 3%, shared this particular design, which created the potential for this exact kind of incorrect gearbox removal.

## Root Causes

- **The wrong bolts were removed from the valve:** the operator, performing what both ExxonMobil and OSHA considered a routine operations task, mistakenly removed the screws securing the valve's pressure-containing top-cap rather than the ones attaching the gearbox to its mounting bracket.
- **A hazardous minority valve design mixed in among hundreds of others:** the specific design that made this mistake possible existed on only about 3% of the unit's plug valves, meaning the same task performed correctly hundreds of other times could still go catastrophically wrong on this small subset without any additional warning.
- **An accepted practice never received a formal hazard analysis:** CSB found that removing gearboxes to manually operate malfunctioning plug valves was a widely accepted, routine practice in the alkylation unit, but this practice had never been evaluated through a proper safety hazard analysis to identify exactly the failure mode that occurred.
- **Long-standing reliability issues with the gearboxes themselves:** CSB's investigation noted this refinery had experienced ongoing reliability problems with these plug valve gearboxes, which was part of why manual removal-and-bypass had become a routine, accepted response rather than a rare exception.

## Lessons Learned

1. A task performed correctly hundreds of times doesn't mean it's actually safe if even a small minority of the equipment involved has a different, more hazardous design. This incident's whole causal chain traces back to roughly 3% of valves looking similar enough to the other 97% to invite the same handling, with catastrophically different consequences.
2. Any "accepted practice", especially one that emerged informally in response to recurring equipment problems, deserves a formal safety hazard analysis specifically because its routine, everyday nature is exactly what lets a serious risk hide in plain sight.
3. Equipment with a design variant capable of causing a different failure mode needs to be clearly and physically distinguishable from the standard version, not left to operator memory or visual similarity to catch the difference under normal working conditions.
4. Recurring equipment reliability problems (like the gearbox issues here) that lead to informal workaround practices are themselves a process safety signal worth addressing at the root. Treating the underlying unreliable equipment, not just training around it, removes the need for a risky workaround altogether.
