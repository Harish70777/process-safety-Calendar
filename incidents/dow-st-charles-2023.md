## What Happened

On January 17, 2023, at 10:45 p.m., approximately 670 gallons of hot boiler feedwater were accidentally released and pooled at the Union Carbide Corporation St. Charles Operations facility, a Dow Chemical subsidiary, in Hahnville, Louisiana. One employee was seriously injured after stepping into the pool of hot water. The sequence began eight days earlier, when site personnel planned maintenance on a control valve in the boiler feedwater piping system and discovered water leaking past two valves upstream of it. To allow the maintenance to proceed, personnel opened two bleed valves in series between the leaking valves and attached a hose to route the leaking hot feedwater to a nearby sump. After maintenance was completed on January 17, operators worked to recommission the control valve and return it to service. In doing so, they did not use the site's operational readiness checklist, which specifically required personnel to walk down the piping and valve lineup to verify correct positioning before startup. As a result, when hot feedwater was reintroduced to the system, the two bleed valves remained open and the temporary hose remained connected, and hot water began releasing through them. A control board operator noticed a low boiler feedwater pressure alarm and dispatched an outside operator to investigate; personnel identified the leak and closed an upstream valve to stop it. About an hour later, an outside operator attempting to close the two open bleed valves inadvertently stepped into a depression where the released hot water had pooled, submerging his lower leg above the top of his rubber boot and sustaining burns to his lower leg and foot.

## Root Causes

- **The boiler feedwater system was started up with bleed valves left open:** the temporary bleed valves installed to route leaking water during maintenance were never closed before the system was recommissioned and reintroduced to full pressure and flow.
- **A temporary hose remained connected to the piping during startup:** along with the open valves, the hose used to route the maintenance-period leak was never removed before the system returned to service.
- **The operational readiness checklist was not used:** the site's own procedure specifically required a physical walk-down of the piping and valve lineup before startup, a step that would very likely have caught both the open valves and the still-connected hose, but this step was skipped.
- **An operator stepped into a pool of hot water that had accumulated in a depression:** while trying to close the open valves after the leak was discovered, the operator was injured by hot water that had already collected in a low area, a hazard that may not have been immediately visible.

## Lessons Learned

1. Operational readiness checklists exist specifically to catch exactly this kind of gap between a temporary maintenance configuration and the equipment's normal operating state, and skipping this step removes the one systematic check designed to catch it.
2. Temporary modifications made during maintenance, like bleed valves and hoses installed to manage a leak, need to be explicitly tracked and verified as removed or restored before returning equipment to service, not assumed to be handled as part of general recommissioning.
3. Recognizing and marking hazardous accumulation areas, like a depression where hot water had pooled, needs attention during emergency response to a leak, since the response to stop a leak can itself create a secondary hazard for responders working in the area.
4. Any procedure requiring a physical verification step, like a walk-down before startup, needs organizational reinforcement strong enough that personnel don't treat it as optional when time pressure or the routine nature of a task makes skipping it seem reasonable.
