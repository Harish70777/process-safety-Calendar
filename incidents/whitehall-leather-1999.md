## What Happened

A truck driver arrived at Whitehall Leather Company in Whitehall, Michigan, during the night shift to deliver a load of sodium hydrosulfide solution. During prior deliveries on that same shift, the shift supervisor had only ever received a different chemical the plant commonly called "pickle acid," which was actually ferrous sulfate despite the informal name. The supervisor assumed this new delivery of sodium hydrosulfide was also pickle acid and directed the driver to unload it into the facility's pickle acid tank. As the sodium hydrosulfide solution was pumped into the tank already containing ferrous sulfate, the two chemicals reacted and produced hydrogen sulfide gas, a highly toxic substance. The truck driver was exposed to the gas and died; one Whitehall Leather employee was also injured. Neither ferrous sulfate nor sodium hydrosulfide carried an NFPA hazard rating, and neither was a chemical listed under OSHA's Process Safety Management standard, leaving this specific chemical combination without any regulatory requirement forcing formal hazard evaluation before the incident occurred.

## Root Causes

- **Chemicals were unloaded based on assumption rather than verification:** the shift supervisor directed the delivery based on his prior experience with different deliveries on that shift, without confirming what the truck driver had actually brought.
- **An informal onsite nickname created confusion about chemical identity:** the tank's contents were commonly referred to as "pickle acid," but this name didn't correspond to the chemical's actual identity, ferrous sulfate, creating a naming gap that made a mismatch easier to miss.
- **No verification step existed before unloading a delivered chemical:** the facility had no procedure requiring confirmation that an incoming delivery matched the intended receiving tank before transfer began.
- **The specific hazard of mixing these two chemicals wasn't recognized in advance:** because neither chemical carried an NFPA rating or fell under OSHA PSM coverage, this particular incompatibility had not been identified through any formal regulatory or internal hazard evaluation process before the fatal delivery occurred.

## Lessons Learned

1. Any chemical delivery needs positive verification against the receiving tank's actual, correct contents before unloading begins, not reliance on an operator's memory of what previous deliveries on a given shift typically contained.
2. Informal, colloquial names for chemicals used at a facility ("pickle acid" for ferrous sulfate here) can create genuine confusion when they don't match the chemical's actual identity. Clear, accurate labeling that matches the chemical's real name reduces this risk.
3. Chemical incompatibility hazards exist independent of whether either individual chemical carries a high hazard rating or falls under a specific regulatory program. This incident demonstrates that a fatal reaction can occur between two chemicals neither considered independently high-risk by existing rating systems.
4. Facilities receiving bulk chemical deliveries need a documented verification procedure covering every delivery, not just chemicals perceived as unusually hazardous, since the danger in this incident came specifically from an unexpected combination rather than either chemical alone.
