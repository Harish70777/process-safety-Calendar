## What Happened

At approximately 9:30 a.m. on May 15, 2023, a fire broke out at Marathon Petroleum's Galveston Bay Refinery in Texas City, Texas, involving one of the facility's three gasoline-making ultraformers, specifically the 75,000 barrel-per-day Ultraformer 3 unit, the largest of the site's two reformers used to convert refining byproducts into octane-boosting gasoline components. According to sources familiar with the incident, the fire began when workers were performing routine maintenance and a seal failed on the unit. A subsequent investigation identified the actual source of the explosion as a leaking pump; Marathon had flagged this pump as needing maintenance, but that work, along with other upkeep, had been deferred as the company prioritized maximizing production. Had the maintenance proceeded as originally planned, a crack in the pump would likely have been identified and the fire prevented. A 55-year-old machinist was killed in the fire, and several other contract workers sustained burns to their wrists, hands, and faces. This was the refinery's worst tragedy since 2005, when the same facility, then owned by BP, was the site of one of the deadliest refinery disasters in U.S. history, killing 15 workers and injuring more than 170 others.

## Root Causes

- **A pump already flagged for maintenance was allowed to keep running:** Marathon had identified this specific pump as needing repair, but the maintenance was postponed rather than carried out promptly.
- **Maintenance was deferred to prioritize production:** the decision to delay this and other planned upkeep reflected a broader push to maximize output during a period of strong refining margins, a pattern reported across the industry during the same period.
- **A crack in the pump went undetected because planned inspection didn't happen:** had the deferred maintenance occurred on schedule, the crack that ultimately caused the leak would very likely have been identified and addressed before it led to a release and fire.
- **A seal failure during maintenance work released flammable process fluid:** the immediate trigger for the fire was a seal failure on the reformer unit, which ignited and caused the fatal blaze.

## Lessons Learned

1. Maintenance identified as necessary through a facility's own inspection process needs to be scheduled and completed on a timeline that reflects the actual risk, not deferred indefinitely based on production priorities or favorable market conditions.
2. Decisions to postpone flagged maintenance during periods of high demand or strong margins deserve explicit risk evaluation weighing the deferred safety work against the financial incentive to keep running, not an assumption that flagged equipment will hold until a more convenient time.
3. This incident occurring at the same refinery as the catastrophic 2005 BP Texas City disaster is a stark reminder that a facility's history doesn't guarantee institutional learning carries forward through ownership changes, since Marathon acquired this site in 2013, eight years after the earlier disaster.
4. Equipment known to be degraded, once flagged through any inspection or monitoring process, represents an identified risk that exists until it's actually addressed. Treating a known issue as managed simply because it's been logged, without following through on the repair, provides no real protection.
