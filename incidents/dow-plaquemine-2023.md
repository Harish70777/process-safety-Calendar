## What Happened

At approximately 9:15 p.m. on July 14, 2023, a series of explosions and fires occurred at Dow Chemical Company's Louisiana Operations Glycol II plant in Plaquemine, Louisiana. The explosions caused significant damage to nearby process equipment, and the resulting fire burned for nearly two days before it was fully extinguished. More than 31,000 pounds of ethylene oxide, a reactive, flammable chemical and known human carcinogen, were released into the atmosphere during the incident. Local authorities issued a shelter-in-place order affecting hundreds of nearby residents while the situation was brought under control. No injuries or fatalities resulted from the incident, though CSB's investigation noted that circumstances could have produced a significantly more severe outcome. In the four years before this explosion, the Plaquemine facility had already experienced ten separate emergency incidents, with half of those occurring at the same Glycol II unit where this explosion originated.

## Root Causes

- **A pattern of ten prior emergency incidents at the same facility went unresolved:** in the four years leading up to this explosion, the Plaquemine site had already experienced ten separate emergency incidents, a frequency CSB's investigation treated as a clear signal of underlying, unaddressed process safety gaps.
- **Half of those prior incidents specifically involved the Glycol II unit:** the unit where this explosion occurred had already shown a recurring pattern of problems before the July 2023 incident, indicating this specific process presented an elevated, previously demonstrated risk.
- **The explosion and resulting fire burned for nearly two days:** the scale and duration of this event reflects a release and fire that significantly exceeded routine process upsets, consistent with a underlying equipment or process failure of real severity.
- **A large quantity of a known carcinogen was released with limited advance warning capacity:** more than 31,000 pounds of ethylene oxide escaped during the incident, a toxic release of a scale that required an immediate shelter-in-place order covering hundreds of residents.

## Lessons Learned

1. A facility experiencing repeated emergency incidents, ten in four years here, needs a genuine root-cause-level response, not incident-by-incident firefighting. A pattern this frequent at the same unit is a strong leading indicator of a more serious failure eventually occurring.
2. When half of a facility's incident history clusters around one specific unit, that unit deserves prioritized, focused hazard review rather than being treated the same as the rest of the facility, since its own track record already identifies it as elevated risk.
3. Community shelter-in-place capability needs to scale with the realistic worst-case release from a facility's inventory. A 31,000-pound release of a known carcinogen affecting hundreds of residents underscores how significant offsite consequences can be even when no one is injured on-site.
4. The absence of injuries or fatalities in an incident of this scale reflects fortunate circumstances as much as effective safety systems. CSB's own framing noted the potential for a substantially worse outcome, a reminder that a "no injury" result doesn't necessarily mean the underlying hazard was well controlled.
