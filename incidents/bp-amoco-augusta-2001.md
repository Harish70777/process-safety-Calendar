## What Happened

BP Amoco Polymers produced Amodel, a high-performance nylon thermoplastic, at its Augusta, Georgia facility. During an attempted production startup, downstream mechanical problems forced operators to abort the process; an unusually large amount of partially reacted waste plastic was diverted into a polymer catch tank designed to receive exactly this kind of overflow material. Rather than remaining inert, the diverted plastic continued reacting and decomposing inside the closed 500-gallon vessel, generating gas and causing the contents to foam. The expanding foam pushed into connecting pipes and emergency vents, where it solidified and blocked them, including a pressure gauge that would otherwise have shown the vessel's true condition. With its normal pressure relief paths obstructed, the tank pressurized without any visible warning sign to workers. Three employees, Heinrich Kohl, John Rowland, and George Sanders, approached the vessel to address what they believed was a routine plugged drain and began unbolting the vessel's cover to clean it out, unaware the tank behind it was under significant pressure. When enough bolts were removed, the remaining ones gave way and the cover blew off violently, expelling hot plastic that struck the three workers and ruptured nearby tubing; hot fluid escaping from the broken tubing ignited, causing a fire. Two workers died at the scene, and the third was pronounced dead shortly after at the hospital.

## Root Causes

- **The reactive hazard of decomposing plastic in the catch tank was never recognized:** BP Amoco had not conducted a formal hazard analysis of how the catch tank behaved under abnormal conditions, like receiving an aborted-startup overflow, and so never identified that the material could continue reacting and pressurizing the vessel.
- **Operating procedures didn't account for this specific scenario:** because the reactive decomposition hazard wasn't recognized, procedures for opening and cleaning the catch tank never included steps to verify the vessel was actually depressurized first.
- **Prior near-miss incidents were not adequately investigated:** CSB found the facility had experienced earlier signs of the same underlying problem, plugged drains and minor fires involving this equipment, without those incidents being investigated closely enough to reveal the thermal decomposition hazard driving them.
- **A blocked pressure gauge hid the vessel's true condition:** solidified foam had obstructed the instrumentation that would normally have shown workers the tank was pressurized, removing the one direct warning sign available before they began opening it.

## Lessons Learned

1. Equipment designed to receive abnormal or diverted process material, like this catch tank, needs its own dedicated hazard analysis covering exactly those abnormal conditions, not just the vessel's behavior under routine, expected use.
2. Prior near-miss incidents involving the same equipment, even ones that seem minor, like a plugged drain, deserve genuine investigation into their underlying cause. Here, that investigation would very likely have surfaced the same reactive decomposition hazard before it became fatal.
3. Never assume a vessel is at atmospheric pressure before opening it, especially if there's any indication of an abnormal process history, like an aborted startup or unusual material diversion. Physical verification matters more than assumption.
4. Instrumentation critical to worker safety decisions, like a pressure gauge on equipment about to be opened, needs a way to detect when it's obstructed or otherwise not providing a reliable reading. A blocked gauge that simply shows nothing unusual can be more dangerous than no gauge at all, since it creates false confidence.
