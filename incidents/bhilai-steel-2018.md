## What Happened

At approximately 10:30 to 10:45 a.m. on October 9, 2018, an explosion occurred in a gas pipeline connected to the Coke Oven Battery Complex No. 11 at the Bhilai Steel Plant, operated by the state-owned Steel Authority of India Limited (SAIL) in Chhattisgarh, India. The pipeline had reportedly shown uneven pressure for two days before the incident, and a scheduled maintenance job was underway to address it. According to workers on site, the maintenance was intended to follow established protocol, but when the joints in the pipeline were opened, the coke oven gas ignited, causing a fire and explosion approximately 400 feet above the ground. More than 24 employees, including contract workers, were present at the site when the explosion occurred. Nine people were killed, most of the victims burned so severely that identification required DNA testing, and 14 others were injured, several critically. This was the second major fatal gas-related incident at the same plant in just over four years; a June 2014 gas leak at the facility's water pump house had already killed six employees, including two deputy general managers, and affected roughly 30 to 40 others.

## Root Causes

- **The pipeline had shown uneven pressure for two days before the incident:** this abnormal condition was identified and was the reason maintenance work was scheduled, but the underlying cause of the pressure irregularity was not resolved before workers began physically opening the pipeline's joints.
- **Maintenance was performed by opening joints on a coke oven gas line:** the specific maintenance action, opening pipeline joints, directly exposed workers to the flammable gas inside at the moment the pipeline failed or ignited.
- **A previously fatal gas incident at the same facility had already occurred:** the June 2014 water pump house gas leak, which killed six people, represented a directly relevant prior incident at the same plant involving hazardous gas exposure to personnel.
- **Worker and union accounts suggest safety measures had been repeatedly requested but not fully implemented:** trade unions reported having made repeated requests for improved safety at the plant, and workers indicated the disaster could have been avoided had adequate safety measures been in place.

## Lessons Learned

1. An identified abnormal condition, like pipeline pressure that has been uneven for multiple days, needs its root cause diagnosed and resolved before workers physically intervene on the equipment, not addressed by opening joints without first understanding why the pressure was behaving abnormally.
2. Maintenance procedures on live coke oven gas lines need to account for the specific ignition and explosion risk of opening pipeline joints, including verification that the line has been properly isolated and purged before any physical opening occurs.
3. A prior fatal gas-related incident at the same facility, as occurred here with the 2014 water pump house leak, needs a genuine facility-wide review of gas handling safety across all units, not treatment as an isolated event specific to where it occurred.
4. Worker and union safety concerns raised repeatedly before an incident deserve serious, documented follow-up. When affected workers and their representatives report that requested safety improvements were not implemented, that feedback is a direct, actionable signal worth treating as seriously as any formal audit finding.
