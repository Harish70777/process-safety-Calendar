## What Happened

A distillation column at First Chemical Corporation's Pascagoula facility had been shut down five weeks before the incident as part of routine operations, believed to be fully isolated with all heat sources removed. In reality, steam was slowly leaking through deteriorated shut-off valves that operators didn't realize had failed, continuing to heat roughly 1,200 gallons of mononitrotoluene (MNT), a chemical related to TNT, that remained inside the 145-foot-tall column. Over the following weeks, the trapped MNT slowly heated past 400 degrees Fahrenheit and began to decompose into unstable byproducts. Plant operators did not monitor the column's internal temperature during the shutdown, and when a liquid-level alarm activated the day before the explosion, no action was taken to investigate it. At approximately 5:25 a.m. on October 13, workers heard a loud rumbling noise and observed smoke venting from the tower; they took shelter in the unit control room, located only 50 feet from the column's base. The column exploded violently minutes later, blowing off roughly 35 feet from its top and knocking all three workers in the control room to the ground, showering them with shattered glass. Flying debris ruptured a nearby storage tank containing paranitrotoluene, igniting a fire that burned for nearly three hours; other debris landed just feet from a large anhydrous ammonia cylinder without striking it. A community shelter-in-place was called, though CSB later found this instruction was not effectively communicated to area residents.

## Root Causes

- **Steam continued heating supposedly isolated material through leaking valves:** the shut-off valves meant to fully isolate the column during shutdown had deteriorated without detection, allowing steam to keep heating the 1,200 gallons of MNT left inside for weeks.
- **No temperature monitoring during the shutdown period:** operators had no ongoing visibility into the column's internal temperature, meaning the material's slow decomposition into unstable byproducts went completely unnoticed until the explosion.
- **A liquid-level alarm the day before the explosion was not investigated:** the system provided a direct warning sign in the final 24 hours, but no action was taken to determine what it meant.
- **The control room sat too close to the hazard, without blast protection:** at just 50 feet from the column and not reinforced to withstand blast pressure, the room offered workers essentially no protection once the explosion occurred.
- **No adequate hazard analysis existed for the continuous MNT distillation process:** CSB found First Chemical had not applied lessons learned from hazard analyses conducted on similar processes elsewhere in the plant to this specific column.

## Lessons Learned

1. Equipment believed isolated during a shutdown needs verification, not assumption. Deteriorated valves let steam continue heating material for weeks without anyone realizing the column was not actually in the safe state it was thought to be.
2. Reactive or thermally unstable materials left in equipment during a shutdown need active temperature monitoring for the duration, since decomposition can develop slowly and silently until it reaches a critical point.
3. Any alarm, including a seemingly unrelated one like a liquid-level alarm, deserves investigation rather than dismissal, especially on equipment believed to be idle. This incident's clearest missed warning came just a day before the explosion.
4. Control rooms and other occupied buildings near hazardous process equipment need siting and construction that accounts for realistic worst-case events. A blast-unprotected room only 50 feet from a 145-foot column offered no meaningful protection when that column failed.
5. Community shelter-in-place instructions need a verified communication path to actually reach residents, not just an issued order. CSB found this instruction never effectively reached the people it was meant to protect.
