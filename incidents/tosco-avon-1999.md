## What Happened

Workers at the Tosco Avon Refinery were attempting to replace a section of piping attached to a 150-foot-tall crude fractionator tower while the process unit remained in operation. The pipe, which had developed a pinhole leak, contained flammable naphtha. The work permit authorizing the repair called for the line to be drained, steamed, and isolated before removal began, but these requirements were not met, and the work proceeded anyway. Over the 13 days between when the leak was first identified and the day of the fatal accident, Tosco personnel had numerous opportunities to reassess the hazards of the repair and take additional safety measures, including the option of shutting down the fractionator entirely to eliminate both the naphtha source and any potential ignition sources, but the job was never re-evaluated and the unit was never shut down. When workers finally removed the unisolated piping, naphtha released directly onto the hot fractionator tower and ignited. Flames engulfed five workers positioned at different heights on the tower structure; three were contractors, two employed by a scaffold erection company and one by a crane company. Four workers were killed, and the fifth sustained serious injuries. This was the second fatal incident at the same refinery in two years; CSB found management deficiencies were also a significant factor in the earlier 1997 explosion that killed one worker and injured 46.

## Root Causes

- **Work permit requirements to drain, steam, and isolate the line were not met:** the piping removal proceeded despite the authorized work permit explicitly requiring these safety steps first, a direct deviation from the plan that had actually been approved.
- **No formal hazard evaluation was conducted for the repair:** management never assessed the specific dangers of removing unisolated, naphtha-containing piping next to a hot, operating fractionator tower before or during the job.
- **Thirteen days passed without any reassessment of the developing situation:** as the repair dragged on with the line still not successfully drained, nobody at the refinery treated this delay as a trigger to step back and reconsider whether the work should continue under these conditions.
- **No systematic job planning and authorization process existed:** Avon lacked a structured system to ensure maintenance work of this kind received appropriate scrutiny, management involvement, and safety specialist review before proceeding.
- **No audits of line-breaking, lockout/tagout, or blinding procedures had occurred in three years:** CSB found that periodic audits would likely have detected and corrected the pattern of serious deviations from safe work practices that had become normalized at the refinery, including opening piping containing flammable liquids before draining it.

## Lessons Learned

1. Work permit requirements exist specifically to control identified hazards; proceeding with a job when required safety steps like draining and isolation haven't actually been completed defeats the entire purpose of the permit system.
2. A maintenance task that stretches on far longer than expected, as this one did across 13 days, deserves an active reassessment of whether the original safety plan is still adequate, not just continued effort toward the original approach.
3. Nonroutine maintenance on hazardous, energized process equipment needs a formal, documented hazard evaluation before work begins, especially when the task involves removing piping containing flammable material near an operating unit.
4. Periodic audits of safety-critical work practices, like line breaking and lockout/tagout procedures, are what catch a pattern of gradual deviation before it causes a catastrophic incident. Three years without one here meant unsafe practices had time to become normalized.
5. A prior fatal or serious incident at the same facility needs to translate into genuine management system change, not just a memory. This was the second fatal accident at this refinery in two years, with strikingly similar management oversight failures identified both times.
