## What Happened

At approximately 9:10 p.m. on June 22, 2023, a 5,200-gallon polyethylene storage tank ruptured at the Darling Ingredients facility in Wadesboro, North Carolina, fatally injuring the employee who was refilling it. The tank was supposed to be refilled with aluminum chloride, but sulfuric acid was added instead. Approximately 80 gallons of 93 percent sulfuric acid were introduced to the roughly 60 gallons of aluminum chloride remaining in the tank, triggering a chemical reaction that caused the vessel to fail. Darling's own investigation concluded the reaction generated hydrogen gas from a reaction between sulfuric acid and aluminum metal, which ignited at its high autoignition temperature and caused an explosion, but CSB's review of post-incident photographs found the damage inconsistent with that scenario, since reaching the temperature the company's theory required would have melted the tank's polyethylene material at a much lower threshold than what was observed. CSB's own analysis concluded the more likely mechanism was a reaction between aluminum chloride and sulfuric acid producing hydrogen chloride vapor, which built pressure inside the tank until it separated the vessel's body from its base. Approximately 130 pounds of hydrogen chloride vapor were released during the incident.

## Root Causes

- **Sulfuric acid was mistakenly added to a tank meant to receive aluminum chloride:** the fundamental error was delivering and adding the wrong chemical to this specific tank, setting up the reactive combination that followed.
- **The tank was not designed to withstand the pressure the resulting reaction generated:** whether from hydrogen gas or hydrogen chloride vapor, the polyethylene vessel's rating did not account for the possibility of this particular chemical combination occurring inside it.
- **The company's own investigation reached a conclusion inconsistent with the physical evidence:** Darling's theory of the reaction mechanism required internal temperatures the tank's material damage didn't support, indicating the investigation didn't fully reconcile its conclusion against available evidence before finalizing it.
- **The specific procedural or communication failure that led to the wrong chemical being delivered isn't detailed in available public summary information:** while the outcome is clear, the exact breakdown, whether in labeling, delivery verification, or work instructions, that allowed sulfuric acid to reach an aluminum chloride tank warrants the same scrutiny given to similar wrong-chemical incidents elsewhere in this database.

## Lessons Learned

1. Verifying the identity of a chemical before adding it to any storage tank is a non-negotiable step, regardless of how routine the refilling task has become. This incident is a direct echo of the same wrong-chemical delivery pattern documented elsewhere in this database (MGPI Atchison, Whitehall Leather), each with a different specific mechanism but the same underlying failure.
2. Storage tank material and design ratings need to account for the realistic worst-case chemical combination that could occur inside them, including scenarios resulting from human error, not just the intended chemical's properties alone.
3. Incident investigations need their conclusions checked against all available physical evidence before being finalized, since a plausible-sounding theory that doesn't match the actual damage pattern can lead a company toward the wrong corrective action.
4. Wrong-chemical delivery incidents recur across the industry with enough regularity that any facility receiving bulk chemical deliveries should treat positive identity verification, not just visual or procedural assumption, as a standard, non-skippable step for every delivery.
