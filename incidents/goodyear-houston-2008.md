## What Happened

During maintenance work the day before the incident, several valves on a heat exchanger containing pressurized anhydrous ammonia were closed, including a valve that isolated the exchanger from its relief valve, done to allow replacement of a burst rupture disk. When pressurized steam was later introduced to the piping during a cleaning operation, the exchanger had no path to safely relieve the resulting overpressure and ruptured violently, releasing ammonia and hurling debris that struck and fatally injured an employee walking through the area. Five other workers were exposed to the ammonia release, and a sixth was injured while exiting the area. The response that followed compounded the tragedy: a malfunction in the plant's computerized badge tracking system delayed generating an accurate list of who was in the affected area, and because the fatally injured worker was herself a member of the emergency response team, her absence from the evacuation muster point wasn't flagged as unusual. Her body wasn't discovered, buried in rubble in a dimly lit area, until roughly six hours after the explosion.

## Root Causes

- **Valves closed during maintenance blocked the relief path:** isolating the exchanger from its relief valve to replace a rupture disk removed the very safeguard that should have prevented the vessel from over-pressurizing once steam was reintroduced.
- **Inadequate handoff between maintenance and operations:** the valve configuration left from the prior day's maintenance work was not adequately communicated to the personnel conducting the subsequent cleaning operation.
- **No emergency evacuation drills for four years:** despite a formal schedule calling for drills four times a year, workers told CSB none had actually been conducted in the four years before the accident.
- **Worker accountability system failed under real conditions:** ammonia vapor and activated emergency water sprays physically prevented some workers from reaching alarm pull-boxes, and personnel responsible for headcount accountability were, in some cases, unaware that role was part of their job.
- **A single point of failure in emergency response depended on the very people it was meant to track:** because the missing worker was part of the response team, her absence wasn't correctly interpreted as a sign something was wrong, a structural gap in how the accountability process worked.

## Lessons Learned

1. Never leave a pressure vessel without a verified, functioning relief path, if a valve isolating a relief device must be closed for maintenance, that vessel needs to stay out of service (no pressurizing operations like steam cleaning) until the relief path is confirmed restored.
2. Maintenance-to-operations handoff needs to be explicit and verified, not assumed, the next shift or next operation needs positive confirmation of exactly what valve configuration was left in place, not just a general understanding that "maintenance was done."
3. Scheduled emergency drills that don't actually happen are worse than no schedule at all, they create false organizational confidence that response capability has been tested when it hasn't.
4. Emergency accountability systems need to work even when the people responsible for headcounts are themselves potentially affected, build redundancy into who tracks who, so a missing responder doesn't fall through the exact gap their role was meant to prevent.
