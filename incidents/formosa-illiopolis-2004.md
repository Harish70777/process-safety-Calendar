## What Happened

During a routine cleaning operation on one PVC reactor, an operator mistakenly opened the bottom valve of a different, nearby reactor that was actively running and under pressure, apparently bypassing an active pressure safety interlock in the process. This released a large quantity of highly flammable vinyl chloride monomer (VCM), which formed a vapor cloud and detonated. The explosion and resulting fire destroyed much of the facility and ignited PVC resin stored in an adjacent warehouse, producing a thick smoke plume that forced a 48-hour evacuation of residents within a mile of the plant. Five workers were killed and three others severely injured; the facility was destroyed and never reopened.

## Root Causes

- **A single procedural safeguard protected against a catastrophic release:** bypassing the pressure interlock on the operating reactor relied on nothing more than verbal supervisor permission, there was no independent, hardware-based layer of protection behind that single procedural step.
- **The exact same failure mode had happened before, twice, and was not corrected:** CSB found a nearly identical incident had occurred in 2003 at Formosa's Baton Rouge facility, and again in February 2004, just two months before this explosion, at this same Illiopolis site. Corrective actions identified after the February incident were never implemented.
- **Operator error enabled by system design, not just individual mistake:** the operator opened the wrong reactor's valve while working from a written procedure meant to control a hazard with catastrophic potential, CSB's investigation concluded the deeper failure was organizational, in relying on a procedure alone to prevent an error of this consequence, not simply an individual's mistake.

## Lessons Learned

1. Never let a single procedural step, especially one that can be satisfied with verbal permission alone, be the only thing standing between normal operation and a catastrophic release. High-consequence bypass actions need independent, engineered safeguards, not just a supervisor's spoken approval.
2. A near-identical incident at a sister facility, or even the same site months earlier, is not a separate event to file away, it's a direct warning that needs a real corrective action plan, tracked to completion, before the same failure repeats.
3. When investigating an operator error, ask what about the system made that error possible or even likely, this incident is a clear case where blaming the individual would have missed the organizational design failure that was the real root cause.
4. Reactors performing different functions (one being cleaned, one actively operating) need physically distinct, hard-to-confuse controls and clear field verification steps, proximity and visual similarity between vessels in different states is itself a hazard worth engineering out.
