## What Happened

Shortly before noon on June 17, 1991, workers at the Albright and Wilson Americas phosphorus chemicals plant in North Charleston, South Carolina, began mixing chemicals to produce a flame retardant when an unexpected reaction triggered an explosion and fire. Sheets of corrugated aluminum siding covering the building were blown across the plant grounds, and the structure lost part of its walls and roof. Six people were killed immediately; three more died in the days following from their injuries, bringing the final death toll to nine. Notably, only one of the six people killed on the day of the explosion was an Albright and Wilson employee. The other five, along with all four of the most critically injured survivors, worked for a subcontractor that was installing insulation around pipes near the mixing apparatus at the time of the blast. Thirty-three people in total were injured, including at least three firefighters who responded to the scene; four survivors were burned over at least half their bodies. The plant, which employed roughly 200 people across nine separate production units on the site, shut the damaged unit down indefinitely, while the other eight units sustained only minor or no damage. Company officials later concluded an unexpected reaction had caused the explosion, though the precise chemical mechanism was never made public in available records.

## Root Causes

- **An unexpected chemical reaction occurred during mixing:** company officials determined that combining the chemicals needed to produce the flame retardant triggered a reaction that wasn't anticipated, leading directly to the explosion and fire.
- **Subcontractors were working in close proximity to an active mixing process:** the majority of the fatalities and most severe injuries were subcontractor employees performing unrelated insulation work near the mixing apparatus rather than the process operators themselves.
- **The specific cause of the unexpected reaction was never publicly determined:** available records don't indicate that a definitive root-cause chemical mechanism was ever released, leaving the precise trigger for the reaction unresolved in the public record.

## Lessons Learned

1. Contractors performing unrelated work, like insulation installation, near an active chemical mixing process face real risk from that process's hazards even though their own task has nothing to do with the chemistry involved. Coordinating contractor work locations with active process hazards is a genuine safety responsibility, not a scheduling afterthought.
2. When a majority of an incident's casualties are subcontractor employees rather than the facility's own workforce, that pattern deserves scrutiny into whether contractors received the same hazard awareness and protective planning as direct employees working in the same area.
3. "Unexpected chemical reaction" as an explanation points to a gap in hazard evaluation during process design or operation. If a reaction can occur unexpectedly during a mixing step, the process hazard analysis for that step didn't fully anticipate the range of conditions or interactions actually possible.
4. Facility siting and internal layout need to account for the worst-case failure of any single process unit. Here, damage was largely contained to one of nine units, but the fatalities extended well beyond that unit's own operators to nearby contractors, underscoring how blast effects can reach people who aren't part of the hazardous process itself.
