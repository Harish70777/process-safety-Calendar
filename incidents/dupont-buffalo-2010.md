## What Happened

At DuPont's Yerkes chemical plant near Buffalo, New York, a contract welder and his foreman were repairing an agitator support atop an atmospheric storage tank as part of routine maintenance. The 10,000-gallon tank had recently held polyvinyl fluoride slurry and had been shut down, cleaned, and inspected before the repair work began; DuPont's hot work permitting process had been followed and all required paperwork completed. What DuPont's process hazard analysis had not accounted for was that this tank remained connected to two other in-service tanks through an overflow line, and flammable vinyl fluoride vapor from those tanks could migrate into the tank being repaired. Personnel had monitored the atmosphere directly above the tank before authorizing the hot work but never tested the interior of the tank itself. Just after 11:00 a.m. on November 9, 2010, sparks or heat from the welding ignited vinyl fluoride vapor that had silently accumulated inside the tank. The explosion blew most of the tank's steel cover off, leaving it and the attached agitator assembly hanging over the tank's edge, supported by only a small section still attached. The welder died instantly from blunt force trauma; the foreman working nearby suffered first-degree burns and other injuries. Nearby residents felt the blast from nearly a mile away.

## Root Causes

- **DuPont's process hazard analysis wrongly assumed the tank couldn't reach flammable vapor levels:** the company had determined that any vinyl fluoride entering the tank being repaired would remain below its flammable limit, an assumption CSB found was incorrect and never adequately verified.
- **The tank being repaired was not isolated from tanks still in service:** an overflow line connected the tank undergoing hot work to two operating tanks, allowing flammable vapor to migrate in undetected, a connection that should have been positively isolated before hot work began.
- **No monitoring was performed inside the tank itself:** DuPont's hot work procedures required checking the atmosphere above the tank, but not testing the actual interior space where the flammable vapor had accumulated, precisely where the hazard existed.
- **The employee who authorized the hot work permit lacked relevant process knowledge:** CSB's investigation specifically found the DuPont employee who signed the permit had no knowledge of the process the tank was connected to or the hazards associated with it, undermining the permit system's ability to catch this exact risk.

## Lessons Learned

1. Hot work permits and procedures need to require monitoring the actual interior of any tank or vessel on which welding, cutting, or grinding will occur, not just the surrounding atmosphere. This is the single clearest, most repeated finding across CSB's broader body of hot work investigations, including several other incidents in this database.
2. Equipment believed to be isolated for maintenance needs verified, positive isolation, not an assumption based on the equipment being "shut down." An overflow line connecting this tank to two in-service tanks was enough to introduce a fatal hazard despite the maintenance tank itself being properly prepared.
3. Whoever signs off on a hot work permit needs genuine, specific knowledge of the process and hazards connected to that equipment. A signature from someone unfamiliar with the actual risk provides a false sense of review without the real safeguard a knowledgeable reviewer would provide.
4. Process hazard analyses need to be revisited and verified against reality, not simply trusted once written. DuPont's own hazard analysis assumption about vapor levels in this tank turned out to be wrong, and that error persisted until it caused a fatality.
