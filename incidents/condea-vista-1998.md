## What Happened

Roughly three months before the incident, CONDEA Vista's Baltimore facility changed its linear alkyl benzene production process, replacing the direct addition of aluminum chloride to a reactor with powdered aluminum, which would combine with hydrogen chloride inside the vessel to form the aluminum chloride needed for the reaction. Shortly after this change was introduced, a sludge-like residue of coarse aluminum powder, aluminum chloride, and hydrocarbons began accumulating and plugging the bottom of the reactor, roughly 180 gallons of it by the time of the incident. On October 13, 1998, operators attempted to clear the plugged reactor by injecting steam directly into it. The steam immediately triggered a runaway reaction with the trapped aluminum and aluminum chloride sludge, over-pressurizing the vessel, which exploded and caught fire. The explosion released hydrogen chloride, benzene, aluminum chloride, aluminum oxide, and soot into the atmosphere, and the resulting fire took approximately two hours to extinguish. Five people were injured, including a worker who fell 30 feet from a catwalk and suffered back and internal injuries. The blast was strong enough to shake homes in the surrounding area.

## Root Causes

- **A process change was made without a formal management of change review:** switching from direct aluminum chloride addition to powdered aluminum was a real modification to the chemical process, but CSB found it wasn't run through the kind of systematic MOC review that could have identified the sludge-formation and reactivity risks this specific change introduced.
- **The new process created an unanticipated sludge buildup:** the switch to powdered aluminum led to a residue accumulating and plugging the reactor in a way the original process apparently hadn't, a direct physical consequence of the change that went unaddressed.
- **Steam was injected into a reactor containing reactive metal sludge:** attempting to clear the blockage by adding steam directly triggered a violent reaction between the steam and the trapped aluminum and aluminum chloride, the exact combination of materials and conditions that caused the explosion.
- **No hazard analysis evaluated the new process's failure modes:** CSB's broader review found that neither this incident nor a similar one that same year (a fatal Equilon Enterprises refinery fire in Washington state) involved a genuine emergency requiring rapid decisions. Time was available in both cases to analyze the situation more thoroughly, but wasn't used that way.

## Lessons Learned

1. Any change to a chemical process, including substituting one raw material form for another, like powdered aluminum for aluminum chloride, needs a documented management of change review specifically evaluating how the substitution affects the process's hazards, not just its intended chemistry.
2. Unexpected byproducts or residues appearing after a process change, like the sludge buildup here, are a direct signal that the change's consequences weren't fully understood. That signal deserves investigation before routine maintenance responses (like steam injection) are applied to it.
3. Clearing a blockage in process equipment needs its own hazard evaluation, especially when the blocked material's composition is unusual or not fully characterized. Steam, water, or other common clearing methods can trigger a dangerous reaction with certain trapped materials, as happened here.
4. CSB's own comparison of this incident to a separate fatal 1998 refinery fire found both situations allowed time for careful analysis rather than requiring split-second decisions. A properly executed management of change program is specifically designed to catch exactly this kind of risk before a routine operational decision turns into an emergency.
