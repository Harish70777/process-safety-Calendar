## What Happened

During a facility shutdown for maintenance, a contract crew was performing welding and grinding, "hot work", on a storage tank at the DeRidder pulp and paper mill. The tank contained non-condensable gases and flammable material that had not been adequately identified or controlled as a hazard before the hot work began. The resulting ignition caused a powerful explosion, forceful enough to launch the tank into a different area of the plant. Three contract workers performing the hot work were killed, and seven others were injured. Strikingly, this was not the first time this exact failure pattern had occurred at a Packaging Corporation of America facility: in 2008, a nearly identical hot-work tank explosion at the company's Tomahawk, Wisconsin plant had also killed three workers, prompting CSB at the time to issue a safety bulletin specifically on preventing hot work deaths around tanks, a bulletin that evidently did not prevent this second, separate incident nine years later.

## Root Causes

- **Hot work performed on a tank without adequate hazard identification:** the flammable and non-condensable gas hazard inside the tank was not properly recognized and controlled before spark-producing work began directly on or above it.
- **A regulatory coverage gap:** CSB's investigation found the specific non-condensable gas system involved was not covered by the OSHA Process Safety Management standard, leaving a gap in the formal safety requirements that would otherwise have applied to a hazard of this nature.
- **The same company had already experienced this exact failure pattern:** a nearly identical hot-work explosion had killed three workers at a different PCA facility in Tomahawk, Wisconsin in 2008, the lesson from that earlier incident did not translate into a lasting company-wide corrective action that prevented recurrence at a different site nearly a decade later.

## Lessons Learned

1. Never perform hot work on or near a tank without first positively verifying, through testing, not assumption, that it's free of flammable material and adequately purged; the presence of "non-condensable gases" is exactly the kind of overlooked hazard that hot work permits need to specifically screen for.
2. A regulatory standard not technically covering a specific hazard doesn't mean that hazard is safe to leave unmanaged, internal hazard identification needs to go beyond minimum compliance boundaries.
3. A serious incident at one company facility should trigger action across every facility with similar equipment or processes, not just the specific site where it happened, this incident is a stark example of a corporate-level lesson from 2008 not reaching a sister facility before a repeat tragedy in 2017.
4. Hot work remains one of the most consistently fatal and most preventable categories of process safety incident, CSB has repeatedly emphasized this across multiple investigations; treat any spark-producing work near tanks, vessels, or piping that has ever carried flammable material as inherently high-risk, requiring rigorous, verified hazard clearance every single time.
