## What Happened

A single Airgas employee was transferring liquid nitrous oxide from storage into a trailer truck at the company's Cantonment, Florida manufacturing facility, one of only a handful of nitrous oxide production plants in the United States. During the initial loading, a pump heated the nitrous oxide above its safe operating limits. Nitrous oxide, while stable under normal conditions, can decompose explosively when conditions shift unfavorably, exceeding those critical thermal limits appears to have triggered a decomposition reaction that propagated from the pump into the trailer truck, causing a catastrophic explosion. The blast killed the lone employee working at the facility that day and heavily damaged the plant, halting nitrous oxide production there indefinitely. CSB's investigation found the explosion could be traced directly to the absence of any effective process safety management system at the facility, and further discovered that most of the federal regulations requiring such systems at hazardous chemical facilities simply don't apply to nitrous oxide manufacturers.

## Root Causes

- **No evaluation of inherently safer design options:** even though heat generated by the pump was a known hazard, Airgas never evaluated safer design alternatives that could have eliminated the need for that specific pump, and the risk it carried, altogether.
- **No management of change review when the pump was installed:** the company did not perform a management of change review or hazard analysis before installing the pump that ultimately overheated the nitrous oxide, a step that could have identified and controlled the thermal hazard before it ever became operational.
- **Existing safeguards were likely ineffective:** a safety interlock meant to automatically shut down the pump under unsafe conditions, along with flame arrestors, were found to have likely been ineffective and failed to prevent the incident.
- **A genuine regulatory gap:** CSB determined Airgas was not in violation of any federal regulation at the time of the explosion, OSHA and EPA process safety management requirements largely don't extend to nitrous oxide manufacturing facilities, leaving this entire industry segment without a mandated safety management framework that other high-hazard chemical processes are required to have.

## Lessons Learned

1. A known hazard (like heat generated by process equipment) deserves a genuine inherently-safer-design evaluation, not just acceptance and downstream mitigation, eliminating the hazard's source is a stronger control than managing around it.
2. Any new equipment installation in a process handling a material capable of hazardous decomposition needs a documented management of change review, regardless of how routine the equipment might seem.
3. Safety interlocks and similar automated safeguards need periodic verification that they actually function as designed, an interlock that's "likely ineffective" provides false assurance that's arguably worse than having no safeguard at all, since it can suppress the urgency to find alternative protections.
4. Regulatory coverage gaps are a real, industry-level risk factor, not just a compliance technicality, this incident is a direct case where an entire manufacturing sector operated for years without a legal requirement for the kind of safety management system that could have prevented a fatality, a genuinely important context for anyone in an industry judging its own safety posture solely against "are we compliant."
