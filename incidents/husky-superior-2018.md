## What Happened

While shutting down the fluid catalytic cracking unit (FCCU) for planned maintenance, a worn slide valve failed to maintain the barrier meant to keep air separated from hydrocarbons inside the unit. Air was inadvertently directed through the regenerator into the reactor, main column, and gas concentration unit, where accumulating oxygen formed a flammable mixture and reacted with pyrophoric material inside the equipment, creating an ignition source. Two vessels — the primary and sponge absorbers — exploded, propelling over 100 metal fragments (some several feet long) up to 1,200 feet, one of which punctured a nearby asphalt storage tank. Roughly 17,000 barrels of hot asphalt spilled and later ignited, causing further fires across the refinery. 36 people were injured; the City of Superior evacuated over 2,500 residents due to concern over a nearby hydrogen fluoride (HF) tank that was struck by debris but not breached — CSB later found that debris had come within 150 feet of the HF tank, closer than the asphalt tank it did hit, meaning a much more severe toxic release was a near-miss away from happening.

## Root Causes

- **Worn slide valve failed to isolate air from hydrocarbons:** the valve, designed for complete shutoff, had eroded over years of service to the point it could no longer maintain the seal needed during the unit's non-routine shutdown state.
- **Ineffective safeguards:** the company's existing safety systems did not account for this specific worn-valve failure mode and were found by CSB to be inadequate to prevent the resulting flammable mixture from forming.
- **Failure to learn from a near-identical prior incident:** CSB explicitly found the company had not adequately absorbed the lessons from a 2015 ExxonMobil Torrance refinery explosion, which resulted from the same underlying mechanism — a worn slide valve in an FCC unit allowing air and hydrocarbons to mix during non-routine operation.
- **Inadequate process knowledge:** the refinery had changed ownership multiple times, and at the time of the incident Husky Energy had owned it for only six months; in-house FCC expertise was limited, with minimal external technical engagement to catch gaps in understanding transient shutdown hazards.

## Lessons Learned

1. Slide valves and similar erosion-prone isolation components need a mechanical integrity program specifically addressing wear over their service life — "designed for complete shutoff" doesn't remain true forever without inspection.
2. When another company's CSB report describes a failure mechanism relevant to your equipment, treat it as an urgent internal audit trigger, not background reading — this incident is a direct, well-documented case of a lesson from one refinery not reaching another with the same equipment design.
3. Ownership transitions are a genuine process safety risk window: new owners need a deliberate plan to capture and validate institutional process knowledge, especially for complex transient operations like unit shutdowns.
4. Facility siting analysis needs to account for debris and projectile paths from foreseeable equipment failures, not just normal operating hazards — this incident came within about 50 feet of striking a highly toxic HF inventory instead of an asphalt tank.
