## What Happened

Approximately 37 hours before the fire, the Equilon Enterprises oil refinery in Anacortes, Washington, lost electric power and steam supply, creating abnormal process conditions in the facility's delayed coking unit. Rather than requiring a formal review of these unusual operating conditions before resuming work, the plant continued operating under circumstances that fell outside its normal, previously reviewed process parameters. On November 25, 1998, this combination of abnormal conditions culminated in a fire in the delayed coking unit, killing six refinery workers. CSB later grouped this incident with a separate fatal explosion that same year at CONDEA Vista's Baltimore facility in a joint safety bulletin on management of change, finding that neither incident involved an emergency requiring rapid, split-second decisions. In both cases, time had been available to analyze the developing situation more thoroughly before it escalated to a fatal event.

## Root Causes

- **A loss of power and steam created abnormal process conditions:** the electrical and steam supply disruption 37 hours before the fire pushed the delayed coking unit outside its normal operating envelope, conditions the facility's existing procedures had not been designed around.
- **No management of change review was triggered by the abnormal conditions:** CSB found that resuming and continuing operations under these unusual circumstances did not go through the kind of formal MOC review that could have identified the specific hazards created by the power and steam loss.
- **Available time was not used for adequate analysis:** CSB's investigation specifically noted that both this incident and the CONDEA Vista explosion the same year involved situations where personnel had time to study what was happening more carefully, but a more analytical, structured approach wasn't applied before conditions escalated.
- **Contributing factors accumulated over the 37-hour period:** CSB found numerous contributing factors developed during the extended period between the power loss and the fire, factors a systematic MOC program would have been positioned to catch and address.

## Lessons Learned

1. A significant utility disruption, like a loss of electric power or steam supply, that pushes a process outside its normal operating conditions should trigger a formal management of change review before operations continue, not just an operational workaround to keep running.
2. An extended period between an initiating abnormal event and eventual failure, 37 hours in this case, represents real opportunity for intervention. The time existing to investigate more thoroughly is itself a safety resource that needs to actually be used, not just available in principle.
3. CSB's joint review of this incident alongside CONDEA Vista's found a common thread: neither was a rapid-decision emergency. Properly conceived and executed management of change programs are specifically designed to prevent incidents that develop over hours or days, not just instantaneous failures.
4. Both OSHA's Process Safety Management standard and EPA's Risk Management Program require a formal MOC program for facilities handling highly hazardous materials. This incident is a direct illustration of the consequence when that requirement isn't functioning in practice, even where it technically exists on paper.
