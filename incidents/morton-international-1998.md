## What Happened

Workers at Morton International's Paterson, New Jersey plant began what they expected to be a routine six to eight hour production run of Automate Yellow 96, a dye used to tint petroleum fuel products, made by reacting ortho-nitrochlorobenzene (o-NCB) with 2-ethylhexylamine (2-EHA) inside a 2,000-gallon reactor kettle nearly four decades old. At 7:40 p.m., steam was turned on to the kettle and the reaction began normally. Roughly 40 minutes later, the reaction accelerated beyond what the kettle's cooling system could remove, and workers made desperate attempts to cool the reactor that ultimately failed. The rising temperature drove a secondary runaway reaction, further decomposing the dye and generating enough pressure to violently rupture the vessel. The explosion and resulting fire injured nine workers, including two seriously, some suffering burns, contusions, and twisted joints from the force of the blast. Toxic ortho-nitrochlorobenzene and flammable material were released into the air and carried into the surrounding residential neighborhood, with chemical fallout detected as far as half a mile from the plant. Local authorities required residents in a ten-square-block area to shelter in place and issued a health advisory, though the full extent of community exposure was never determined. Morton discontinued production of Automate Yellow 96 following the incident.

## Root Causes

- **Morton had not adequately evaluated or controlled the runaway reaction hazard:** neither a preliminary hazard assessment conducted in 1990 nor a follow-up hazard analysis five years later ever considered the possibility of a runaway chemical reaction, despite this being one of the most common and well-understood reactive chemical hazards in the industry.
- **Insufficient cooling capacity for the reaction's actual behavior:** because the runaway hazard wasn't recognized, the kettle was never equipped with cooling capacity adequate to remove heat fast enough if the reaction accelerated beyond its expected rate.
- **No emergency shutdown or venting system existed:** when the reaction did run away, operators had no engineered system to safely stop the reaction or relieve the vessel's rising pressure before it ruptured.
- **A prior process change removed a built-in safety margin:** the plant had previously revised the process from a semi-batch method, adding the reactive material in four partial additions, to a full-batch method that added the entire charge at once, a change made to reduce worker exposure to o-NCB but which Morton did not recognize also created a more hazardous reaction condition.

## Lessons Learned

1. Reactive chemical hazards, especially runaway reactions, need to be explicitly evaluated in every process hazard analysis handling exothermic chemistry, not assumed absent just because a process has run without incident for years. Two separate hazard reviews here missed this exact risk.
2. Cooling system capacity needs to be sized against the reaction's worst credible heat generation rate, not just its expected, normal behavior. A kettle that can handle routine heat output may still be completely inadequate once a reaction begins to run away.
3. Any reactor handling an exothermic reaction needs engineered emergency relief, whether through venting, shutdown, or both, as a backup to normal cooling. Manual, in-the-moment attempts to cool a runaway reaction, as workers tried here, are not a substitute for a designed safety system.
4. A process change made for one safety reason, like reducing worker exposure during material addition, can introduce a different hazard the change wasn't evaluated for. Any process modification needs review against its full range of possible safety effects, not just the specific risk it was intended to address.
