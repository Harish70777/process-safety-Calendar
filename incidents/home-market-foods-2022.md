## What Happened

Two HVAC contractors were working near refrigeration machinery at a food processing plant — performing work unrelated to the facility's anhydrous ammonia refrigeration system, according to the fire chief who responded ("they were doing nothing associated with the ammonia system"). An ammonia pipe was accidentally severed during their work, releasing anhydrous ammonia — a colorless, toxic gas that becomes deadly at concentrations around 300 parts per million. Responding firefighters measured levels exceeding 6,000 parts per million on arrival, more than 20 times the deadly threshold, and were initially unable to enter the building. One contractor, 68-year-old Richard Arguin, died from the exposure; the second contractor was hospitalized with serious injuries. The EPA later reached a $1.1 million settlement with Home Market Foods after inspecting both the Norwood facility and a related cold storage warehouse, finding the company had failed to meet several Clean Air Act accident prevention requirements for its ammonia risk management program.

## Root Causes

- **An ammonia pipe was severed during unrelated maintenance work:** contractors performing HVAC work in proximity to refrigeration equipment came into contact with ammonia piping despite not being engaged in any task related to the ammonia system itself.
- **Risk Management Program deficiencies:** EPA's subsequent inspection found the company had not met several Clean Air Act accident prevention requirements for handling anhydrous ammonia, indicating the facility's formal chemical safety program had gaps beyond just this single incident.
- **Inadequate hazard awareness for contractors working nearby:** workers performing tasks unrelated to the ammonia system were positioned close enough to strike ammonia piping, suggesting insufficient hazard communication or physical safeguarding around the hazardous piping for personnel not specifically trained on it.
- **No apparent isolation or protection for nearby non-ammonia work:** the ammonia piping was vulnerable to accidental contact from work that had nothing to do with it — a physical separation or protection gap between hazardous process piping and general maintenance activity in the same area.

## Lessons Learned

1. Any piping carrying a hazardous material (like anhydrous ammonia) that runs through or near areas where unrelated maintenance work occurs needs physical protection or clear marking sufficient to prevent accidental contact, especially from contractors unfamiliar with the facility's specific hazards.
2. Contractors performing work anywhere near refrigeration or process equipment need a hazard briefing covering what hazardous materials are present nearby, even when their assigned task is unrelated — "not working on the ammonia system" doesn't mean immune to an ammonia release if physically working near ammonia piping.
3. A formal Risk Management Program isn't just a compliance document — EPA's finding of accident prevention requirement gaps after the fact underscores that the program's actual implementation, not just its existence on paper, determines whether it prevents incidents like this one.
4. Ammonia's extreme danger at low concentrations (deadly around 300 ppm, with responders measuring over 6,000 ppm here) means any suspected release near ammonia-containing equipment demands immediate evacuation and a trained hazmat response — this is not a hazard where "let's see how bad it is" is ever an appropriate first response.
