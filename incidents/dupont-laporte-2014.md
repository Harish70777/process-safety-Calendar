## What Happened

Days before the incident, water inadvertently entered methyl mercaptan feed piping and, combined with cold weather, formed an ice-like hydrate that blocked the line. Operators spent days trying to clear the blockage. A 2011 process hazard analysis at this same facility had already identified hydrate formation in this piping as a risk, but adequate safeguards were never put in place afterward. On the night of the incident, workers believed the hydrate blockage had cleared and, investigating what they thought was an unrelated pressure problem, went to drain what they believed was mostly water from vent header piping inside a poorly ventilated manufacturing building. The liquid was actually methyl mercaptan under pressure. When a worker manually opened two sets of drain valves, roughly 24,000 pounds of the highly toxic, flammable chemical escaped and vaporized inside the building. Personnel investigated the developing leak without respiratory protection; four workers died from a combination of asphyxiation and acute methyl mercaptan exposure, and a fifth was injured.

## Root Causes

- **Flawed engineering design and lack of adequate safeguards:** CSB identified this as the direct cause, the piping and building ventilation design did not adequately protect against a release of this kind inside an occupied space.
- **A known hazard was identified but not addressed:** a 2011 PHA had already flagged hydrate formation in this exact piping as a risk, but DuPont La Porte never established the safeguards that finding called for, the same hazard that ultimately caused the fatal event.
- **No respiratory protection used during troubleshooting:** personnel investigating a suspected leak did so without wearing protective equipment, despite handling a chemical with an extremely low safe-exposure threshold.
- **Poor communication across the days-long troubleshooting effort:** workers on the night of the incident believed the earlier hydrate blockage was resolved and misidentified what they were dealing with as a routine pressure issue rather than a residual hazard from the prior days' work.
- **Broader process safety management system deficiencies:** CSB's report cited weaknesses across safety culture assessment, auditing and corrective action follow-through, management of change, safe work practices, and toxic gas detection.

## Lessons Learned

1. A process hazard analysis finding is only as good as the safeguard implementation that follows it, treat unaddressed PHA action items as open, tracked risk, not a completed exercise.
2. Multi-day troubleshooting efforts need a clear, documented handoff of what's actually been resolved versus what's still uncertain, don't let "we think it's cleared" become the operating assumption for the next shift.
3. Respiratory protection protocols for known highly toxic chemicals should be non-negotiable during any troubleshooting or investigation activity, regardless of what the leak is initially believed to be.
4. When a facility handles multiple highly toxic chemicals, workers need equally rigorous training and hazard awareness for all of them, CSB specifically noted the importance of not treating some toxic chemicals as more "routine" or lower-priority than others at the same site.
