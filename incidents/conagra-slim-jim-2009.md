## What Happened

Contractors from Energy System Analysts were installing new gas-fired water heaters at the ConAgra Foods Slim Jim processing plant in Garner, North Carolina. As part of the work, they performed a "gas blow," purging air from newly installed gas piping by venting natural gas directly into the atmosphere to clear the lines before startup. Rather than directing this purge outdoors, the contractors vented the gas inside the building. The escaping natural gas accumulated and formed a flammable cloud inside the plant. At approximately 11:27 a.m. on June 9, 2009, the cloud ignited; CSB was never able to determine the specific ignition source given the extent of the resulting damage. The explosion collapsed roughly 15,000 square feet of the packaging area, partially damaged thousands more square feet of roof structure, and blew one wall outward into the parking lot, crushing nearby vehicles. The blast also ruptured refrigeration piping used in Slim Jim processing, releasing a significant quantity of anhydrous ammonia that complicated the emergency response as fumes spread through the command post and triage areas. Three employees, Barbara McLean Spears, Rachel Mae Poston Pulley, and Lewis Junior Watson, were killed. Approximately 41 other people were injured, including burns, blunt trauma, and ammonia inhalation, among them three firefighters who responded to the scene. ConAgra resumed Slim Jim production at the plant roughly six weeks after the explosion.

## Root Causes

- **Natural gas was purged indoors rather than outdoors:** the gas blow procedure vented flammable gas directly into the occupied plant building instead of to a safe outdoor location, allowing a flammable atmosphere to accumulate inside an occupied space.
- **This practice was not recognized as the serious hazard it represented:** CSB's broader review of gas blow incidents, including this one and others in this database, found this venting method was in wide informal use across multiple industries without being treated as the significant ignition and explosion risk it actually poses.
- **Refrigeration piping in the path of the blast was compromised:** the explosion's force ruptured ammonia refrigeration lines used in the food processing operation, adding a toxic release on top of the initial explosion and complicating rescue and evacuation.
- **The specific ignition source was never determined:** extensive structural damage from the explosion prevented investigators from identifying exactly what ignited the accumulated gas cloud, though the presence of any of numerous potential ignition sources in an occupied industrial building made ignition close to inevitable once the flammable atmosphere had formed.

## Lessons Learned

1. Purging gas piping by venting directly to atmosphere needs to happen outdoors, in a location and manner that prevents flammable accumulation, never inside an occupied building regardless of how routine or expedient the practice has become in informal industry use.
2. This incident is directly connected to the same underlying hazard documented elsewhere in this database at Kleen Energy: CSB grouped both incidents together specifically because the "gas blow" practice was found to be a recurring, serious, and preventable cause of fatal explosions across multiple industries.
3. Facilities using ammonia refrigeration systems need to account for the possibility that an unrelated incident, like an explosion from another cause entirely, could compromise refrigeration piping and introduce a toxic release on top of whatever the initial event already caused.
4. When damage is severe enough that an ignition source can't be conclusively identified after the fact, the practical lesson is to control the flammable atmosphere itself rather than rely on eliminating every possible ignition source; an occupied industrial building will always contain enough potential ignition points that preventing gas accumulation is the safeguard that actually matters.
