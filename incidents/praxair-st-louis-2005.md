## What Happened

On a Friday afternoon with temperatures reaching 97 degrees Fahrenheit, gas cylinders stored in the open on asphalt at Praxair's St. Louis compressed gas distribution facility absorbed heat radiating from the pavement and direct sunlight, raising the temperature and internal pressure of the propylene stored inside them. At approximately 3:20 p.m. on June 24, 2005, a propylene cylinder's pressure relief valve began venting gas. CSB investigators concluded static electricity generated by the escaping vapor and liquid most likely ignited the leaking propylene. Security camera footage showed the resulting fire spreading rapidly to other cylinders; eventually more than 8,000 cylinders caught fire, with many launching like rockets, some flying up to 800 feet and starting additional fires throughout the surrounding Lafayette Square neighborhood. The fire could not be extinguished until most of the flammable gas cylinders had already been consumed. No one was killed in the immediate incident, but a 32-year-old St. Louis resident, Minnie Cooper, died 11 days later; the city medical examiner ruled her death was triggered by inhalation of smoke and fumes from the explosion, likely while she was outdoors near the facility. Praxair permanently closed the facility following the incident.

## Root Causes

- **Pressure relief valve set points were too low for propylene in hot weather:** CSB found the industry-standard set points specified for these valves allowed venting well below the pressure that would actually damage the cylinders, meaning normal hot-weather conditions alone could trigger release.
- **Cylinders were stored on heat-absorbing asphalt in direct sunlight:** the storage surface and lack of shading meant cylinders experienced significantly elevated temperatures beyond ambient air temperature, accelerating internal pressure buildup on an already hot day.
- **No deluge system or fixed fire nozzles existed to cool cylinders during a developing fire:** once the initial venting and ignition occurred, the facility had no engineered means to actively cool nearby cylinders and prevent the fire from spreading to them.
- **A pattern of similar incidents had already been identified:** CSB's investigation found four similar propylene cylinder incidents had occurred since 1997, indicating this was a recognized, recurring failure mode in the industry rather than an isolated event.
- **Relief valve performance degrades with each use:** CSB found that every time a pressure relief valve opens, its performance deteriorates, making it progressively more likely to vent gas at even lower pressures in the future, compounding the underlying design issue over a valve's service life.

## Lessons Learned

1. Pressure relief valve set points need to account for realistic worst-case ambient conditions, including extreme heat, not just standard design assumptions. A set point technically compliant with industry standards can still allow dangerous venting under genuinely foreseeable weather.
2. Outdoor storage of pressurized flammable gas cylinders needs to account for radiant heat from paved surfaces and direct sun exposure, both of which can meaningfully raise cylinder temperature and pressure beyond ambient air conditions alone.
3. Active cooling capability, like deluge systems, provides a real safeguard against a single cylinder failure cascading into a much larger event. Its absence here meant an initial, contained release escalated into thousands of cylinders eventually catching fire.
4. A pattern of similar incidents across an industry, four in this case since 1997 alone, is a strong signal that a design or operational standard itself needs revisiting, not just individual facility-level corrective action after each new occurrence.
5. Community risk from industrial facilities isn't limited to on-site personnel. This incident's one fatality was a resident with no connection to the facility, exposed to smoke and fumes simply from proximity, a reminder that facility siting and community protection measures matter as much as on-site safety systems.
