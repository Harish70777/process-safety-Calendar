## What Happened

Marcus Oil and Chemical processed petrochemical waste byproducts into polyethylene wax inside a series of steel pressure vessels, several of which had been purchased secondhand in the early 1990s and modified for use at the facility. One of these vessels, Tank No. 7, had previously been modified to add internal heating coils; the 2-foot-diameter opening used for that work was resealed by welding a steel patch plate over it. On the evening of December 3, 2004, during a routine production run, that repair weld failed catastrophically. The 50,000-pound vessel ruptured violently, igniting a fire that burned for approximately seven hours before firefighters extinguished it. The explosion was felt across a wide area of southwest Houston; steel fragments were thrown up to a quarter mile from the plant, breaking windows and damaging several nearby buildings, including two churches, a house, and a social club. Three Houston firefighters were slightly injured while responding, and several residents sustained minor cuts from flying glass.

## Root Causes

- **Defective repair welds significantly weakened the vessel:** CSB's testing found the patch-plate weld on Tank No. 7 had reduced the vessel's structural strength by more than 75 percent, welding quality that fell well short of accepted industry standards for pressure vessels.
- **No qualified welder or proper welding procedure was used:** the repair that ultimately failed was not performed to the standard a certified pressure-vessel welder following a documented procedure would have followed.
- **No pressure test was performed after the repair:** had the vessel been hydrostatically tested after the weld repair, as required by the National Board Inspection Code, the defective weld would very likely have been identified before the vessel was returned to service.
- **No state or city requirement mandated compliance with recognized pressure vessel codes:** Texas did not require Marcus Oil to comply with the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code or the National Board Inspection Code for this kind of repair, leaving the facility without a regulatory backstop that would have caught the substandard weld.

## Lessons Learned

1. Any weld repair on a pressure-retaining vessel needs to be performed by a qualified welder following a documented, code-compliant procedure. This isn't a formality. CSB's investigation found the difference here was a vessel operating at roughly a quarter of its intended strength.
2. Pressure testing after any structural repair is the verification step that catches a defective weld before it's returned to hazardous service; skipping it means the vessel's actual integrity is simply unknown until it fails.
3. Where state or local regulation doesn't mandate a recognized pressure vessel code, voluntary compliance with ASME and National Board standards is still the meaningful safety bar. The absence of a legal requirement doesn't reduce the underlying physical risk.
4. Modifying secondhand or surplus equipment for a new application, as this facility did when installing internal heating coils, requires the same rigor as designing new equipment from scratch. Prior service history elsewhere doesn't guarantee a modification was executed to a safe standard.
