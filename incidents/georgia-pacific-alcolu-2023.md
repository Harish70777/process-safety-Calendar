## What Happened

At 9:20 a.m. on February 24, 2023, approximately two gallons of a sodium hydroxide and water caustic solution were accidentally released, seriously injuring an employee at the Georgia-Pacific Wood Products facility in Alcolu, South Carolina. A new pump for unloading caustic solution from tank trucks had been installed the day before the incident, and this was the first time Georgia-Pacific used it. When employees turned on the pump to begin transferring caustic solution, it failed to move fluid and began leaking. Rather than fully depressurizing and draining the pump before investigating, employees decided to flush it out with water from a fire hose to avoid getting caustic solution on their hands during disassembly. An employee connected the fire hose to the pump and partially opened the valve; the pump's plastic casing could not withstand the pressure supplied by the firewater and cracked, spraying caustic solution and water out and seriously injuring one employee. Following the incident, Georgia-Pacific modified its caustic unloading system to eliminate the need for this kind of pump entirely.

## Root Causes

- **A newly installed pump was used for the first time without established operating experience:** the pump had been in service for less than a day, meaning any issues with its actual performance or limitations under real conditions hadn't yet had a chance to surface through normal use.
- **Pressurized firewater was introduced to a pump not rated for that pressure:** rather than using a method appropriate for the pump's actual pressure rating, the flushing approach chosen exposed the equipment to pressure it wasn't designed to withstand.
- **The pump's plastic casing failed under pressure it wasn't built to handle:** the specific material and construction of the pump casing gave way once subjected to firewater pressure beyond its design limits.
- **Depressurizing and draining the pump before intervention was skipped in favor of a workaround:** the decision to flush with firewater, intended to make disassembly cleaner and safer for workers' hands, introduced a different and ultimately more severe hazard than the one it was meant to avoid.

## Lessons Learned

1. Newly installed equipment needs a management of change review, including verification of its actual pressure and material ratings against any procedure that will be used with it, before it's put into first use, not discovered through trial and error on day one.
2. Any decision to use pressurized water, or any pressurized fluid, to address a malfunctioning piece of equipment needs to first confirm that equipment's actual pressure rating, since firewater pressure can exceed what many process pumps and their casings are built to handle.
3. Workarounds designed to solve one hazard (caustic exposure to hands during disassembly) need evaluation for whether they introduce a different, potentially more severe hazard, rather than being adopted simply because they address the immediate concern.
4. Simplifying a hazardous material handling system, as Georgia-Pacific ultimately did by eliminating the pump entirely, is often a more durable safety improvement than adding procedural controls around equipment whose fundamental limitations contributed to an incident.
