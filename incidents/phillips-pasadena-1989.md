## What Happened

During routine maintenance on a settling leg (a line used to remove solidified polyethylene "logs" from a reactor), a contractor crew opened the line without the reactor being properly isolated. An 8-inch ball valve, the only barrier between the open line and the live reactor, was left unsecured, and roughly 85,000 pounds of highly flammable process gas (ethylene, isobutane, and other hydrocarbons) escaped in under two minutes. With no gas detectors or automatic warning systems on site, the vapor cloud spread unnoticed through the plant until it reached an ignition source, triggering an initial blast equivalent to roughly 2.4 tons of TNT, followed by a chain of further explosions including a 20,000-gallon isobutane tank. 23 workers were killed and 314 injured; the complex sustained roughly $750 million in damage.

## Root Causes

- **No effective lockout/isolation on the valve:** the single ball valve isolating the settling leg from the live reactor had no independent lock or blind flange backup, a single point of failure that, once defeated, had nothing behind it.
- **No process hazard analysis had been performed** on the polyethylene unit, so foreseeable failure modes like valve misalignment or incomplete isolation during this exact maintenance task were never formally identified.
- **Contractor management failure:** maintenance was subcontracted to a crew that OSHA's investigation found was inexperienced and inadequately trained on the specific hazards of the task, later documented in dozens of instances of missing operator and supervisor training.
- **No gas detection or warning systems:** even after the release began, there was no instrumentation in place to alert workers to the growing vapor cloud before it reached ignition.

## Lessons Learned

1. Never rely on a single valve as your only isolation barrier for hazardous energy, use double block-and-bleed or a physical blind, especially for any line connected to a live, pressurized process.
2. A documented process hazard analysis must cover routine maintenance tasks, not just normal operation, "we do this every week" is exactly the kind of familiarity that erodes rigor.
3. Contractor safety management is your responsibility, not theirs, verify training and competency for hazardous tasks before work begins, regardless of who's doing the work.
4. Gas detection and emergency shutdown systems are not optional in units handling large flammable inventories; this incident, alongside Piper Alpha the year before, was central to OSHA finalizing the Process Safety Management standard (1910.119) in 1992.
