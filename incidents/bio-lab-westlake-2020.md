## What Happened

Category 4 Hurricane Laura tore roofs off portions of the Bio-Lab facility, which manufactured pool and spa sanitizing chemicals built primarily around trichloroisocyanuric acid (TCCA). TCCA dissolves harmlessly in large volumes of water like a swimming pool, but when it's wetted by a small amount of water and doesn't fully dissolve, it can undergo a chemical reaction that generates heat, decomposes, and produces toxic chlorine gas. With the roof damaged, rainwater reached stored TCCA and triggered exactly that reaction, initiating a fire. More than 450 metric tons of TCCA were on site at the time — the company had managed to truck 825,000 pounds off-site ahead of the storm, but two additional trucks never arrived, leaving roughly a million pounds behind. The resulting fire and toxic chlorine plume forced a shelter-in-place order for the surrounding community and closed a stretch of Interstate 10 for more than 28 hours. No one was injured, but the facility was destroyed, stayed closed for over two years, and was eventually rebuilt at a cost of $250 million.

## Root Causes

- **Inadequate preparation for extreme weather:** CSB found Bio-Lab had not implemented industry guidance for hurricane preparation that was specifically developed and published following the 2017 Arkema Crosby incident — a nearly identical failure mode (flooding/storm damage reaching reactive chemicals) at a different company three years earlier.
- **A 2010 hazard analysis recommendation was never acted on:** Bio-Lab had conducted a process hazard analysis a decade before the incident that recommended assessing whether facility buildings, including their roofs, could withstand hurricane-strength winds — that recommendation was never implemented.
- **Delayed emergency response:** CSB found the company's response to the developing event was delayed by roughly five and a half hours, extending how long the reaction was allowed to progress before it was meaningfully addressed.
- **No regulatory coverage for this reactive hazard:** TCCA is not covered by OSHA's Process Safety Management standard, meaning Bio-Lab's hurricane and hazard preparation for this specific chemical was entirely voluntary rather than legally mandated.

## Lessons Learned

1. Industry safety guidance developed after another company's incident is a direct, actionable warning for anyone handling a similar hazard — this incident is a stark example of that guidance existing, being publicly available, and still not being adopted before a near-identical failure repeated.
2. A hazard analysis recommendation that specifically identifies a building's vulnerability to extreme weather needs to be tracked to completion, not left as a decade-old unimplemented finding — this is the same pattern seen elsewhere in this database where identified fixes went unactioned until an incident proved them necessary.
3. Reactive chemical hazards that fall outside OSHA PSM or EPA RMP coverage don't become less dangerous for lacking regulatory oversight — voluntary hazard preparation needs to be treated with the same rigor as a legally mandated program, especially for a facility in a hurricane-prone region.
4. Evacuating product ahead of a forecasted extreme weather event needs a realistic logistics plan with margin for disruption — two trucks failing to arrive left roughly a million pounds of reactive material on site, and that gap between planned and actual evacuation is exactly the kind of execution risk worth planning around in advance.
