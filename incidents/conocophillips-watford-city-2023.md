## What Happened

During the morning of February 24, 2023, a lone worker operating multiple oil and gas facilities near Watford City, North Dakota, was fatally injured after hydrocarbons and hydrogen sulfide displaced the oxygen inside a small, enclosed, climate-controlled building housing process equipment. With outside temperatures at negative 26 degrees Fahrenheit, the building's interior was kept at a comfortable 65 degrees. A pump inside was not operating as designed, causing an adjacent vessel to begin overfilling. To prevent the facility from shutting down, the employee connected a temporary hose to the vessel and manually drained its liquid, mostly water and hydrocarbons, into a bucket inside the roughly 90-square-foot building, then left temporarily to work at other locations. During that time, the drained hydrocarbons vaporized inside the sealed building and began displacing oxygen. About twenty minutes later, the employee returned, likely knocked over the bucket, releasing additional hydrocarbons and hydrogen sulfide vapor, and breathed in the resulting oxygen-depleted atmosphere. He was asphyxiated. The building had no installed gas detectors and was not ventilated. The employee did carry a personal gas detection monitor, but it was turned off and left inside his truck at the time of the incident. No company procedure existed providing instructions for safely draining liquids from vessels located inside enclosed buildings.

## Root Causes

- **Hydrocarbon liquid was drained into an open container inside a sealed building:** rather than draining outside or through a closed system, the liquid was released into a bucket inside an unventilated space, allowing it to vaporize and accumulate.
- **No procedure existed for safely draining vessels inside enclosed buildings:** ConocoPhillips had no documented instructions covering this exact task, leaving the worker to improvise a method without formal guidance on the hazard it created.
- **The building had no gas detection or ventilation:** even after hydrocarbons and hydrogen sulfide began accumulating, nothing in the building itself would have alerted anyone, including the returning worker, to the developing oxygen-deficient atmosphere.
- **The worker's personal gas monitor was not in use:** although he was equipped with a device specifically meant to warn of exactly this hazard, it was turned off and left in his truck rather than worn during the task.

## Lessons Learned

1. Draining hydrocarbon liquids indoors, even into a seemingly contained bucket, needs to be recognized as a genuine confined-space and toxic-atmosphere hazard. Vaporization inside an unventilated space can silently create a lethal atmosphere with no visible warning.
2. Enclosed buildings housing oil and gas process equipment, especially climate-controlled structures built for operator comfort in extreme weather, need installed gas detection and ventilation appropriate to the hazards of the equipment inside, not just insulation for temperature control.
3. Personal gas monitors provide protection only when actually worn and turned on. A device left in a truck offers no warning at the moment it matters, and company policy needs an enforced verification step, not just an issued device, to close this gap.
4. Lone workers performing tasks with any toxic or oxygen-displacement risk are in a uniquely vulnerable position, since no coworker is present to notice a collapse or call for help. Facilities relying on lone workers in hazardous environments need engineering controls (detection, ventilation, monitoring) that don't depend on a second person being present to catch a developing emergency.
