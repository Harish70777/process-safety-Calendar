## What Happened

At approximately 5:15 a.m. on January 15, 2007, a carbon steel piping spool failed catastrophically in the crude unit at the Chevron Richmond Refinery in California, igniting a fire. The failed piping was later found to contain a low percentage of silicon, well below the level needed to resist sulfidation corrosion, the same gradual, sulfur-driven thinning mechanism that would cause a far larger, fatal pipe failure at the same refinery five and a half years later. One nearby employee sustained minor burns and was treated and released from the hospital. Contra Costa Health Services issued a shelter-in-place order for surrounding residents, lifting it later that morning once the fire was contained. Following this incident, Chevron's own internal engineering group issued a technical report specifically recommending 100 percent component inspection of high-temperature carbon steel piping susceptible to sulfidation corrosion across the refinery. That recommendation was not implemented at the Richmond refinery in the years that followed. When CSB later investigated the catastrophic August 6, 2012 pipe rupture and fire at the same refinery, which engulfed 19 employees in a vapor cloud and sent roughly 15,000 residents to seek medical treatment, investigators found the specific piping component that failed in 2012 fell within exactly the kind of high-risk piping this 2007 incident had already flagged for priority inspection.

## Root Causes

- **Sulfidation corrosion in low-silicon carbon steel piping caused the failure:** the piping spool that ruptured had a silicon content low enough to make it significantly more vulnerable to sulfur-driven corrosion than piping built to later material standards.
- **A resulting company-wide inspection recommendation was never implemented at Richmond:** Chevron's own internal technical report following this incident specifically called for 100 percent inspection of similarly vulnerable piping, but this program was not carried out at the Richmond refinery before the far more severe 2012 incident.
- **A subsequent management of change narrowed the scope of a related upgrade:** CSB's broader investigation found that a later recommendation to upgrade piping metallurgy to a more corrosion-resistant grade was implemented through an MOC process that narrowed its scope, allowing the specific component that ultimately failed in 2012 to remain unupgraded.
- **This incident provided direct, specific advance warning of the failure mode that recurred:** the 2007 fire wasn't a generic near-miss; it was the same damage mechanism, in the same piping system, at the same refinery, identified with enough specificity to generate an internal recommendation that, if followed, would very likely have prevented the 2012 disaster.

## Lessons Learned

1. A facility's own internal engineering recommendations, generated in direct response to an actual incident, carry the same weight as an external regulatory finding. This 2007 incident produced a specific, actionable inspection recommendation from within Chevron's own technical staff, not a vague or speculative concern.
2. Management of change reviews for safety upgrades need scrutiny for scope creep. CSB found a later MOC process that was meant to implement a corrosion-resistant piping upgrade instead narrowed its own scope, leaving the eventually catastrophic component out of the upgrade.
3. A near-miss or moderate-consequence incident involving a well-understood, recurring industry hazard, like sulfidation corrosion, deserves treatment as a serious leading indicator, not a contained, closed event. The five-and-a-half-year gap between this 2007 fire and the fatal 2012 rupture represented real, available time to act on the warning this incident provided.
4. This incident, together with the 2012 disaster already documented elsewhere in this database, forms one of the clearest examples in the historical record of an organization receiving a direct, specific warning about an exact failure mode and not closing the gap before it recurred with far more severe consequences.
