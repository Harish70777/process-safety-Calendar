## What Happened

At a poultry processing plant using liquid nitrogen to rapidly freeze prepared food products, a "bubbler tube", part of the liquid nitrogen control system in a freezer room, was bent, likely during maintenance work. The bent tube disabled the system's ability to monitor and regulate nitrogen levels, allowing the freezer room to fill with an unsafe quantity of liquid nitrogen that overflowed and rapidly vaporized into an odorless, invisible cloud four to five feet high. Because liquid nitrogen vaporizes into a gas that displaces breathable oxygen, workers in and near the freezer room began collapsing from asphyxiation. Critically, the workforce had not been trained on the deadly nature of this hazard: CSB found at least 14 employees entered the freezer room or surrounding area to investigate the commotion or attempt to rescue colleagues, not realizing the air itself had become lethal. Six workers died from asphyxiation, several while trying to save others; three additional employees and a responding firefighter were seriously injured. CSB's investigation also found that Messer, the company that leased the liquid nitrogen equipment and freezer to Foundation Food Group, had identified safety and nonconformance issues with the facility's practices but continued supplying liquid nitrogen anyway.

## Root Causes

- **A single bent tube disabled the entire safety-critical control system:** CSB explicitly found this design was vulnerable to a single point of failure, once the bubbler tube was bent, there was no independent backup to catch the resulting loss of level control.
- **No gas monitoring or warning alarms:** the facility had no atmospheric monitoring system to detect dangerously low oxygen levels or elevated nitrogen concentration, meaning there was no automated warning before or as the hazard developed.
- **Workers were never trained on the lethality of an odorless gas hazard:** employees did not understand that liquid nitrogen vaporizing in an enclosed space could silently and rapidly become fatal, a critical training gap that directly led rescuers to become victims themselves.
- **Inadequate emergency preparedness for this specific hazard:** the facility had no established procedure directing workers to stay clear of a suspected asphyxiant release and let trained responders with proper equipment handle it, rather than approaching directly.
- **The equipment supplier continued service despite known nonconformance:** Messer had identified issues with FFG's safety practices related to the equipment but did not withhold liquid nitrogen supply or otherwise escalate the concern to prevent continued unsafe use.

## Lessons Learned

1. Any safety-critical monitoring or control system with a single point of failure, one component whose damage disables the entire safeguard, needs redundancy, especially when the consequence of failure is this severe. A bent tube should not be capable of silently defeating an entire safety system.
2. Atmospheric monitoring and alarms are essential wherever cryogenic asphyxiants like liquid nitrogen are used in or near enclosed spaces, an odorless, invisible hazard absolutely requires instrumented detection, since human senses provide no warning at all.
3. Worker training on asphyxiation hazards needs to be explicit and vivid enough that employees understand not to approach a suspected gas release, however instinctively they want to help a collapsed colleague, this incident's greatest tragedy is that trying to help is what killed most of the victims.
4. Equipment suppliers who identify safety nonconformance at a customer's site carry real responsibility to escalate that concern meaningfully, continuing to supply a hazardous material despite known safety gaps at the receiving facility is not a neutral business decision.
