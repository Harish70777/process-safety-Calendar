## What Happened

For more than 100 days before the incident, a dangerous substance called "popcorn polymer" — prone to forming in high-purity butadiene processes — had been steadily accumulating and expanding inside a section of piping connected to out-of-service equipment, a "dead leg" left over when a process pump was taken offline. The growing polymer pushed against the pipe walls until, just before 1:00 a.m. the night before Thanksgiving, the section ruptured. Roughly 6,000 gallons of liquid butadiene emptied from the fractionator in under a minute, instantly vaporized into a cloud, and ignited about two minutes later. The explosion's pressure wave destroyed parts of the facility, was felt up to 30 miles away, and damaged nearby homes and buildings; contained fires fed by continuing leaks from damaged equipment burned for more than a month. The butadiene unit was completely destroyed. **Note on injury count:** CSB's own press release described the injuries as "two TPC employees and a security contractor," while a U.S. Department of Justice crime-victim notice citing the CSB report directly by page number states four TPC employees and one contractor were injured. This entry uses the DOJ figure (5 total) as it cites the report's specific page, but readers should be aware of this discrepancy between sources.

## Root Causes

- **An unaddressed dead leg accumulated a known hazard for months:** the piping section that failed had been sitting disconnected from active flow, allowing popcorn polymer to build up undetected and unmanaged for well over 100 days.
- **A 2016 internal hazard analysis recommendation was never implemented:** three years before the explosion, TPC's own internal hazard analysis had recommended regularly flushing piping connected to out-of-service equipment — a step CSB concluded would likely have prevented this exact failure. It was never acted on.
- **No remote emergency isolation valves:** the facility lacked remotely operated isolation capability that could have limited the scale of the explosions once the initial release began.
- **No policy for shutting down and cleaning units after high popcorn polymer levels:** CSB found TPC did not have sufficient internal procedures instructing employees to shut down and clean butadiene units after experiencing elevated popcorn polymer readings — treating a known warning sign as something to monitor rather than act on.

## Lessons Learned

1. A hazard analysis recommendation that's identified but never implemented offers zero protection — this incident is a direct case of exactly the fix that would have prevented it sitting unactioned for three years.
2. Dead legs in high-purity chemical service need an active, ongoing management program — flushing, monitoring, or elimination — not a one-time installation decision followed by years of neglect.
3. Elevated readings of a known hazardous byproduct (like popcorn polymer) need a clear, mandatory response procedure — shut down and clean — rather than being treated as an operational data point to note and continue past.
4. Remote isolation capability remains one of the most consistently recurring gaps across incidents in this database — its absence here, as in several other entries, directly extended how severe the consequence became once a release began.
