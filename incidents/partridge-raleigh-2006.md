## What Happened

Four contractors from Stringer's Oilfield Services arrived at the Partridge-Raleigh oilfield in rural Smith County, Mississippi, to weld piping connections between two oil storage tanks. In preparation, only one of the tanks involved, holding crude oil along with flammable vapors including ethyl benzene, xylene, toluene, and naphthalene, was emptied and cleaned; the adjacent tanks were not. The welder used a lit oxy-acetylene welding torch itself to test one tank for the presence of flammable vapor, detecting none, and the crew proceeded with the job. The foreman and two other maintenance workers assisted the welder from on top of the tanks, connected by a utility ladder laid between them. As the welding began, sparks ignited flammable vapor venting from one of the still-uncleaned tanks. The flame flashed back into the tanks, and pressure from the burning vapor blew the tops off two of them. Three of the four workers were thrown by the force of the explosion and died from blunt force trauma. The fourth, the welder, suffered a broken ankle and hip but survived; CSB attributed his survival specifically to the safety harness he was wearing, which kept him from falling all the way to the ground. CSB's subsequent investigation found OSHA had not inspected either Partridge-Raleigh or Stringer's Oilfield Services in the three years before the explosion, and had not conducted a single planned inspection at any of the nearly 6,000 oilfields across Mississippi in the preceding five years.

## Root Causes

- **Not all tanks involved in the hot work were emptied and cleaned:** only one of the tanks being connected by welding had gone through proper preparation; the others still contained crude oil and its associated flammable vapors.
- **A lit welding torch was used to test for flammable vapor:** rather than using a proper combustible gas detector, the crew used the ignition source itself, the torch, to check whether vapor was present, a method that provides no real margin of safety if vapor is in fact present nearby.
- **No effective hot work procedure governed the job:** neither Partridge-Raleigh nor Stringer's Oilfield Services followed established safe practices for hot work, which CSB defines as any welding, cutting, or grinding activity capable of generating ignition sparks near flammable material.
- **Regulatory oversight of the oilfield sector was minimal:** CSB found OSHA had not inspected this specific site in three years and had conducted no planned inspections across the entire Mississippi oilfield sector, roughly 6,000 sites, in the preceding five years, removing an external check that might have caught these unsafe practices earlier.

## Lessons Learned

1. Every vessel involved in a hot work job needs to be independently prepared, emptied, cleaned, and verified, not just the one most directly being welded. Connected or adjacent tanks can still introduce a fatal hazard if they're treated as out of scope for preparation.
2. Testing for flammable vapor with the ignition source itself, as happened here with a lit welding torch, provides no genuine safety margin; a proper combustible gas detector needs to be used before any hot work begins, every time.
3. Fall protection and safety harnesses can matter even in incidents primarily caused by explosion or fire, not just falls. The one survivor here credited his survival specifically to wearing a harness that prevented a fatal fall after being thrown by the blast.
4. Regulatory inspection frequency matters as a real safety backstop, not just a compliance formality. CSB's finding that an entire state's oilfield sector had gone five years without a single planned inspection points to a systemic gap that let unsafe practices go unchecked far longer than they otherwise might have.
5. This was the fourth fatal oilfield accident CSB had investigated since 1998 at the time of this report's release, underscoring hot work around hydrocarbon storage as a persistent, recurring hazard across the oil production industry, not an isolated failure specific to this one site.
