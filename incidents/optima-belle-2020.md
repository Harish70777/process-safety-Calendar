## What Happened

Optima Belle, a toll manufacturer that processes materials under contract using its own equipment, was dehydrating a chlorinated isocyanurate compound (trade named CDB-56, a form of sodium dichloroisocyanurate dihydrate) on behalf of Clearon Corporation, using a pressure-rated rotary double cone dryer — the first time either company had used this equipment for this specific compound. During the drying process, the compound underwent an unanticipated decomposition reaction, releasing gases that pushed the dryer's internal pressure above its design limit. At approximately 10:00 p.m., the dryer exploded, releasing toxic chlorine gas; metal debris and fragments were propelled both within the facility and off-site, striking a methanol pipe that then caught fire. One Optima Belle employee was killed, two others were evaluated for respiratory irritation, and one member of the public was injured — local authorities issued a shelter-in-place order for residents within a two-mile radius. Property damage was estimated at $33.1 million. CSB's investigation found that Clearon — the company that actually owned deep knowledge of this chemical — knew from studies dating to the 1970s and 1980s that the compound could decompose at temperatures far lower than the 240–250°C figure listed on its own safety data sheet, but this more accurate, more dangerous information never made it into the documentation Optima Belle used to design the dehydration procedure.

## Root Causes

- **An inaccurate safety data sheet withheld the real hazard:** Clearon's own safety data sheet for the compound listed a decomposition temperature range the company knew, from decades-old internal studies, to be inaccurately high — Optima Belle designed its process based on information that understated the actual thermal risk.
- **Poor process knowledge transfer in a toll manufacturing arrangement:** Clearon provided process safety information to Optima Belle but lacked effective systems for managing and communicating that knowledge accurately, leaving Optima Belle with an inadequate understanding of the hazards in the material it was processing.
- **No thermal hazard assessment was performed:** neither company conducted laboratory or pilot-scale studies of this specific dehydration operation before running it at production scale, despite it being a new application of a reactive chemical in equipment neither had used for this purpose before.
- **Equipment not designed for a decomposition reaction scenario:** the dryer's cooling and pressure relief systems were not verified adequate to handle the pressure buildup a decomposition reaction could produce — a scenario the companies hadn't anticipated because they lacked accurate thermal data in the first place.
- **A regulatory gap:** CSB noted that reactive chemicals like this compound are not currently covered by OSHA's Process Safety Management standard or EPA's Risk Management Program rule, leaving this category of hazard without the regulatory safety-system requirements that apply to many other high-hazard processes.

## Lessons Learned

1. In any toll manufacturing or contract processing arrangement, the company that owns deep knowledge of a chemical's hazards carries real responsibility for ensuring that knowledge — including inconvenient historical data — actually reaches the company doing the processing. An outdated or optimistic safety data sheet doesn't protect anyone.
2. Running a reactive chemical through equipment or a process neither party has used before is exactly the situation that calls for laboratory or pilot-scale thermal hazard testing first — skipping that step because the compound seems "well-known" on paper is a real, addressable gap.
3. Pressure vessel design — including relief and cooling system sizing — needs to account for the worst credible reaction scenario (like an unexpected decomposition), not just normal operating conditions, especially when processing a compound with known or suspected reactive hazards.
4. Reactive chemical hazards sitting outside current PSM and RMP regulatory coverage is a real industry-wide gap, not a one-off technicality — CSB has flagged this pattern across multiple investigations in this database (Bayer CropScience, Airgas), and it's worth any facility handling reactive materials treating rigorous hazard analysis as necessary regardless of whether it's legally mandated.
