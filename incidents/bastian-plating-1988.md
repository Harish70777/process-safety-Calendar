## What Happened

At approximately 5:00 to 5:30 a.m. on June 28, 1988, a worker at the Bastian Plating Company in Auburn, Indiana, was cleaning a five-foot-deep vat in the facility's sub-basement using muriatic acid. The vat had previously held zinc cyanide, and residual cyanide compounds reacted with the acid to generate hydrogen cyanide gas, a highly toxic substance. The worker collapsed while inside the confined space. Three coworkers, seeing him collapse, attempted to rescue him without any respiratory protection and were themselves overcome by the fumes. A fifth employee, Craig Fogel, was also attempting a rescue when another worker pulled him away from the vat, an intervention that likely saved his life, though he was still critically injured and hospitalized. Four workers died that day; a fifth victim died two days later, bringing the total to five fatalities. Twenty additional people were injured, including three Auburn police officers and roughly a dozen firefighters who responded to the scene and were treated for cyanide contamination exposure, with symptoms including nausea and headaches. This incident was described at the time as the worst confined-space industrial accident in U.S. history.

## Root Causes

- **Incompatible chemicals were mixed during a routine cleaning task:** muriatic acid used to clean the vat reacted with residual zinc cyanide compounds still present from the vat's prior use, generating deadly hydrogen cyanide gas.
- **The task wasn't recognized as a confined-space hazard requiring special precautions:** cleaning a five-foot-deep vat in a sub-basement was treated as routine maintenance rather than confined-space entry work requiring atmospheric testing and specific safety protocols.
- **Coworkers attempted rescue without any respiratory protection:** the instinctive response to a collapsed worker, entering the same hazardous space to help, multiplied the casualties rather than mitigating them, since none of the would-be rescuers had protective equipment.
- **The vat was not purged of residual cyanide compounds before cleaning began:** whatever cleaning or rinsing process preceded the acid application wasn't sufficient to remove cyanide residue capable of generating toxic gas upon acid contact.

## Lessons Learned

1. Cleaning any vessel that previously held cyanide compounds with an acid-based cleaning agent requires verification that no cyanide residue remains, given the well-documented reaction between acids and cyanide compounds to produce deadly hydrogen cyanide gas.
2. Confined space entry procedures, including atmospheric testing before entry and use of appropriate respiratory protection, need to apply to any enclosed vessel work, not just tasks that are obviously labeled hazardous. A vat used for a cleaning task can carry the same fatal risk as one used for the original hazardous process.
3. Rescue response training for coworkers needs to specifically address the danger of entering a hazardous atmosphere without protection, even when the instinct to immediately help a collapsed colleague is powerful. This incident's casualty count grew specifically because untrained, unequipped rescuers followed that instinct into the same lethal environment.
4. Responding emergency personnel, including police and firefighters, need advance information about the specific hazard present at an industrial site before entering, since a dozen or more responders here were themselves exposed and required treatment during what should have been a controlled rescue operation.
