## What Happened

In the packaging area of a chemical distribution facility, an operator was filling a 300-gallon portable steel tank ("tote") with ethyl acetate, a flammable solvent, using a technique known as splash filling, pouring the liquid in from above rather than filling from the bottom, a method known to generate static electricity more readily. The operator secured the fill nozzle with a steel weight and stepped away; moments later, a popping sound signaled the tote had ignited. Employees attempted to fight the fire with a handheld extinguisher before evacuating as it grew into a series of explosions, destroying the facility's main warehouse structure. CSB's investigation found that the fill nozzle and hose assembly weren't designed for flammable liquid transfer at all, and none of the conductive metal components involved, the nozzle, hose, and the steel weight suspended by a wire, were bonded and grounded, leaving every metal surface involved isolated and free to accumulate a static charge. This was the second Barton Solvents facility fire in three months: a similar static-spark, bonding-and-grounding failure had destroyed the company's Wichita, Kansas facility in July 2007.

## Root Causes

- **Inadequate electrical bonding and grounding:** none of the metal components in the fill nozzle, hose, and steel weight were electrically connected to each other or to ground, leaving them susceptible to accumulating and discharging static electricity.
- **Equipment not designed for flammable liquid transfer:** the nozzle and hose being used were not intended for this kind of service, meaning the equipment itself lacked the design features that would normally control static risk during flammable liquid handling.
- **Splash filling technique used for a flammable solvent:** pouring liquid from above rather than using a proper submerged or bottom-fill method is a known higher-risk practice for generating static charge, and was used here without adequate compensating controls.
- **No automatic fire suppression in the packaging area:** the area where the fire started had no sprinkler system and was directly adjoined to the flammable liquid storage warehouse, allowing the fire to spread into a much larger inventory.
- **A near-identical failure had already occurred at a sister facility three months earlier:** the July 2007 Wichita explosion involved the same underlying static-spark and bonding/grounding failure mode, the lesson from that incident had not yet translated into corrected practice at this second site.

## Lessons Learned

1. Any equipment used to transfer flammable liquids needs to be specifically rated and designed for that service, repurposed general-purpose nozzles and hoses may lack the bonding continuity that flammable-service equipment is specifically built to provide.
2. Splash filling of flammable liquids is a known static-generating practice; submerged or bottom-fill methods that minimize turbulence and free-fall are the safer standard where feasible.
3. Packaging and transfer areas handling flammable liquids need automatic fire suppression, especially where they're directly connected to bulk storage, a fire that starts small in a packaging operation can rapidly become a warehouse-scale event without that layer of protection.
4. A serious incident at one company facility needs to trigger an urgent, company-wide review of every other facility performing similar operations, this is the second time in this database (after Packaging Corporation of America) where the same failure mode repeated at a sister site within a short window, this time just three months apart.
