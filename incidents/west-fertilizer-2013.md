## What Happened

A fire broke out inside a wooden storage building at the West Fertilizer Company facility, which held roughly 30 tons of fertilizer-grade ammonium nitrate (FGAN) in wood-framed bins. Volunteer firefighters and emergency responders arrived and began fighting the fire, unaware of just how close they were standing to a detonation hazard, ammonium nitrate can transition from a contained fire to a detonation under the right conditions, and the combustible building materials and lack of any automatic sprinkler system meant the fire had already been burning uncontrolled for many minutes. The FGAN detonated, killing 15 people (12 of them emergency responders), injuring roughly 260, and destroying or damaging homes, a school, and a nursing home in the surrounding town.

## Root Causes

- **Combustible building construction:** the FGAN was stored in a wood-framed building with wood bins, construction materials that let an ordinary fire develop and directly heat the ammonium nitrate stockpile.
- **No automatic fire suppression:** the facility had no sprinkler system, so nothing suppressed the fire in its early stages before responders arrived and before it grew large enough to threaten detonation.
- **Inadequate facility siting relative to the surrounding community:** homes, a school, and a nursing home were sited close enough to the facility that a worst-case event caused mass casualties and destruction well beyond the plant boundary.
- **No hazard recognition by first responders:** firefighters and other emergency personnel were not aware of the detonation risk from a sustained fire near ammonium nitrate and approached the fire directly rather than from a safe standoff distance.

## Lessons Learned

1. Ammonium nitrate storage requires non-combustible construction and, ideally, automatic fire suppression, this is a solvable engineering problem, not an inherent risk of the material.
2. Facility siting decisions for hazardous storage need to account for worst-case consequence distances, not just current land use, communities can and do grow up around older facilities.
3. Emergency responders need to know, in advance, which local facilities store detonatable materials and what standoff distance a fire there requires, this information has to reach local fire departments before an incident, not be discovered during one.
4. This incident directly drove changes to NFPA fertilizer storage codes and increased EPA/OSHA scrutiny of ammonium nitrate storage facilities nationwide, a strong example of how facility-level failures become industry-wide regulatory change.
