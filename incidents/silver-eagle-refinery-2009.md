## What Happened

A 10-inch pipe at the bottom of a reactor in the refinery's mobile distillate dewaxing unit — in service since 1993 and carrying pressurized hydrogen — catastrophically ruptured. CSB's metallurgical analysis found no inspection records for the pipe at any point in its service life; sulfur compounds in the process stream had caused sulfidation corrosion to progressively thin the pipe walls, undetected, over 16 years. The rupture released a large quantity of hydrogen that ignited immediately, and the resulting blast wave traveled from the refinery into an adjacent residential subdivision, damaging more than 100 homes — two severely, including one knocked completely off its foundation. CSB's chairman noted the incident was, by chance alone, non-injurious: no one happened to be in the immediate area when the pipe failed. This was the same refinery's second major incident that year — a separate flash fire in January 2009 from a different storage tank had seriously burned four workers.

## Root Causes

- **Sulfidation corrosion, undetected for the pipe's entire service life:** the same corrosion mechanism seen in other refinery incidents in this database (Chevron Richmond) progressively thinned the pipe wall with no inspection ever having caught it.
- **No inspection records existed for the failed pipe:** despite being in service since 1993, CSB found no evidence the pipe had ever been inspected — a complete gap in the mechanical integrity program for this specific piece of equipment.
- **Industry reliance on inspection strategy over inherently safer design:** CSB's chairman explicitly linked this incident to a broader pattern seen across multiple refinery investigations — mechanical integrity programs that emphasize periodic inspection rather than using inherently safer materials or designs to control known damage mechanisms like sulfidation corrosion in the first place.
- **Facility proximity to residential development:** the refinery's location adjacent to a subdivision meant a process failure inside the fence line had a direct, serious consequence for neighboring homes and residents.

## Lessons Learned

1. Sulfidation corrosion is a well-documented, well-understood damage mechanism in refineries processing sulfur-containing streams — any piping in this service without a documented, current inspection history is a real, not theoretical, risk.
2. Relying primarily on periodic inspection to catch corrosion, rather than addressing the underlying susceptibility through material selection or design, means any single missed or absent inspection cycle becomes a direct path to failure — inherently safer design reduces that dependency.
3. A "no injuries" outcome from a piping failure of this severity is genuinely a matter of chance, not evidence of adequate safety margin — treat near-misses of this type with the same corrective rigor as if the outcome had been fatal.
4. Facility siting near residential areas raises the stakes of any mechanical integrity gap — a corrosion failure that might otherwise be a contained industrial incident becomes a community-scale event when process equipment sits close to homes.
