## What Happened

At a dry corn milling facility that had recently connected the discharge of two rotary gap mills in an effort to increase production, employees working the night shift smelled smoke and began investigating its source within the milling process areas. While they were still searching, an explosion occurred, followed by fire. CSB determined the likely origin was a "smoldering nest" — an accumulation of corn product that had ignited inside process piping. That initial explosion generated a flame front that spread through interconnected piping into other buildings, dislodging accumulated dust from overhead surfaces throughout the facility and fueling a cascade of secondary explosions. Five employees were killed and 14 others injured, out of 19 people on site that night; the facility was destroyed, causing over $15 million in property damage. CSB's investigation found that although many of the products handled at the mill met the technical definition of combustible dust, the company neither understood nor formally recognized the hazard — no dust hazard analysis had ever been performed, and fugitive dust was managed only under a food safety program, not a combustible dust safety program. Investigators also found the facility's safety culture had normalized smoldering fires as a routine, unremarkable occurrence rather than a serious hazard signal.

## Root Causes

- **No dust hazard analysis was ever performed:** despite handling materials that met the definition of combustible dust, Didion never conducted the kind of formal analysis that would have identified and driven mitigation of the explosion risk.
- **Dust management was food-safety-driven, not safety-driven:** the only formal program addressing fugitive dust existed to meet food safety and sanitation standards, not to control combustible dust explosion risk — a program built for the wrong hazard entirely.
- **Interconnected process equipment allowed an initial, contained event to become a cascading disaster:** connecting the discharge of two rotary gap mills to boost production created a pathway for an explosion originating in one part of the process to propagate rapidly into other buildings.
- **Smoldering fires had been normalized as routine:** CSB found the facility's safety culture treated smoldering conditions — exactly the kind of precursor that preceded this fatal event — as an unremarkable, expected part of operations rather than an urgent hazard signal.
- **Falsified compliance records:** subsequent investigation and criminal prosecution found the company had submitted false Clean Air Act compliance certifications related to air pollution prevention equipment, environmental, and sanitation logs — a pattern of misrepresenting the facility's actual safety and environmental condition.

## Lessons Learned

1. Any facility handling combustible dust needs a dedicated dust hazard analysis, separate and distinct from food safety or sanitation programs — those programs solve a different problem and won't surface explosion risk even when they're being followed correctly.
2. Process modifications made to increase production — like connecting two mills' discharge streams — need explicit review for how they change fire and explosion propagation paths, not just throughput impact.
3. A "smoldering" condition in a facility handling combustible dust is never routine — it's a precursor event that deserves immediate, serious investigation and response, not a shrug born of past incidents that didn't escalate.
4. Falsifying compliance records doesn't just create legal exposure — it actively obscures the true state of a facility's risk from everyone, including the people making day-to-day operating decisions who may be relying on that same false picture of safety.
