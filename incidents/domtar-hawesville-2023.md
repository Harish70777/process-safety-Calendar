## What Happened

At approximately 5:05 p.m. on March 29, 2023, toxic hydrogen sulfide gas was accidentally released at Domtar Paper Company's mill in Hawesville, Kentucky, seriously injuring one operator and injuring two others. Three operators were tasked with circulating an acid-cleaning solution through process equipment to remove solid buildup impairing its performance, a task requiring one operator to stand directly over a tank and pour solid sulfamic acid powder into its opening. The tank should have contained only water at the time, but a valve had been left open, allowing a "weak wash" process stream containing sodium sulfide to enter the tank before the operators added the sulfamic acid. When the sulfamic acid combined with the sodium sulfide already present, it generated toxic hydrogen sulfide gas. The operator standing directly over the tank opening lost consciousness from the gas evolving out of the tank. A second operator, positioned elsewhere, was able to call for help over the plant radio system but lost consciousness shortly after. A third operator was seriously injured attempting to help the first, losing consciousness while trying to assist him, falling to the floor, rolling through a guardrail, and falling roughly 11 feet to a lower level of the structure. Two other Domtar employees heard the distress call and entered the area to help; all three affected operators eventually regained consciousness, with two able to walk outside unassisted and the third transported to a hospital for treatment.

## Root Causes

- **A valve was left open, allowing a contaminated process stream into the tank:** rather than containing only water as expected, the tank received a "weak wash" stream containing sodium sulfide because this valve wasn't closed.
- **The cleaning procedure did not specify that this valve should be closed during normal operation:** Domtar's own procedures didn't identify closing the weak wash valve as a required step, meaning following the written procedure as given didn't prevent the contamination.
- **Sulfamic acid was added to a tank already containing an incompatible chemical:** the operator adding the acid had no way of knowing the tank's actual contents had changed from the expected water, since the valve issue wasn't visible or flagged.
- **A coworker was seriously injured attempting to rescue the first affected operator:** the instinct to immediately help a collapsed colleague led a second responder into the same hazardous atmosphere, resulting in his own loss of consciousness and a serious fall injury.

## Lessons Learned

1. Written operating procedures need to explicitly specify the state of every relevant valve for a given task, including valves that should remain closed during normal operation, not just the valves actively involved in the task itself.
2. Adding a reactive chemical like sulfamic acid to any tank needs verification that the tank's actual contents match what's expected, especially when a valve elsewhere in the system could introduce an unexpected stream without an obvious visual indication.
3. Rescue response to a collapsed coworker in a potentially toxic atmosphere needs training that emphasizes assessing the hazard and using appropriate protection before entering, not immediate, unprotected intervention, since the instinct to help directly caused a second serious injury here.
4. Combining sulfamic acid with sodium sulfide to generate hydrogen sulfide is a well-documented reactive hazard; any cleaning or maintenance procedure involving these chemicals needs explicit safeguards against unintended cross-contamination between process streams that could bring them together unexpectedly.
