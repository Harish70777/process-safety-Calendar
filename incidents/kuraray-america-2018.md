## What Happened

During startup of a chemical reactor following a scheduled maintenance shutdown, pressure inside the vessel built to excessive levels through a chain of process safety management failures. When the reactor's high-pressure alarm sounded, a clear signal of a serious developing problem, workers were not evacuated from the area. The reactor's emergency pressure relief system activated as designed, but its discharge piping was aimed horizontally into the air near where workers were positioned, rather than directed to a safe location. Flammable ethylene vapor vented directly into the occupied area and found an ignition source, causing a fire. 23 workers, all adult men in Kuraray's maintenance department, were injured, most from burns and from injuries sustained while running to escape, including topical burns and injured backs and knees. CSB's investigation identified 17 distinct safety issues contributing to the incident, ranging from the relief system's discharge design to disabled safety interlocks and inadequate alarm response procedures.

## Root Causes

- **Emergency relief system discharged toward an occupied area:** the single most direct cause CSB identified, had the pressure relief system been designed to vent to a safe location instead of horizontally near workers, the flammable gas released should not have harmed anyone at all.
- **Nonessential workers present during startup and upset conditions:** personnel who didn't need to be in the immediate area during this inherently higher-risk operating phase were not kept clear of it.
- **The high-pressure alarm was not treated as an evacuation trigger:** CSB explicitly found Kuraray should have evacuated workers when the alarm sounded, since it was signaling a serious reactor problem, the alarm response procedure did not translate a clear warning into protective action.
- **Safety interlock disabling:** among the 17 safety issues identified, a disabled safety interlock was a contributing factor, a protective system that should have been active during this operation had been taken out of service.
- **Broader process safety management gaps:** CSB's full list of 17 issues spanned process hazard analysis, operating procedures, operator training, equipment design, and alarm management, indicating this wasn't a single-point failure but a chain of compounding gaps.

## Lessons Learned

1. Emergency pressure relief systems must discharge to a genuinely safe location, this incident is about as direct a demonstration as exists that a relief system doing exactly what it was designed to do can still cause mass injury if its discharge path wasn't designed with occupied areas in mind.
2. A high-pressure alarm during startup is an evacuation trigger, not a data point to monitor, build explicit, unambiguous alarm response procedures that translate a serious warning signal directly into protective action, especially during known higher-risk operating phases like startup.
3. Minimize the number of personnel present in hazardous areas during startup and upset conditions to only those essential to the task, every additional person in the area during a known higher-risk operation increases the potential injury toll if something goes wrong.
4. Safety interlocks that get disabled, for whatever operational reason, need a rigorous, tracked process for re-enabling them before resuming normal operation; a disabled interlock is a removed layer of protection, and its status needs active management, not passive assumption that it will be restored.
