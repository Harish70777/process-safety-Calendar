## What Happened

On November 13, 2005, a series of explosions occurred over roughly an hour at the No. 101/102 Petrochemical Plant, part of the Jilin Petrochemical Corporation complex operated by the state-owned China National Petroleum Corporation in Jilin City, China. The explosions originated at a nitration unit for aniline production, where processing tower T-102 became jammed during abnormal operations. A worker attempting to clear the clog mishandled the process, and the facility's monitoring systems failed to detect the developing problem before it escalated. The explosions shattered windows 100 to 200 meters away and forced the evacuation of an estimated 10,000 residents, including students at two nearby universities, amid fears of further explosions and chemical exposure. Fires burned until the following morning. Five workers were killed, and 70 others were injured. The explosions released approximately 100 tons of pollutants, principally benzene and nitrobenzene, into the nearby Songhua River, contaminating the water supply for millions of people across Jilin and Harbin, a downstream city of roughly four million residents. Chinese government officials took approximately ten days to publicly disclose the spill; in the interim, local authorities in Harbin told residents that water service was being suspended for routine maintenance, prompting public disbelief and unfounded rumors of an impending earthquake. The toxic slick eventually traveled roughly 80 kilometers down the Amur River into Russia, with benzene concentrations at one point measured at 108 times the national safety standard.

## Root Causes

- **A processing tower became jammed during abnormal operating conditions:** the T-102 nitration tower's clog created conditions outside normal operations that the facility wasn't adequately prepared to handle safely.
- **A worker mishandled the process while attempting to clear the jam:** the specific action taken to address the blockage directly contributed to triggering the explosion sequence.
- **Monitoring systems failed to detect the developing problem:** the facility's instrumentation and monitoring didn't provide adequate warning of the abnormal conditions before they escalated to an explosion.
- **Government disclosure of the resulting environmental disaster was significantly delayed:** roughly ten days passed before officials publicly acknowledged the chemical spill into the Songhua River, during which affected communities were given misleading explanations for water service disruptions.

## Lessons Learned

1. Clearing a jammed or clogged process vessel needs a documented, hazard-evaluated procedure, not an ad hoc response improvised in the moment, especially in a nitration process handling reactive, toxic chemicals like aniline and benzene precursors.
2. Monitoring and instrumentation systems need verified capability to detect abnormal process conditions specifically during non-routine operations, since routine monitoring that works during normal operation may not adequately flag the unique hazards of an abnormal condition like a jammed tower.
3. Downstream water contamination risk from a chemical facility explosion needs to be part of emergency response planning from the outset, given how this incident's consequences extended far beyond the immediate blast area to affect water supplies for millions of people across international borders.
4. Transparent, timely public disclosure following an industrial incident with community health implications is itself a critical safety practice. The ten-day delay here, compounded by misleading initial explanations, undermined public trust and delayed protective action that affected communities could have taken sooner.
