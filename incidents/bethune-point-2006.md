## What Happened

Three City of Daytona Beach employees were removing a steel roof, damaged in a prior hurricane, that covered two chemical storage tanks at the Bethune Point Wastewater Treatment Plant, one tank empty and the other holding 3,000 gallons of methanol used as a nutrient source for bacteria in the wastewater treatment process. On January 11, 2006, the lead mechanic and a third worker were using an acetylene cutting torch to cut the metal roof into sections, working directly above the methanol tank's vent. At approximately 11:15 a.m., sparks showering down from the torch ignited methanol vapors escaping from the vent, creating a fireball on top of the tank. The flame flashed back down into the tank itself, causing an internal explosion that ruptured methanol piping and fittings throughout the system. The resulting fire engulfed the tank and the workers positioned above it. Two municipal employees were killed, and a third was severely injured. CSB's investigation found that the methanol piping and valves connected to the tank were constructed of PVC plastic rather than steel, and that the tank vent was equipped with an aluminum flame arrester, both factors that contributed to how the explosion cascaded into a larger fire once ignition occurred.

## Root Causes

- **Hot work was performed directly above a flammable vapor source:** cutting with a torch immediately above the methanol tank's vent placed an ignition source directly in the path of the flammable vapors the vent was designed to release.
- **The City of Daytona Beach had not implemented adequate hot work controls:** CSB found no effective program in place to identify the hazards of welding or cutting near flammable material storage before this specific task began.
- **The city's hazard communication program failed to convey methanol's dangers:** employees performing the roof removal were not adequately informed of the specific flammability hazards methanol vapor posed at the point where they were working.
- **Non-steel piping materials contributed to the failure's severity:** the methanol piping and valve system was built from PVC rather than steel, a design choice CSB and the piping designer's own review found unsuited to safely containing methanol under the conditions of an ignition event.
- **No evaluation existed for non-routine tasks like this one:** the City of Daytona Beach had no program to assess the safety of infrequent or one-off jobs, like hurricane-damage roof removal, before workers began them.

## Lessons Learned

1. Hot work near any vent releasing flammable vapor needs to be recognized and controlled as a distinct, serious hazard, including physically relocating the work, monitoring the atmosphere, or temporarily isolating the vent source, rather than proceeding based on the roof simply needing repair.
2. Hazard communication needs to actually convey the specific risk of the chemicals present at a job site, not just note their presence in general terms. Workers cutting metal directly above a methanol vent needed to understand precisely what made that location dangerous.
3. Materials selection for chemical piping and valves needs to account for fire and explosion resistance appropriate to the chemical's hazard class, not just routine handling. CSB's finding that PVC piping and an aluminum flame arrester were used here points to a design gap that made the consequences of ignition significantly worse.
4. Non-routine or infrequent tasks, like removing a storm-damaged roof, deserve the same hazard evaluation as regular operations, arguably more, since workers and management have less established experience recognizing the specific dangers a new, one-off task might introduce.
5. This incident is part of a broader, documented pattern CSB has tracked across multiple industries: welding and cutting near tanks containing flammable material is one of the most frequent and deadly categories of incident the agency investigates, a pattern that shows up again elsewhere in this database (Motiva Delaware City, Sunoco Nederland, PCA DeRidder).
