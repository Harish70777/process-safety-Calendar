## What Happened

Under pressure to resume production of the pesticides methomyl and Larvin after a lengthy maintenance shutdown, plant management began startup of the process before completing a proper pre-startup safety review and while bypassing safety interlocks, including starting up with computer control systems that had not been fully checked out. During the rushed startup, a thermal runaway reaction developed inside a pressure vessel called a residue treater. The vessel over-pressurized and violently exploded, flying roughly 50 feet through the air, demolishing process equipment, and colliding with the methomyl manufacturing unit in a large fireball. Two operators working nearby were killed; eight others, plant workers, contractors, and responding firefighters, reported symptoms of chemical exposure. Investigators found the exploding vessel's trajectory came close to striking piping atop a nearby tank holding methyl isocyanate (MIC), the same chemical responsible for the Bhopal disaster, which could have caused a far more catastrophic toxic release had the debris traveled a slightly different path.

## Root Causes

- **Premature startup driven by production pressure:** the process was restarted before verification steps were complete, specifically because of pressure to resume production of two commercially important pesticides.
- **Bypassed safety interlocks:** the plant proceeded with startup while bypassing safety interlocks and using computer control systems that had not been checked out, CSB explicitly called this combination unacceptable given that startups are inherently hazardous operations even under normal conditions.
- **Inadequate pre-startup safety review:** a comprehensive pre-startup checkout, had it been performed as required, would very likely have revealed the multiple dangerous conditions present before the vessel was ever brought back online.
- **Operator fatigue:** CSB examined evidence that unit operators had worked very high overtime in the three months before the accident, averaging nearly 20 hours of overtime per week, with repeated 12- to 18-hour shifts and few days off, as a possible contributing factor.
- **Poor emergency communication:** more than 10 minutes elapsed before the company was able to alert emergency dispatch, and even then the information provided was inadequate; the company also delayed and obstructed information sharing with local emergency responders and the public afterward.

## Lessons Learned

1. Production pressure is never sufficient justification for skipping or rushing a pre-startup safety review, the entire purpose of that review is to catch exactly the kind of dangerous condition that a rushed startup creates.
2. Bypassing safety interlocks during startup, inherently one of the highest-risk operating modes, compounds risk rather than simply removing an inconvenience; treat any interlock bypass during startup as requiring the same rigor as a full management-of-change review.
3. Sustained operator overtime at this level (repeated 12+ hour shifts, minimal days off, sustained over months) is itself a safety risk factor that deserves the same seriousness as an equipment deficiency, fatigue degrades the very judgment operators need most during hazardous operations like startup.
4. Facility siting and equipment layout should account for the trajectory of foreseeable equipment failures, especially near highly toxic inventories, this incident came within a matter of feet and degrees of trajectory from potentially triggering a Bhopal-scale event at a U.S. facility, and that near-miss deserves to be treated with the same weight as if it had actually happened.
