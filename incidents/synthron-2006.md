## What Happened

Synthron manufactured acrylic polymers for paint and coating additives inside a 1,500-gallon batch reactor, reacting acrylic monomers in a mixture of flammable solvents. To fill a customer order that exceeded the plant's normal batch size, managers decided to combine what would normally be two smaller batches into one larger run, increasing the total monomer charge by 45 percent and adding all of the extra material during the reaction's initial stage. None of the staff reviewing this change recognized how significantly it would increase the reaction's heat output; CSB's later testing found the change more than doubled the maximum heat the reaction would generate. That heat exceeded the cooling capacity of the reactor's overhead condenser, which, CSB discovered, had not been cleaned or inspected in 30 years and was fouled with scale, rust, and sediment that had already degraded its cooling capability by at least 25 percent. The reaction ran away, and rising pressure forced solvent vapor out through the reactor's hatchway, secured with only 4 of the 18 clamps the manufacturer recommended. Operators fled the production building and gathered just outside a doorway; a flammable vapor cloud that had formed inside ignited and exploded, destroying the facility and fatally burning a maintenance supervisor who had remained inside. Fourteen others, including two passersby, were injured. The blast broke windows a third of a mile away and damaged two churches and a home, both later condemned.

## Root Causes

- **A batch size increase was made without recognizing its reactive hazard consequences:** the decision to combine two batches into one, adding 45 percent more monomer, was reviewed by on-site chemists and managers, but none of them understood how much it would increase the reaction's heat output.
- **A severely fouled condenser had lost much of its cooling capacity:** three decades without cleaning or inspection left the cooling equipment unable to remove heat at anywhere near its intended rate, right when the modified batch demanded more cooling capacity than ever.
- **Inexperienced staff without reactive chemistry training:** CSB found the plant had no chemical engineers on staff and none of the key personnel evaluating the batch change had prior polymerization experience or training in recognizing reactive hazards.
- **No process hazard analysis had ever been performed on the reactor:** Synthron had never conducted the kind of systematic hazard review that could have identified the reactor's vulnerability to exactly this kind of runaway scenario.
- **A longstanding practice of improperly securing the reactor hatch:** using only 4 of 18 recommended clamps meant the hatch gave way at a pressure well below the vessel's rated working pressure, directly enabling the vapor release that led to the explosion.

## Lessons Learned

1. Any change to a batch chemical process, including something that seems as straightforward as combining two smaller batches into one, needs a documented review specifically evaluating its effect on reaction heat output, not just a general read-through by available staff.
2. Cooling equipment critical to controlling an exothermic reaction needs a real inspection and cleaning schedule. Three decades without maintenance left this condenser operating at a fraction of its rated capacity without anyone realizing it.
3. Personnel evaluating reactive chemical hazards need genuine training and experience in reaction engineering, not just general chemistry background. This incident's investigators found that experience gap directly contributed to nobody catching the danger in the batch-size decision.
4. A process handling reactive, exothermic chemistry deserves a real process hazard analysis, especially when that process has apparently operated for years without one. The absence of a PHA here meant this reactor's specific vulnerabilities were never systematically identified before they caused a fatality.
