## What Happened

On July 19, 2009, a hydrocarbon gas release occurred in the hydrogen fluoride (HF) alkylation unit at CITGO's Corpus Christi East Refinery in Texas. CSB's investigation determined that a control valve suddenly failed, causing a blockage of liquid that triggered violent shaking in the process recycle piping. This shaking broke two threaded pipe connections, releasing highly flammable hydrocarbons that ignited, causing extensive damage and a fire that burned for several days. The fire critically injured one CITGO employee, and a second employee was treated for possible hydrogen fluoride exposure during emergency response activities. The incident released approximately 42,000 pounds of hydrogen fluoride from equipment and piping within the unit. CITGO's water spray system was intended to absorb the released HF, but during the response, the refinery nearly exhausted its stored water supply and had to begin pumping seawater as a backup; this transfer experienced multiple failures, including ruptures of barge-to-shore transfer hoses and water pump engine failures. CSB, citing scientific literature on HF absorption, later concluded the water mitigation system in place was inadequate for a release of this scale. In December 2009, before completing its final investigation, CSB took the unusual step of issuing urgent safety recommendations, calling on CITGO to immediately improve its emergency water mitigation system and commission third-party safety audits of its hydrogen fluoride units at both this refinery and its Lemont, Illinois facility.

## Root Causes

- **A control valve failed suddenly, causing violent piping shake:** the failure created a liquid blockage that generated enough mechanical shaking in the process recycle piping to break physical connections.
- **Threaded pipe connections broke under the resulting stress:** rather than a more robust welded connection, threaded connections in the affected piping gave way once subjected to the vibration from the valve failure, directly enabling the hydrocarbon release.
- **The HF water mitigation system was inadequate for a release of this scale:** CSB's review of scientific literature found the water spray system's design fell short of what would be needed to reliably absorb a hydrogen fluoride release of the magnitude that actually occurred.
- **The stored water supply for mitigation was nearly exhausted during the response:** CITGO's water reserve wasn't sufficient to sustain the mitigation effort throughout the multi-day fire, forcing an improvised seawater backup that itself experienced multiple equipment failures.

## Lessons Learned

1. Process piping subject to potential valve-failure-induced shaking needs connections and supports designed for that dynamic loading, not just static operating conditions. Threaded connections proved insufficient here once significant mechanical vibration developed.
2. Hydrogen fluoride water mitigation systems need sizing and design verified against realistic worst-case release scenarios, using current scientific understanding of HF absorption, not assumptions that may predate more recent research.
3. Emergency mitigation resources, like a stored water supply, need capacity planning for extended-duration incidents. A multi-day fire exhausted CITGO's water reserve and forced a backup plan that itself proved unreliable under pressure.
4. When CSB determines an imminent hazard exists, urgent recommendations issued before a final report is complete reflect a judgment that waiting for the full investigation process would leave a serious risk unaddressed too long, an approach worth taking seriously by any facility that receives one.
