## What Happened

During a planned shutdown, two separate contractor crews — Universal Blastco and Rimcor — were performing simultaneous maintenance in two connected process vessels, an "upflow tower" and a "downflow tower," at a pulp bleaching unit. Blastco's crew was applying fiberglass matting and flammable resin to repair the corrosion-resistant interior of the upflow tower. The resin hardened too slowly in the cold overnight temperatures, so the crew began using an electric heat gun to warm it — without communicating this decision to either Evergreen Packaging or the Rimcor crew working in the connected downflow tower. Around 5:15 a.m., the heat gun fell into a five-gallon bucket of the flammable resin, igniting a fire. The Blastco workers in the upflow tower escaped safely, but smoke and flames spread quickly through the connecting pipe into the downflow tower — constructed, like the upflow tower, of combustible fiber-reinforced plastic — fatally injuring both Rimcor workers trapped inside.

## Root Causes

- **A heat gun was used near flammable material without recognizing the ignition risk:** CSB found the heat gun was capable of reaching temperatures well above the resin's flashpoint, but the Blastco crew did not recognize this as an ignition hazard in a confined space containing flammable material.
- **No communication between the two contractor crews or the facility owner:** Blastco's decision to introduce a new ignition source (the heat gun) was never shared with Evergreen or the Rimcor crew working in the directly connected vessel — a decision that, had it been communicated, could have prompted a stop-work discussion before the fire started.
- **Inadequate pre-job planning:** CSB found Blastco had not effectively planned for the possibility that cold temperatures would slow the resin's cure time, and had no established procedure or troubleshooting guide for addressing that foreseeable problem safely.
- **Combustible materials in vessel and connecting pipe construction:** both towers and the pipe connecting them were built from fiber-reinforced plastic, which contributed to how quickly fire and smoke spread from the ignition point into the occupied downflow tower.

## Lessons Learned

1. Introducing any new ignition source into a confined space or connected process vessel — even something as seemingly minor as a heat gun — needs explicit communication and hazard review with every party working in connected spaces, not just the crew directly using it.
2. Two vessels connected by piping should be treated as a single shared hazard space for permitting and hazard communication purposes, even if different contractors consider them separate work areas.
3. Foreseeable environmental conditions (like cold temperatures slowing a resin's cure) need pre-planned safe solutions identified before work begins — improvising a fix mid-task, like introducing a heat source, is exactly when unplanned ignition risks get introduced.
4. Combustible construction materials in process vessels compound the consequence of any ignition event — where combustible construction is unavoidable for process reasons (like corrosion resistance here), hot work and heat-generating activities near those vessels need correspondingly stricter controls.
