## What Happened

On the evening of April 12, 2004, MFG Chemical was attempting its first production-scale batch of triallyl cyanurate at its Dalton, Georgia facility. The company had not adequately planned for scaling up the reaction from laboratory quantities to full production volume, and had not evaluated how much heat the reaction would actually generate at that scale. At approximately 9:30 p.m., the reaction went out of control, rapidly over-pressurizing a 4,000-gallon reactor. The runaway reaction activated an emergency vent, releasing highly toxic and flammable allyl alcohol along with toxic allyl chloride directly into the surrounding community for more than eight hours. More than 200 families were forced to evacuate their homes. Neither MFG's own personnel nor the responding Dalton Fire Department had appropriate personal protective equipment, specifically fully encapsulating chemical suits, to safely approach and address the release directly, so responders instead sprayed water on the vapor cloud from a distance to help control it, a decision that sent contaminated runoff into nearby waterways and killed aquatic life across several miles of creek. One MFG worker suffered chemical burns, and 154 people in total, including 13 police officers and 2 ambulance personnel, required decontamination and treatment for chemical exposure. MFG later paid $270,000 in civil penalties to settle a Clean Air Act enforcement action.

## Root Causes

- **No adequate hazard analysis for scaling up the reaction:** MFG moved directly from laboratory-scale testing to a full 4,000-gallon production batch without properly evaluating the heat the reaction would generate at that much larger scale.
- **Cooling and process controls were not designed for the reaction's actual behavior:** the safety systems and instrumentation in place were not adequate to prevent or safely manage a runaway exotherm once the reaction began accelerating.
- **Responders lacked the protective equipment the release actually required:** appropriately protective Level A chemical suits were needed to safely approach the toxic cloud directly, and neither the facility nor the fire department had them on hand.
- **Inadequate emergency planning at the facility, city, and county level:** CSB found gaps in emergency response planning by MFG, Whitfield County, and the city of Dalton all contributed to how severe the community impact became once the release began.

## Lessons Learned

1. Scaling up a chemical reaction from lab quantities to full production volume needs its own dedicated hazard analysis. The heat and pressure behavior of a reaction can change substantially at scale in ways lab-scale testing alone won't reveal.
2. Process safety and emergency planning need to explicitly account for reactive chemical hazards, since these can develop and escalate far faster than many other process safety scenarios, leaving little time to respond once a runaway reaction begins.
3. Community and local emergency responders need pre-established access to the appropriate protective equipment for the specific hazardous materials handled at nearby facilities. Discovering that gap during an actual release, as happened here, leaves responders with limited options.
4. First-time production runs, of a new chemical, at a new scale, or both, carry meaningfully elevated risk and deserve corresponding scrutiny beforehand. This was MFG's first production batch of this specific product, and the reaction's actual behavior caught the company without adequate controls in place.
