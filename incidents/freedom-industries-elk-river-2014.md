## What Happened

An aboveground storage tank at a chemical storage and distribution facility on the Elk River developed two small holes in its floor from pitting corrosion — the tank had not been internally inspected in at least ten years, and investigators later found evidence suggesting it may not have been inspected in 30–40 years. Crude MCHM (4-methylcyclohexyl methanol, used in coal processing), mixed with a related chemical called PPH, leaked through the holes. The tank's secondary containment wall — the engineered backup meant to catch exactly this kind of leak — had cracks and holes of its own from years of disrepair, and did not stop the chemical from escaping into the Elk River, roughly a mile and a half upstream of West Virginia American Water's treatment plant intake. The contamination forced a "do-not-use" order on tap water for up to 300,000 residents across nine counties for the better part of a week; more than 300 people sought medical treatment for possible exposure. Freedom Industries' communication about what exactly had spilled and how much was found by CSB to be deficient — the company did not disclose that a second chemical (PPH) was also part of the release until 12 days after the incident began.

## Root Causes

- **Severe, undetected pitting corrosion from lack of inspection:** the tank went at least a decade — likely far longer — without an internal inspection that could have caught the developing corrosion before it breached the tank floor.
- **Secondary containment had degraded and failed:** the containment wall specifically designed to catch a tank leak had its own cracks and holes from disrepair, defeating the layer of protection meant to prevent exactly this kind of environmental release.
- **No regulatory requirement mandated tank inspection:** at the time, aboveground chemical storage tanks like this one were not subject to a mandatory inspection regime, which meant a decade-plus inspection gap wasn't even a compliance violation.
- **Deficient emergency communication:** the company was slow and incomplete in communicating what chemicals and quantities were actually involved to the water utility, emergency responders, and the public — delaying an accurate public health response during an active contamination event.

## Lessons Learned

1. Secondary containment is a real engineered safeguard, not a symbolic one — it needs the same inspection and maintenance rigor as the primary vessel it's protecting against, since a failed containment wall provides zero actual protection when the primary tank fails.
2. The absence of a regulatory inspection mandate is not evidence that infrequent inspection is safe — this incident became one of the direct drivers for West Virginia's subsequent storage tank inspection legislation specifically because the regulatory gap had been real and consequential.
3. When a hazardous material release threatens a public water supply, complete and immediate chemical disclosure isn't optional communication — it's the information a water utility needs to know whether treatment is even possible, and how to protect the public in the meantime.
4. This incident illustrates that "no fatalities" and "catastrophic" aren't mutually exclusive — 300,000 people without safe water for days, hundreds seeking medical care, and a company bankruptcy are a genuinely severe outcome even without a single death, and deserve the same root-cause rigor as a fatal incident.
