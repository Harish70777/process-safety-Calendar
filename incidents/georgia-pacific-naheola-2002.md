## What Happened

Tank trucks periodically delivered sodium hydrosulfide (NaSH), a process chemical, to an unloading station at the Georgia-Pacific Naheola pulp and paper mill. In the 24 hours before the incident, 15 trucks had made deliveries, each losing up to five gallons of NaSH into a collection pit at the site, a normal, expected part of the unloading process. That pit drained into an acidic process sewer system, and when the spilled sodium hydrosulfide reached the acidic sewer contents, it reacted to release hydrogen sulfide gas, a highly toxic gas that can cause immediate death at concentrations as low as 700 parts per million. The toxic gas vented up through a nearby fiberglass manhole cover and engulfed a group of workers nearby. Nearly everyone affected was a contractor from Burkes Construction working on an unrelated construction project in the area, with no connection to the NaSH unloading process at all. Two contractors were killed and eight other people were injured, including construction workers and a delivery driver; responding paramedics reported symptoms consistent with hydrogen sulfide exposure themselves.

## Root Causes

- **A reactive chemical hazard built into the sewer system's basic design:** the connection allowing NaSH-contaminated water to reach an acidic sewer had existed since the plant was built decades earlier, under a previous owner, without anyone recognizing the two would react to form a lethal gas.
- **No hazard analysis of the sewer system itself:** CSB found neither Georgia-Pacific nor the prior owners had adequately analyzed or controlled the hazards of the sewer system, including the potential for hazardous chemical reactions between substances that could end up there.
- **Contractors working nearby had no reason to know a hazard existed:** the workers killed and injured were doing unrelated construction work and were never informed that toxic gas could be generated by a completely separate process happening near them.
- **A routine, accepted operational loss was actually feeding the hazard:** losing a few gallons of NaSH per delivery into the collection pit was treated as an unremarkable part of normal operations, not recognized as directly supplying the reactive ingredient that caused the release.

## Lessons Learned

1. Process sewers and drainage systems deserve the same hazard analysis rigor as process equipment. A drain connecting two different areas of a facility can become a reaction vessel if incompatible chemicals both have a path into it, even unintentionally.
2. "Normal operational loss" (like minor spillage during routine delivery) is not automatically safe just because it's expected and small. Evaluate where that lost material actually goes and what it could contact downstream.
3. Contractors and anyone working near an active industrial process need to be informed of hazards that could reach them, even from processes that seem unrelated to their own task. This incident's fatalities were entirely bystanders to the process that killed them.
4. A design flaw inherited from a previous owner or an old construction decision doesn't become less dangerous with time or changes in ownership. This hazard existed for decades across at least two corporate owners before it was recognized, only after it had already killed two people.
