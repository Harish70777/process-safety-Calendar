## What Happened

At approximately 6:40 a.m. on January 4, 1966, an operator at the Feyzin oil refinery near Lyon, France, attempted to drain water from a 1,200 cubic meter pressurized propane storage sphere through a vertical drain leg fitted with two manual isolation valves in series. Contrary to the established operating procedure, the lower valve was opened only halfway before the upper valve was opened further; when a blockage, likely ice or hydrate formed in the near-freezing conditions, suddenly cleared, propane gushed out under pressure. The valve handle used to operate the upper valve fell off and could not be reinstated, and the lower valve had frozen in its half-open position, making it impossible to stop the release. The two operators retreated and sounded the alarm. With almost no wind, the escaping propane formed a large, slowly drifting vapor cloud rather than dispersing. At approximately 7:15 to 7:30 a.m., roughly 35 minutes after the leak began, the cloud reached a car stopped on a nearby service road and ignited; the driver suffered severe burns and later died. A blowtorch-like flame quickly developed beneath the sphere, and firefighters from the refinery and nearby Lyon arrived to attempt cooling the vessel and surrounding equipment. At approximately 8:45 a.m., roughly two hours after the initial leak, the sphere suddenly ruptured in a catastrophic boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion (BLEVE), producing a fireball roughly 300 meters in diameter. The explosion instantly killed 17 to 18 people, including 11 firefighters, and injured approximately 80 more, roughly 40 of them severely. Flying debris from the explosion damaged the support legs of an adjacent propane sphere, which was not adequately fire-protected; it too eventually ruptured in a second BLEVE. Three further spheres toppled when their unprotected support legs failed in the heat, though these ruptured without exploding. The fire, which also spread to nearby crude oil and petrol storage tanks, took approximately 48 hours to bring fully under control.

## Root Causes

- **Isolation valves were operated in the wrong sequence, contrary to procedure:** opening the lower valve only halfway before fully opening the upper valve, rather than following the established draining procedure, set up the conditions for the subsequent uncontrolled release.
- **A dropped valve handle prevented reclosing the valve during the release:** once the blockage cleared and propane began gushing out, the operator was unable to refit the wrench needed to close the upper valve, removing the fastest available means of stopping the leak.
- **Both isolation valves froze in place during the release:** the extreme cooling effect of the rapidly vaporizing propane caused both valves to freeze, first the upper valve after the handle was lost, then the lower valve as well, eliminating any remaining option to isolate the leak manually.
- **Adjacent storage spheres were not adequately fire-protected:** the support legs of the neighboring spheres lacked sufficient fire protection to withstand the heat of the fire and the impact of flying debris, allowing the disaster to cascade into a second BLEVE and multiple additional vessel failures.

## Lessons Learned

1. Manual valve operating sequences specified in a procedure exist for specific technical reasons, and this incident shows how directly deviating from that sequence, even by a seemingly small margin, can directly cause a catastrophic, unrecoverable release.
2. Valves used for draining or isolating pressurized liquefied gas need engineering and maintenance verified against the specific risk of freezing during release, given that freezing valves were the direct reason this leak couldn't be manually stopped once it began.
3. Fire protection for support structures on pressurized storage vessels needs to account for both direct flame exposure and the possibility of impact damage from a nearby vessel's failure, since this disaster's escalation from one BLEVE to a second, plus multiple additional vessel failures, depended specifically on inadequate fire protection for structures that weren't the original point of failure.
4. This incident is widely credited as the event that first brought the BLEVE phenomenon into serious engineering and regulatory attention; prior to Feyzin, pressurized tanks were commonly built without specific protection against this failure mode, and this disaster directly drove changes in French legislation covering BLEVE-susceptible zone identification and minimum separation distances that influence process safety design worldwide today.
