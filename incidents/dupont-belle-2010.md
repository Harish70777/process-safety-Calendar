## What Happened

Over 33 hours in January 2010, DuPont's Belle, West Virginia plant experienced three separate toxic chemical releases. First, an alarm led operators to discover that 2,000 pounds of methyl chloride, a toxic and extremely flammable gas, had been leaking unnoticed into the atmosphere for five days. The next morning, workers found a leak in a pipe carrying oleum, producing a fuming cloud of sulfur trioxide. That afternoon, while the plant was still on a "safety pause" prompted by an earlier near-miss involving a frayed phosgene hose of the identical type, a second phosgene hose ruptured during a routine operation, spraying a 32-year veteran operator, Carl "Danny" Fish, in the face and chest with phosgene — a chemical so toxic it was used as a chemical weapon in World War I. Fish died in the hospital the following night. Two other workers who came to his aid were also exposed to phosgene but apparently without lasting effects. CSB found the hoses, made from a corrosion-susceptible material, were supposed to be replaced monthly; the one that killed Fish had not been changed in seven months.

## Root Causes

- **Overdue hose replacement:** the phosgene hose that failed was well past its required monthly replacement interval — seven months in service against a one-month standard — allowing corrosion-related degradation to progress undetected.
- **A near-identical near-miss was not treated as an urgent stop-work signal:** the fatal hose failure happened during a safety pause explicitly triggered by an earlier frayed-hose near-miss of the same type — yet operations involving the same hose design continued rather than being fully suspended pending a fix.
- **An undetected 5-day gas leak:** the methyl chloride release that started the 33-hour sequence had gone entirely unnoticed for five days, indicating a significant gap in leak detection for a toxic, flammable gas.
- **Cost considerations overrode a safer design option:** CSB found documentation showing DuPont had considered but rejected, as too costly, measures that could have prevented the fatal incident.
- **Ineffective mechanical integrity program broadly:** across all three releases, CSB identified a pattern of inadequate maintenance practices and hazard recognition, not an isolated equipment failure.

## Lessons Learned

1. A "safety pause" following a near-miss needs to actually halt the specific hazardous activity involved, not just acknowledge the risk while operations continue — this incident's fatal failure occurred using the same hose type that had already shown a warning sign hours earlier.
2. Replacement intervals for safety-critical, corrosion-prone components (like toxic gas transfer hoses) need active tracking and enforcement — a documented monthly requirement that quietly slipped to seven months provided no actual protection.
3. Leak detection systems for toxic and flammable gases need to catch releases in a timeframe far shorter than five days — an undetected leak of that duration represents a significant gap in monitoring capability.
4. When a safer design option is rejected primarily on cost grounds, that decision deserves documented, senior-level risk acceptance — not a routine engineering trade-off — especially for a chemical as acutely hazardous as phosgene. CSB's finding that DuPont, a company with a long-standing safety reputation, had these exact gaps is a sobering reminder that reputation is not a substitute for verified, current safety practice.
