Anniversary: May 27, 1994

Shell Chemical Belpre Polymer Reactor Explosion

Belpre, OH, USA · 3 fatalities, 0 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisMechanical IntegrityEmergency Planning

What Happened

At approximately 6:25 a.m. on May 27, 1994, the Friday before Memorial Day weekend, a catastrophic failure occurred in a 15,000-gallon polymer reactor vessel in the K-1 building at the Shell Chemical plant in Belpre, Ohio, along the Ohio River. OSHA's investigation determined the failure was initiated by a runaway chemical reaction in the plant's Kraton-D polymer production unit. The reactor failure caused an explosion and fire that completely destroyed the K-1 unit. The fire then spread to a nearby chemical storage tank farm; at approximately 6:30 a.m., a second, violent explosion occurred as the fire reached one of the storage tanks. Five more tanks ignited in the following minutes, holding millions of gallons of styrene and roughly 400,000 gallons of diesel fuel, and continued burning for nine hours before firefighters, ultimately numbering roughly 150 responders from dozens of surrounding fire companies, brought the blaze under control. Three plant technicians, Mike Harris, Gary Reed, and George Nutter, were killed. Approximately 1,700 local residents were temporarily evacuated as smoke spread across both sides of the Ohio River, and the fire and firefighting efforts released a mixture of toxic chemicals into the river that were detected more than 20 miles downstream. Shell sold the plant a few years later; it operates today as Kraton Polymers.

Root Causes

  • A runaway chemical reaction developed in the polymer reactor: OSHA's investigation specifically identified this as the initiating event, a reaction that generated conditions beyond what the 15,000-gallon vessel could safely contain.
  • The reactor failure caused a fire that spread beyond the initial unit: rather than being contained to the K-1 building where it started, the fire reached a nearby storage tank farm holding large quantities of flammable material.
  • Six storage tanks ignited in sequence: once the fire reached the tank farm, styrene and diesel fuel storage tanks caught fire one after another, dramatically escalating the scale and duration of the incident well beyond the initial reactor failure.
  • The incident occurred at the start of a holiday weekend: while not established as a direct causal factor, the timing meant the incident developed and required emergency response during a period when staffing patterns and community awareness may have differed from a typical workday.

Lessons Learned

  1. Polymer and other reactive chemical processes need runaway reaction prevention and mitigation systems verified against the reactor's actual worst-case behavior, not just its normal operating envelope. A 15,000-gallon vessel failing catastrophically indicates its safeguards weren't adequate for the reaction that actually occurred.
  2. Storage tank farms sited near reactive process units need fire barriers or sufficient separation distance to prevent an initial process fire from escalating into a much larger tank farm fire. The spread from the K-1 unit to six separate storage tanks turned a serious but contained incident into a nine-hour, multi-agency emergency.
  3. Large flammable liquid inventories near waterways need spill and firefighting runoff containment planning, since this incident's toxic chemical contamination of the Ohio River traveled more than 20 miles downstream, extending the incident's impact well beyond the immediate fire.
  4. Emergency response capacity needs to account for realistic worst-case scenarios involving multiple simultaneous tank fires, not just a single unit incident, given how quickly this event scaled from one reactor failure to six burning storage tanks.
