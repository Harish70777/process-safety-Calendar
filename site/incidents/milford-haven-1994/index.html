Anniversary: July 24, 1994

Texaco Milford Haven Refinery Explosion and Fires

Milford Haven, Wales, UK · 0 fatalities, 26 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityManagement Of ChangeProcess Hazard Analysis

What Happened

At the Pembroke Cracking Company plant within the Texaco refinery, a control valve was actually closed while the control system's indication showed it as open — a mismatch between the physical state of the equipment and what operators could see on their instrumentation. As a result, flammable hydrocarbon liquid continued to be pumped into a process vessel whose outlet was, unknown to operators, shut. The flare system, meant to safely relieve exactly this kind of upset, was not designed to handle an excursion of this scale. The outlet pipe ultimately failed, releasing roughly 20 tonnes of hydrocarbon liquid and vapor, which ignited and exploded, causing a major fire at the flare drum outlet and several secondary fires. The incident happened on a Sunday afternoon with a relatively low number of staff on site, and those who were near the explosion happened to be in advantageous locations — a stroke of good fortune investigators noted was a significant factor in there being no fatalities. 26 people were injured, and the refinery suffered severe damage to process plant, buildings, and storage tanks; some off-site glass damage occurred in the town of Milford Haven, roughly 3 km away.

Root Causes

  • Valve position indication did not match reality: the control system showed the outlet valve as open when it was actually closed, meaning operators had no accurate picture of the vessel's true state as liquid continued to be pumped in.
  • Flare system undersized for this failure mode: the flare system that should have safely relieved the developing overpressure was not designed to cope with an excursion of this specific type and scale, leaving no effective safety net once the mismatch went uncorrected.
  • Inadequate maintenance of plant and instrumentation: the underlying condition that allowed a valve's actual position to diverge from its indicated position pointed to gaps in how instrumentation was maintained and verified.
  • Plant modification without consequence assessment: HSE found that a modification to the plant had been carried out without properly assessing its potential consequences — contributing to the conditions that allowed this failure mode to develop undetected.

Lessons Learned

  1. Never fully trust a single instrumentation reading for a safety-critical valve position without some form of independent verification — a mismatch between indicated and actual state is exactly the kind of failure that can go undetected until it's too late to matter.
  2. Flare and relief systems need to be sized against realistic upset scenarios specific to your process, not just routine operating excursions — "the flare system wasn't designed for this" is a design gap to close before an incident reveals it, not an acceptable explanation afterward.
  3. Any plant modification — however incremental it seems — needs a documented assessment of its potential consequences before implementation, precisely because unassessed changes are how new failure modes quietly enter a process.
  4. This incident is a stark reminder that "no fatalities" doesn't mean "no near-catastrophe" — investigators explicitly credited good fortune (low weekend staffing, workers who happened to be positioned favorably) for the absence of deaths, not a controls-based safeguard. Don't let a lucky outcome substitute for fixing the underlying gap.
