Anniversary: October 29, 2009

Jaipur Oil Terminal Fire

Sitapura, Jaipur, Rajasthan, India · 12 fatalities, 200 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityOperating ProceduresEmergency Planning And Response

What Happened

During a routine after-hours transfer of motor spirit (gasoline) from a storage tank to a neighboring terminal, operators preparing the tank opened valves in the wrong sequence, causing a massive leak from a "hammer blind valve" at the tank's base. Liquid fuel jetted upward under hydrostatic pressure and rapidly generated a huge, largely invisible vapor cloud, detectable mainly by smell, that grew to roughly 1,000 meters in diameter, about four times the size of the vapor cloud at Buncefield four years earlier. The site had no remotely operated shutdown valve, so stopping the leak required someone to physically reach the valve, but personnel attempting to do so were overcome by vapors before they could act, and with an understaffed night shift, no one else was available to intervene. The leak continued uncontrolled for 75 minutes before the cloud found an ignition source and exploded, destroying the entire terminal and its roughly 60 million liters of stored fuel; the resulting fire burned for 11 days.

Note on casualty figures: reported death tolls for this incident vary across sources, from initial day-of reports of 6 to later tallies of 11–12 as some of the critically injured succumbed over the following days and weeks. This entry uses 12, reflecting the higher end of commonly cited later totals, but readers should be aware the exact figure is not perfectly consistent across sources.

Root Causes

  • No remotely operated shutdown valve: the only way to stop the leak was manual intervention at the tank itself, which put responders directly in the path of the vapor cloud they were trying to stop.
  • No site-specific written operating procedures: the transfer operation lacked documented step-by-step procedures, contributing to valves being opened in an unsafe sequence.
  • Inadequate understanding of hazards, risks, and consequences: personnel and management did not fully appreciate how large and how invisible a vapor cloud from this scale of release could become, delaying an appropriately urgent response.
  • Understaffed night shift with no backup: critical, high-risk transfer operations were scheduled after hours with minimal crew, so once initial responders were incapacitated there was no one left on site to escalate or intervene.

Lessons Learned

  1. Any tank transfer operation involving large flammable inventories needs remote, fail-safe shutdown capability, relying on someone physically reaching a valve inside a growing vapor cloud is not a viable emergency response.
  2. Written, site-specific operating procedures for routine but hazardous tasks (like transfers) are not bureaucratic overhead, this incident happened specifically because the correct valve sequence wasn't documented and followed.
  3. A vapor cloud doesn't need to be visible to be lethal or explosive, train personnel to treat any reported fuel odor at this kind of facility as a potentially large, invisible hazard requiring immediate evacuation-distance response, not a "let's go check it out" response.
  4. Scheduling high-consequence operations for times with minimal staffing removes your ability to respond if something goes wrong, staffing levels for hazardous tasks should be sized for the worst case, not the routine case.
