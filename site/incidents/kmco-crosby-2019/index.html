Anniversary: April 2, 2019

KMCO Chemical Facility Fatal Explosion and Fire

Crosby, TX, USA · 1 fatalities, 28 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityTrainingEmergency Planning And Response

What Happened

During production of a sulfurized isobutylene lubrication additive, a cast iron "y-strainer", a filtration component on the suction piping feeding the isobutylene charge pump, ruptured due to brittle overload fracture, opening a hole roughly 15 centimeters across at its widest point. Isobutylene, a highly flammable gas stored and handled as a liquid, escaped and formed a large vapor cloud. A field operator, a trainee with about six months at the facility, and a contractor heard the pipe fail and saw vapor pouring out; the contractor evacuated, but the operator called for help assessing the situation rather than immediately retreating to safety. Despite efforts by operators and supervisors, including shutting down equipment, the vapor cloud found an ignition source (most likely electrical equipment) and ignited, causing an explosion that killed one KMCO employee and seriously injured two others; at least 28 people in total were injured, including both KMCO employees and contract workers. A shelter-in-place order was issued for the surrounding community within a one-mile radius. KMCO filed for bankruptcy following the incident and is no longer in business.

Root Causes

  • Brittle cast iron used in a hazardous, high-pressure application: the failed y-strainer was made from gray cast iron, a material that existing industry standards and good practice guidance either prohibit or explicitly warn against using in this kind of hazardous, pressurized service, CSB found KMCO's hazard evaluations had consistently overlooked or misunderstood this material risk.
  • No pressure relief protection at the failure point: the strainer was installed in a section of piping without a pressure-relief device or protection against potential high-pressure conditions, removing a safeguard that could have prevented the brittle material from reaching failure conditions.
  • Inadequate emergency response training and role definition: CSB found KMCO's emergency response procedures did not properly limit the role of operations staff during an emergency, the plant's culture relied on unit operators to take action to stop releases themselves, which led at least one worker to move toward the flammable vapor cloud rather than away from it.
  • No remote isolation capability: CSB explicitly found KMCO had failed to heed industry guidance calling for remote isolation equipment, which would have let employees safely stop the isobutylene release without having to approach it directly.

Lessons Learned

  1. Material selection for any component in hazardous, pressurized service needs to be verified against current industry standards during hazard evaluation, a brittle material like cast iron in the wrong application is exactly the kind of latent defect a proper hazard evaluation is meant to catch before it becomes a failure.
  2. Every component handling a hazardous process fluid under pressure needs an evaluated relief path, a strainer or filter is not exempt from overpressure protection just because it's a "minor" piping component.
  3. Emergency response training must clearly define when operators should retreat versus intervene, a plant culture that implicitly or explicitly expects operations staff to personally address a developing release, rather than falling back and letting trained emergency responders handle it, puts workers directly in harm's way.
  4. Remote isolation capability for hazardous releases isn't a luxury upgrade, it's the difference between a worker being able to stop a leak from a safe distance versus having to physically approach an active, worsening hazard, exactly the choice a trainee operator faced here.
