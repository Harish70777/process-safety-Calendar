Anniversary: October 2, 2007

Xcel Energy Cabin Creek Hydroelectric Plant Penstock Fire

Georgetown, CO, USA · 5 fatalities, 3 injuries
ContractorsEmergency PlanningTrainingProcess Hazard Analysis

Official CSB Safety Video, via the U.S. Chemical Safety Board's YouTube channel.

What Happened

A crew of industrial painters employed by RPI Coating began applying a new epoxy coating to the steel interior of a 1,530-foot section of the 4,300-foot penstock, an enclosed water tunnel, at Xcel Energy's Cabin Creek hydroelectric plant, located over 1,000 feet underground in the Colorado mountains. Shortly after starting, the crew experienced problems with the spraying equipment and stopped to clean it using methyl ethyl ketone (MEK), a flammable solvent, working in the open penstock atmosphere. MEK vapors ignited, likely from a static spark, triggering a flash fire that quickly grew as it ignited additional buckets of solvent and combustible epoxy material stored nearby. The tunnel had only a single point of egress, a vulnerability both Xcel and RPI personnel had identified during planning, but neither company took action to address it. The fire trapped five of the eleven workers inside the penstock; they were unable to reach the tunnel's one exit. The trapped workers communicated by radio with colleagues and rescue crews for 45 minutes, but Xcel had no technically qualified confined-space rescue responders on duty, and local volunteer firefighters' rescue attempts were unsuccessful. All five trapped workers died from carbon monoxide inhalation, without burn injuries, indicating they succumbed to smoke and fumes rather than the fire itself. Three other workers were injured. Xcel had selected RPI Coating for the job based on the lowest bid, despite RPI holding the lowest possible safety rating, zero, among the competing contractors.

Root Causes

  • Flammable solvent was used to clean equipment inside a confined space: cleaning spray equipment with MEK in the open penstock atmosphere, rather than in a ventilated area outside the confined space or under stricter atmospheric controls, created the flammable vapor concentration that ultimately ignited.
  • A known single point of egress was never addressed: both Xcel and RPI had identified the penstock's single exit as a major safety concern during planning, but neither company took remedial action before work began.
  • The contractor was selected despite a documented history of poor safety performance: RPI Coating had the lowest possible safety rating among bidding contractors, yet was selected based on providing the lowest bid, a decision CSB found reflected inadequate contractor selection and oversight by Xcel.
  • No technically qualified confined-space rescue responders were available: when the fire trapped workers underground, no one on site or in the responding local emergency services had the specific training needed to attempt a qualified confined-space rescue, contributing to the unsuccessful rescue effort.
  • Regulatory standards for flammable atmospheres in confined spaces were insufficient: CSB found existing rules did not adequately prohibit entry or continued occupancy in a permit-required confined space once flammable vapor concentrations exceeded a dangerous threshold.

Lessons Learned

  1. Any task requiring flammable solvents inside a confined space needs its hazards evaluated and controlled before work begins, including whether the work can be done outside the confined space entirely, or under strict, monitored atmospheric limits if it cannot.
  2. A single point of egress identified during safety planning is not a finding to note and move past. If a hazard is significant enough to be raised by personnel from multiple companies during planning, it needs remedial action before the work proceeds, not acknowledgment alone.
  3. Contractor selection needs to weigh safety performance as a real, disqualifying factor, not just cost. Choosing the lowest bidder despite a documented zero safety rating placed workers with a company whose track record had already signaled elevated risk.
  4. Facilities with confined-space work need genuinely qualified rescue capability available before that work begins, not an assumption that local emergency responders will be equipped to handle a specialized rescue. The unsuccessful rescue attempt here reflects a real gap between what was needed and what was actually on hand.
  5. Regulatory thresholds for flammable atmospheres in confined spaces need to be conservative enough to actually prevent this failure mode. CSB's finding that existing standards didn't adequately prohibit continued occupancy at dangerous vapor concentrations points to a gap worth closing at the regulatory level, not just the individual facility level.
