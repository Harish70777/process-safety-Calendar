Anniversary: May 1, 1991

Angus Chemical Sterlington Nitroparaffin Plant Explosion

Sterlington, LA, USA · 8 fatalities, 120 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityIncident InvestigationEmergency Planning

What Happened

On the afternoon of May 1, 1991, workers preparing to check a compressor in the nitroparaffin unit at the Angus Chemical plant in Sterlington, Louisiana, noticed a small fire and sounded the plant's fire alarm. Approximately 30 seconds later, an explosion occurred, followed by a series of smaller secondary explosions. The blast completely destroyed an area of the plant roughly the size of a city block, and its effects were felt as far as eight miles away; shrapnel was thrown across the surrounding area, with pieces weighing up to 150 pounds found nearly a mile from the plant. The explosion shattered windows and tore lighting fixtures from the ceiling of Sterlington Memorial Hospital, located directly across the highway from the plant. Eight people were killed, all employees of IMC Fertilizer, the company that operated the nitroparaffin unit under contract to owner Angus Chemical, including a company vice president who also served as plant manager. Approximately 120 people received medical treatment for injuries, both inside the plant and in the surrounding community, with several in critical condition. Roughly 1,500 homes in the town of Sterlington were evacuated as a precaution against possible asbestos exposure from insulation materials at the site, with residents allowed to return the following evening once air quality was confirmed safe. Federal investigators subsequently fined IMC Fertilizer $10 million for insufficient safety protocols. The plant had operated since 1956 without a single prior fatality before this incident.

Root Causes

  • A fire developed in the nitroparaffin unit shortly before the explosion: workers preparing to inspect a compressor noticed the fire and had just enough time to sound an alarm before the explosion occurred roughly 30 seconds later.
  • Federal investigation found insufficient safety protocols: the $10 million fine levied against IMC Fertilizer reflected a determination that the safety programs in place at the facility were inadequate to prevent this kind of catastrophic failure.
  • Cascading secondary explosions compounded the initial event: the primary explosion triggered a series of additional smaller explosions, extending the damage and hazard well beyond the initial failure point.
  • A long safety history without incident may have contributed to reduced vigilance: the plant's 35-year run without a fatality, while a genuine achievement, is also a pattern seen elsewhere in this database where an extended period of apparent safety can coincide with underlying hazards going unaddressed.

Lessons Learned

  1. A small fire noticed shortly before a catastrophic failure, as happened here with only about 30 seconds of warning, underscores how little time may be available once a hazardous condition becomes visible. Prevention has to happen well before that point, not rely on response time once a fire is spotted.
  2. A federal finding of "insufficient safety protocols" after a catastrophic incident is a serious, specific conclusion, not a generic characterization. It indicates identifiable gaps existed in the facility's actual safety programs, gaps that a proactive audit could plausibly have caught beforehand.
  3. Facility siting near sensitive infrastructure, in this case a hospital directly across the highway, means the consequences of an industrial explosion can directly compromise nearby emergency medical capacity exactly when it's needed most.
  4. A long track record without a fatal incident is not the same as a demonstrably safe process. This plant's 35 years without a prior death didn't prevent this explosion, a pattern worth remembering whenever incident-free duration is treated as reassurance rather than one data point among many.
