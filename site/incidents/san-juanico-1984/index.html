Anniversary: November 19, 1984

San Juanico PEMEX LPG Terminal Disaster

San Juan Ixhuatepec, Mexico · 500 fatalities, 7000 injuries
Facility SitingEmergency PlanningMechanical Integrity

What Happened

At approximately 5:35 a.m. on November 19, 1984, a major fire and a series of catastrophic explosions occurred at the government-owned PEMEX liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) storage and distribution terminal in San Juan Ixhuatepec, roughly 20 kilometers north of Mexico City. The facility, refilled from a refinery 400 kilometers away after nearly emptying the day before, held six spherical tanks and 48 horizontal cylindrical vessels, most filled to 90 percent capacity with approximately 11,000 cubic meters of propane and butane. An 8-inch pipe between a sphere and a series of cylinders ruptured; a pressure drop was noticed in the control room, but operators could not identify its cause, and the release continued uncontrolled for 5 to 10 minutes. A gas cloud roughly 200 by 150 meters and 2 meters high drifted toward a ground-level flare pit and ignited, causing violent ground shock and a number of ground fires. Approximately 15 minutes after the initial release, the first boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion (BLEVE) occurred; over the next hour and a half, a series of additional BLEVEs followed as LPG vessels violently ruptured one after another, hurling debris and burning liquid across the surrounding area and igniting everything it touched. The facility's fire water and water spray systems were disabled in the initial blast, removing a key firefighting resource exactly when it was needed most. Approximately 500 to 600 people were killed, and 5,000 to 7,000 suffered severe burns; roughly 200,000 people were evacuated, and the terminal along with much of the surrounding neighborhood, home to an estimated 40,000 residents living in simple one-story houses, was destroyed. This remains one of the deadliest industrial disasters in world history.

Root Causes

  • A pipe ruptured between storage vessels: the initiating event was the failure of an 8-inch pipe connecting a sphere to a series of cylindrical tanks, releasing pressurized LPG into the atmosphere.
  • Operators could not identify the cause of the pressure drop: although a pressure drop was noticed both in the control room and at a pipeline pumping station, personnel were unable to determine what was happening quickly enough to take effective action before the gas cloud ignited.
  • Gas detection and emergency isolation were ineffective: the facility lacked an effective means of detecting the developing gas release and isolating it before ignition, a gap subsequent technical analysis identified as the single greatest contributor to the disaster's escalation.
  • The facility was sited directly adjacent to dense residential housing: the extraordinarily high death toll resulted specifically from the proximity of a major LPG storage facility to a densely populated neighborhood, meaning the consequence of the failure fell overwhelmingly on residents rather than facility personnel.
  • The fire water system was disabled in the initial blast: the facility's own firefighting infrastructure was taken out of service by the first explosion, removing a critical layer of protection during the hour and a half of escalating BLEVEs that followed.

Lessons Learned

  1. Large-inventory hazardous material facilities need effective, redundant gas detection and automatic emergency isolation capable of stopping a release within seconds to minutes, not systems that depend on operators correctly diagnosing an ambiguous pressure drop under time pressure.
  2. Facility siting is not a secondary consideration for major hazard installations; it is often the single factor most responsible for whether a technical failure remains a contained industrial incident or becomes a mass-casualty community disaster. This remains one of the starkest illustrations in the historical record of that principle.
  3. Critical safety systems like fire water and water spray need engineering resilient enough to survive the facility's own worst-case initial event, since a system disabled by the first explosion provides no protection during the escalating sequence that follows.
  4. This disaster is frequently cited alongside Bhopal, which occurred just two weeks later, as the pair of catastrophes that most directly drove the development of modern process safety management and major hazard regulation worldwide, underscoring how directly today's safety standards trace back to failures of exactly this kind.
