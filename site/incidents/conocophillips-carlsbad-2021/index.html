ConocoPhillips Carlsbad Vacuum Truck Flash Fire

Carlsbad, NM, USA · 0 fatalities, 1 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisOperating ProceduresTraining

What Happened

On October 6, 2021, an accidental release of crude oil and produced water occurred during vacuum truck loading operations at ConocoPhillips' Craig State 36B facility south of Carlsbad, New Mexico. An emulsion layer had periodically developed at the oil and water interface within the facility's heater treater, impairing its efficiency; to address this, ConocoPhillips periodically removed the layer by transferring fluid into a vacuum truck. A contractor connected a three-inch hose between the truck and the heater treater and opened valves on each end, allowing pressurized fluid to flow from the heater treater into the truck without the truck's own vacuum pump running. When the driver detected crude oil reaching the hose, he closed the process valve on the heater treater side, then disconnected the hose from the heater treater while it still contained pressurized fluid. The contents flowed out of the truck and into the atmosphere through the open hose, and the released flammable hydrocarbon vapor ignited, most likely from a component of the heater treater's own fired burner, which had inadvertently been left online during the operation. The resulting flash fire seriously injured the vacuum truck driver. ConocoPhillips' investigation also could not rule out static electricity as a contributing ignition source, since the hose components were non-conductive and the truck was neither electrically bonded nor grounded, and found no predetermined location existed to properly ground or bond a vacuum truck for this task.

Root Causes

  • The hose was disconnected while still pressurized with hydrocarbon fluid: rather than fully depressurizing the line and truck before disconnection, the driver disconnected the hose with fluid and vapor still inside, allowing it to discharge into the atmosphere.
  • No hazard or safety analysis had been performed for this task: ConocoPhillips had no formal process to identify or control the hazards of draining an emulsion layer from its heater treaters, meaning this specific failure mode was never evaluated in advance.
  • The vacuum truck was not electrically grounded or bonded: with no predetermined grounding location and non-conductive hose components, static electricity buildup couldn't be ruled out as a contributing ignition source.
  • The heater treater's fired burner was left online during the operation: an active ignition source remained energized nearby while flammable material was being actively transferred and could potentially escape into the surrounding air.

Lessons Learned

  1. Any hose or line transfer of flammable liquid needs full depressurization and drainage verification before disconnection, not disconnection based on visual confirmation that oil has appeared in the line.
  2. Recurring maintenance tasks, like periodically draining an emulsion layer, need a documented hazard analysis specifically covering that task, even when it's been performed informally many times without incident before.
  3. Vacuum trucks and similar mobile equipment used for hazardous liquid transfer need a designated, verified electrical grounding and bonding point, and this equipment should be used consistently, not treated as optional when convenient.
  4. Fired equipment near an active hydrocarbon transfer operation should be isolated or shut down for the duration of the transfer wherever operationally feasible, removing a readily available ignition source rather than relying on containment alone to prevent ignition.
