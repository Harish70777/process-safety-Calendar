Anniversary: April 21, 1995

Napp Technologies Water-Reactive Chemical Explosion

Lodi, NJ, USA · 5 fatalities, 35 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisEmergency PlanningOperating Procedures

What Happened

Napp Technologies performed toll blending operations for other companies at its facility in Lodi, New Jersey, mixing a commercial gold-precipitating agent that included water-reactive aluminum powder and sodium hydrosulfite. The blending step was expected to take less than an hour but instead continued for nearly 24 hours, during which operators noticed an unexpected reaction developing inside the blender, producing increasing heat and a foul-smelling gas. At some point during this extended, abnormal blending process, water was introduced into the mixture, likely while workers attempted to clear a pipe blockage that had prevented the process from moving to its next step. The water-reactive combination began an accelerating chemical reaction. Workers realized the danger and began an emergency procedure to empty the blender's contents in hopes of halting the reaction, but the mixture exploded during that attempt. The blast and resulting fire devastated a large portion of the 50,000-square-foot production facility and roughly 40 percent of an adjoining warehouse; one of two 20-ton concrete blocks supporting the mixing vessel was thrown 48 feet and punched through a 36-inch-thick concrete and brick wall. Flames reached 100 feet high, and the resulting smoke plume was reported at 1,000 feet high and 10 miles long. Four employees were killed immediately; a fifth died days later from injuries sustained in the blast. Approximately 300 residents were evacuated, and firefighting runoff carrying toxic chemicals flowed into the nearby Saddle River, killing fish for miles downstream. The Lodi Fire Department was not notified of a developing problem at the plant until shortly before the explosion, despite company officials having known about the dangerous reaction for roughly 12 hours beforehand, and had no information on what chemicals were present in the building where the explosion occurred.

Root Causes

  • Water was introduced into a water-reactive chemical mixture: the combination of aluminum powder and sodium hydrosulfite reacts dangerously with water, and water entered the process, likely during an attempt to clear a pipe blockage, triggering the reaction that led to the explosion.
  • A blending operation expected to take under an hour ran for nearly 24 hours: this extended, abnormal duration meant the process was operating well outside its normal parameters for most of a full day without triggering a stop-and-reassess response.
  • The fire department was not notified for roughly 12 hours after the problem was known: company officials were aware of the developing dangerous reaction well before the explosion but did not alert local emergency responders until shortly before the blast occurred.
  • Emergency responders had no information about the chemicals present: when the Lodi Fire Department did respond, they had no advance knowledge of what hazardous materials were involved in the fire they were fighting, a serious gap given the facility's status as one of the community's highest-hazard occupancies.

Lessons Learned

  1. Water-reactive chemicals demand rigorously controlled processes with verified isolation from any water source, including water used incidentally for other purposes like clearing equipment. A momentary lapse in that isolation was enough to trigger a fatal reaction here.
  2. A process running dramatically longer than its expected duration, nearly 24 hours instead of under one, is a clear signal that something has gone wrong and needs a genuine stop-and-investigate response, not continued effort to push the process to completion.
  3. Local fire departments and emergency responders need to be notified the moment a facility recognizes a developing hazardous situation, not only once an explosion has become imminent. A 12-hour gap between company awareness and notification removed the possibility of an earlier, safer intervention.
  4. Communities with high-hazard chemical facilities need those facilities to maintain accessible, current information on the chemicals present, so responders arriving at an emergency know what they're actually dealing with rather than discovering it during the response itself.
