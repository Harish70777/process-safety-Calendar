Anniversary: October 3, 1978

Continental Oil Commerce City Polymerization Unit Explosion

Commerce City, CO, USA · 3 fatalities, 9 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityProcess Hazard AnalysisManagement Of Change

What Happened

At 6:33 a.m. on October 3, 1978, near the end of the night shift, an explosion occurred at the Continental Oil Company (Conoco) refinery in Commerce City, Colorado, on the site now operated as Suncor's Commerce City refinery. Gas fumes leaking from newly installed equipment in a polymerization unit that had been in operation for only two weeks ignited in a fireball, sending flames roughly 60 feet above the refinery's stacks and shattering windows throughout the industrial suburb north of Denver. The explosion registered 3.5 on the Richter scale at a Denver seismological observatory. Two workers, Steve French, 24, and David Hobbs, 32, died beneath debris at the scene; a third, Ron DeHerrera, was fatally injured and died shortly after. Nine other workers were injured, with several remaining hospitalized in serious condition in the following days. Hundreds of firefighters from across the Denver area responded and brought the fire under control within roughly three hours. The refinery's plant manager said the new polymerization unit was approximately 25 percent destroyed, with damage estimated at up to $5 million. A police investigation into an anonymous report claiming the explosion was caused by a bomb found no evidence of a device, and authorities discounted that report.

Root Causes

  • The explosion occurred in a unit that had only been operating for two weeks: the polymerization unit involved was newly installed and had not yet accumulated significant operating history, meaning any latent design, construction, or commissioning issues had limited opportunity to surface before this incident.
  • Gas fumes leaked from newly installed equipment: the direct trigger was a leak specifically from equipment that had recently been installed as part of the new unit, rather than aging equipment with a long service history.
  • New process units carry elevated risk during their initial operating period: equipment that hasn't yet been proven out under real operating conditions can have undetected weaknesses that only manifest once it's actually running, a pattern distinct from failures driven by long-term wear or corrosion.

Lessons Learned

  1. Newly commissioned process units need heightened monitoring and inspection during their initial weeks of operation, precisely because installation defects or design issues haven't yet had the chance to reveal themselves through normal operating experience.
  2. Startup and early operation of new equipment deserve the same rigor as a pre-startup safety review would apply to a full facility, verifying that all connections, welds, and installations meet their intended specification before the unit is trusted to run unsupervised.
  3. Community impact from a refinery explosion, shattered windows and seismic-scale shaking felt throughout a metropolitan area in this case, illustrates how facility siting near residential and commercial areas magnifies even a contained industrial equipment failure into a broader community event.
  4. This incident is one of several in this database's historical record occurring at a single refinery site across decades and different ownership, a pattern worth remembering when evaluating whether a facility's location and design carry persistent underlying risk factors regardless of which company currently operates it.
