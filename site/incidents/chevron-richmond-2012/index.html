Chevron Richmond Refinery Pipe Rupture and Fire

Richmond, CA, USA · 0 fatalities, 6 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityProcess Hazard AnalysisIncident Investigation

What Happened

A carbon steel piping component in the refinery's #4 Crude Unit, installed in 1976, ruptured after decades of sulfidation corrosion (a corrosion mechanism driven by sulfur compounds in crude oil) had thinned the pipe wall well beyond safe limits. The failure released flammable hydrocarbon process fluid, which partially vaporized into a large vapor cloud that engulfed 19 Chevron employees before igniting. All employees escaped without serious injury, but the resulting fire produced a large plume that traveled across the Richmond area, and roughly 15,000 nearby residents sought medical treatment. Investigators found the pipe's silicon content, a trace element known to inhibit sulfidation corrosion, was far below the threshold needed for adequate protection, and metallurgical testing later found a nearly identical corrosion pattern in comparable piping at Chevron's El Segundo refinery, which processed similar crude oil.

Root Causes

  • Sulfidation corrosion of low-silicon carbon steel piping: the specific pipe section had unusually low silicon content, which let corrosion progress far faster than typical for carbon steel piping of that vintage and service.
  • A known internal recommendation from 2002 was not acted on: Chevron's own metallurgists and pipe inspectors had recommended replacing this pipe roughly a decade before it ruptured, the company did not act on its own internal warning.
  • Incomplete process hazard analysis: the facility's PHA of the crude unit did not identify sulfidation corrosion as a hazard mechanism, despite it being a well-documented risk for refinery piping carrying sulfur-containing crude.
  • Corrosion monitoring did not catch the accelerated thinning: existing inspection points and monitoring practices failed to detect the localized, low-silicon-driven corrosion before it progressed to failure.

Lessons Learned

  1. An internal engineering recommendation to replace known-risk equipment is not optional guidance, if your own inspectors flag a component for replacement, that finding needs a tracked, closed-loop corrective action, not just a file entry.
  2. Process hazard analyses need to explicitly evaluate all plausible corrosion and degradation mechanisms for the specific materials and process conditions involved, a generic PHA that doesn't account for chemistry-specific risks like sulfidation corrosion has a real gap.
  3. Silicon content variability in carbon steel piping is a known, quantifiable risk factor, verify it directly for piping in sulfur-bearing service rather than assuming standard carbon steel provides adequate corrosion resistance.
  4. When a corrosion mechanism is found at one facility, check for the same design and material conditions at sister facilities immediately, Chevron's own post-incident inspection of near-identical piping at El Segundo (finding up to 60% wall loss) shows how directly transferable this kind of finding can be across a company's own asset base.
