Anniversary: April 2, 2010

Tesoro Anacortes Refinery Heat Exchanger Explosion

Anacortes, WA, USA · 7 fatalities, 1 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityProcess Hazard AnalysisIncident Investigation

Official CSB Safety Video, via the U.S. Chemical Safety Board's YouTube channel.

What Happened

During restart of a heat exchanger bank in the refinery's naphtha hydrotreater unit following maintenance, one of the exchangers catastrophically ruptured at its weld seams, releasing a large volume of superheated hydrogen and naphtha that ignited instantly. The explosion and resulting fire, burning for more than three hours, killed seven employees (one shift supervisor and six operators) who were working in the immediate vicinity. Investigators found the direct cause was high temperature hydrogen attack (HTHA), a degradation mechanism where hydrogen at elevated temperature and pressure gradually cracks and weakens carbon steel from the inside, invisibly, over years, the affected equipment was nearly 40 years old and had been suffering slow, undetected damage the entire time. It was the deadliest incident at a U.S. refinery since 2005 and the state of Washington's worst industrial disaster in 50 years.

Root Causes

  • High temperature hydrogen attack (HTHA) went undetected for years: the specific carbon steel equipment was vulnerable to this degradation mechanism under its actual operating conditions, but the damage accumulated invisibly until the exchanger failed catastrophically.
  • Weak industry inspection standard: CSB found the American Petroleum Institute's standard for assessing equipment vulnerability to HTHA was inadequate, the tool the industry relied on to judge "is this equipment safe" wasn't reliably answering that question.
  • Repeated process hazard analyses failed to control the hazard: PHAs at the refinery, including some conducted under the previous owner, cited only judgment-based safeguards without verifying those safeguards actually worked, and didn't ensure the number of workers exposed to the hazard was minimized.
  • A complacent safety culture: CSB found the refinery had a pattern of leaks and occasional fires treated as routine over the years, reflecting what the report called a culture that required "proof of danger rather than proof of effective safety implementation."

Lessons Learned

  1. Some equipment degradation mechanisms (like HTHA) are invisible to routine visual inspection, verify your mechanical integrity program actually detects the specific degradation modes relevant to your process conditions, not just that inspections are happening on schedule.
  2. A PHA safeguard that's never been verified to actually work is not a safeguard, it's an assumption. Judgment-based safeguards need real data behind them, not inherited practice.
  3. Industry consensus standards are a floor, not a ceiling, if your specific equipment and operating history suggest elevated risk, don't wait for the standard to catch up before acting.
  4. A pattern of "minor" leaks and small fires treated as routine operational noise is often the clearest available warning sign of a deeper mechanical integrity problem, this incident is a textbook case of that pattern being normalized until it wasn't survivable.
