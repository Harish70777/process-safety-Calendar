Valero Meraux Refinery Hydrocracker Explosion and Fire

Meraux, LA, USA · 0 fatalities, 1 injuries
Management Of ChangeOperating ProceduresMechanical Integrity

What Happened

A pressure safety valve (PSV) on a hydrocracker unit vessel opened to relieve elevated pressure to the flare system, but after doing its job, the valve failed to fully "reseat" back into its closed position. Valero developed a plan to fix this — following the refinery's management of change process for isolating a safety device, with management approval documented — that called for briefly closing one of two 6-inch inlet valves to allow the PSV to reseat properly. An operations supervisor and two field operators were sent to carry out the plan while a board operator monitored system pressure from the control room. Once in the field, the operators raised safety concerns about actually accessing either of the two inlet valves as planned, and made a decision on the spot to close a different valve instead — a 20-inch outlet valve positioned between the PSV and the flare system. That substituted valve immediately failed when closed, triggering an explosion and fire. Flammable gas formed a vapor cloud that ignited; the release also included as much as 5,500 pounds of sulfur dioxide. One Valero operator was seriously injured, and the incident caused approximately $5.15 million in property damage.

Root Causes

  • A pressure safety valve failed to fully reseat after activating: the initial relief event left the PSV not properly closed, creating the underlying condition the field team was sent to correct.
  • The approved plan was changed in the field without going back through the change process: the original, management-approved plan specified closing an inlet valve; operators substituted a different valve — the outlet valve — based on an access concern raised on site, without that substitution going through the same review the original plan had.
  • The substituted valve was not equivalent to the one it replaced: closing the outlet valve, positioned differently in the system relative to the PSV and flare path, did not achieve the same safe isolation the original inlet-valve plan was designed to provide, and failed immediately under the load.
  • No verification step caught the field deviation before it was executed: the board operator was monitoring pressure remotely, but nothing in the process required confirming that the actual valve being closed in the field matched the one specified in the approved plan.

Lessons Learned

  1. A field-level change to an approved plan — even one made for good safety reasons, like avoiding a hard-to-access valve — is still a deviation from a management-of-change-approved procedure, and needs to go back through some form of review, even an expedited one, rather than being decided informally on site.
  2. Substituting one valve for another in a safety-critical isolation plan is not a like-for-like swap unless someone has verified the substitute achieves the same isolation function — position in the system, relief path, and load rating can all differ in ways that matter.
  3. Remote monitoring (a board operator watching pressure) doesn't substitute for verifying that field actions match the approved plan — a communication check confirming exactly which valve was being closed, before it was closed, could have caught this deviation before it caused a failure.
  4. This incident is a useful complement to Formosa Illiopolis and Williams Olefins elsewhere in this database: all three involve a valve-related deviation from what was actually planned or expected, reinforcing how often "the specific valve involved" turns out to be the detail that made the difference between a routine fix and a serious incident.
