Philadelphia Energy Solutions Refinery Fire and HF Release

Philadelphia, PA, USA · 0 fatalities, 5 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityProcess Safety Information

What Happened

A steel pipe elbow installed in 1973 in the refinery's hydrofluoric acid (HF) alkylation unit ruptured after decades of corrosion had eaten the pipe wall down to roughly 7% of its original thickness, far below any threshold that should have triggered replacement. The rupture released a mixture of liquefied hydrocarbon gas (mostly propane) carrying about 2.5% hydrofluoric acid, forming a ground-hugging vapor cloud that ignited within about two minutes, causing a major fire followed by a series of explosions. One explosion hurled a 38,000-pound vessel fragment across the Schuylkill River. In total, roughly 2,270 kg of HF and over 300,000 kg of hydrocarbons were released, with about 276,000 kg burning in the fire; several thousand more pounds of HF were captured in the refinery's water spray mitigation system rather than escaping further. Five people required medical attention; the refinery, sitting less than a mile from roughly 117,000 residents, never reopened and permanently closed.

Root Causes

  • Severely corroded pipe elbow: the failed pipe was made from a steel alloy with nickel and copper content that was acceptable by 1973 standards but has not met international industry standards since they were updated in 1995, the company never replaced it despite the outdated composition.
  • No reevaluation despite regulatory requirements: CSB found the company took no action to reassess this aging equipment even though OSHA and EPA regulations call for periodic reevaluation of piping and similar assets.
  • No remotely operated emergency isolation valves: the HF alkylation unit lacked remote isolation capability that could have stopped hydrocarbons from continuing to flow through the ruptured elbow, even though this isn't explicitly required by current American Petroleum Institute standards, CSB concluded it would very likely have prevented the explosions that followed.
  • Inadequate mechanical integrity program for HF-service piping: given how hazardous a release from this specific service is, the inspection and replacement criteria applied to this pipe were not rigorous enough to catch corrosion of this severity before failure.

Lessons Learned

  1. Material standards evolve for good reasons, equipment installed decades ago under since-superseded specifications needs a deliberate reassessment, not an assumption that "it's always been fine" remains true indefinitely.
  2. For services carrying materials as hazardous as HF, mechanical integrity inspection intervals and replacement criteria should be more conservative than the industry minimum, not aligned to it.
  3. Remote emergency isolation valves are a relatively modest investment that can be the difference between a contained upset and a sustained fire fed by continuing hydrocarbon flow, treat them as a serious design consideration for any high-hazard unit, standard-required or not.
  4. This incident sits alongside Husky Superior (2018) and ExxonMobil Torrance (2015) as a pattern: refineries operating HF alkylation units repeatedly show near-misses or actual releases tied to aging or failed isolation components, a strong argument for treating "inherently safer" alternatives to HF alkylation as a live conversation, not a hypothetical.
