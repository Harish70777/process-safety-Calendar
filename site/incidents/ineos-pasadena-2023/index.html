Anniversary: March 22, 2023

INEOS Phenol Pasadena Tanker BLEVE

Pasadena, TX, USA · 0 fatalities, 1 injuries
Operating ProceduresEmergency PlanningMechanical Integrity

What Happened

At approximately 11:45 a.m. on March 22, 2023, an explosion and fire occurred at the INEOS Phenol cumene production facility in Pasadena, Texas, while liquid bulk trucks were delivering liquid petroleum gas (LPG), a mixture of propylene and propane used as a raw material in cumene production. During offloading, a hose disconnected between the tank trailer and the facility, releasing flammable vapors that ignited. The fire around the tanker truck caused the liquid petroleum gas trapped inside to boil, resulting in a boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion, a BLEVE. The driver of the third-party bulk truck was thrown to the ground and injured; according to a subsequent lawsuit, he and coworkers attempted to evacuate the facility but found themselves initially trapped because various exits were not open, and experienced additional explosions and shock waves during the attempted evacuation. He was transported to a hospital and later treated for injuries to his neck, back, and spine, along with reported psychological trauma. INEOS personnel began firefighting operations immediately, joined by Channel Industries Mutual Aid and Harris County emergency responders; the fire was extinguished by approximately 1:00 p.m. Independent air monitoring downwind of the facility found no degradation in air quality, and officials said there was no danger to the surrounding community. The Harris County Fire Marshal's Office ruled the explosion an accident caused by the unintentional release of liquid petroleum gas from the trailer.

Root Causes

  • A hose disconnected during the tanker-to-facility transfer: the direct trigger for the incident was an unintentional disconnection of the transfer hose between the tank trailer and the facility, releasing flammable LPG vapor.
  • Released LPG ignited during the offloading process: once the hose disconnected and vapor escaped, it found an ignition source, though the specific ignition source was not identified in available public records.
  • The tanker underwent a BLEVE once engulfed in fire: the external fire heated the liquid petroleum gas still trapped inside the tanker to the point of a boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion, a well-documented and severe failure mode for pressurized flammable liquid containers exposed to fire.
  • Facility exits were reportedly not open during the evacuation attempt: according to the injured worker's subsequent lawsuit, he and his coworkers found themselves initially trapped inside the facility because various exits weren't open, delaying their escape during a period when additional explosions occurred.

Lessons Learned

  1. Hose connections used for transferring flammable liquefied gas need verified, redundant securing methods and monitoring during transfer, given that a single disconnection was enough to trigger this entire chain of events.
  2. Any tanker or vessel containing pressurized liquefied gas that becomes exposed to external fire carries genuine BLEVE risk; emergency response protocols need to account for this specific failure mode, including appropriate standoff distances for anyone not directly engaged in firefighting.
  3. Facility emergency egress needs to be verified as open and accessible at all times, particularly in areas where hazardous material transfer operations occur. A worker's account of finding exits not open during an actual emergency represents exactly the kind of gap that egress planning is meant to prevent.
  4. Even when overall consequences are limited to a single injury and no measurable off-site air quality impact, the underlying sequence, hose failure, ignition, and BLEVE, reflects hazards capable of much more severe outcomes under only slightly different circumstances, making full investigation and correction worthwhile regardless of the actual casualty count.
