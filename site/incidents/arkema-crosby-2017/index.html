Arkema Crosby Organic Peroxide Fire (Hurricane Harvey)

Crosby, TX, USA · 0 fatalities, 21 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisEmergency Planning And Response

What Happened

Hurricane Harvey brought an unprecedented volume of rainfall to the Arkema plant, which manufactured organic peroxides, chemicals that must be kept refrigerated below freezing or they self-heat, decompose, and can catch fire. The extreme flooding overwhelmed the site, knocking out both primary power and backup power to the low-temperature warehouses where the organic peroxides were stored. Staff worked to implement the plant's hurricane preparedness plan, moving the peroxides into refrigerated trailers and relocating those trailers to higher ground on site. Three trailers, however, could not be moved in time and were flooded, losing refrigeration entirely. With nothing left to stop the temperature rise, the organic peroxides inside began to self-heat, decompose, and ultimately combust. Over the course of three separate fires, more than 350,000 pounds of organic peroxide burned. All plant employees were evacuated, along with more than 200 residents within 1.5 miles of the facility, who were unable to return home for a week. 21 people sought medical attention for exposure to the resulting fumes and smoke.

Root Causes

  • Inadequate planning for extreme weather events: CSB found a significant, broader industry gap in guidance for facilities on how to prepare specifically for flooding and similarly severe weather scenarios, this wasn't a uniquely Arkema-specific planning failure so much as a reflection of thin standards across the sector.
  • Total power loss was not adequately anticipated: the site's hurricane preparedness planning did not fully account for the possibility of losing both primary and backup power simultaneously, which is exactly what the flooding caused.
  • Insufficient backup refrigeration capacity and mobility: even the contingency plan, moving product into refrigerated trailers, had a hard limit; three trailers could not be relocated to safety before floodwaters caught up with them, and once flooded, they had no remaining path to maintain safe temperature.
  • No robust industry guidance for flooding risk existed: CSB explicitly called out the lack of established guidance helping hazardous chemical facilities assess and prepare for flooding specifically, as distinct from other natural hazards more commonly planned for.

Lessons Learned

  1. Facilities in flood-susceptible areas need a documented analysis of their specific vulnerability to extreme natural events, flooding, high winds, earthquakes, as a formal part of process hazard analysis, not an assumption that general emergency planning covers it.
  2. Contingency plans built around mobile or temporary safeguards (like refrigerated trailers) need a hard deadline and fallback built in, "move it to safety" isn't a complete plan without an answer for what happens if you run out of time or the safe location itself floods.
  3. Total loss of both primary and backup power is a scenario worth explicitly planning for in any facility storing temperature-sensitive reactive materials, treating backup power as inherently reliable understates the real risk during genuinely extreme events.
  4. Climate-driven increases in extreme weather frequency and severity mean facility risk assessments written years ago may no longer reflect current conditions, CSB's call for updated industry-wide flooding guidance is a reminder to periodically revisit "we've always been fine" assumptions against a changing baseline.
