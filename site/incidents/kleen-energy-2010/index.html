Anniversary: February 7, 2010

Kleen Energy Plant Natural Gas Explosion

Middletown, CT, USA · 6 fatalities, 50 injuries
Operating ProceduresProcess Hazard Analysis

What Happened

During construction commissioning at the still-incomplete Kleen Energy power plant, workers were cleaning debris out of newly installed natural gas piping using a "gas blow", a common industry practice of venting high-pressure natural gas (around 650 psi) directly to atmosphere to blast rust, weld slag, and other debris out of the pipe. Over the course of the morning, gas blows released an estimated 400,000 standard cubic feet of natural gas into a congested outdoor area between two heat recovery steam generators, enough to fill a professional basketball arena with an explosive gas-air mixture. With numerous ongoing construction activities nearby, an undetermined ignition source set off the accumulated cloud, killing 6 workers and injuring 50 others, with an explosion large enough to register as a small earthquake.

Root Causes

  • Use of flammable gas as a cleaning medium: venting large volumes of natural gas directly to atmosphere near an active worksite is inherently unsafe, any ignition source in range turns the practice into an uncontrolled explosion risk.
  • No regulatory prohibition existed: the CSB found no specific federal workplace safety standard prohibited this practice at the time, despite the industry's long-standing awareness of similar past incidents.
  • Congested release location: gas was vented into a partially enclosed space between large structures, which slowed dispersion and allowed the gas-air mixture to build to explosive concentration rather than dissipating safely.
  • Numerous uncontrolled ignition sources present: with active construction work ongoing nearby, welding, electrical equipment, and other potential ignition sources were present in the same area the gas was accumulating.

Lessons Learned

  1. Never use a flammable gas as a purge or cleaning medium when a non-flammable alternative (compressed air, nitrogen, steam) exists, this trade-off is the entire root cause of this incident.
  2. If a hazardous work practice isn't explicitly banned by regulation, that's not evidence it's safe, "no rule against it" and "safe" are not the same thing.
  3. Any planned release of flammable material, however routine, needs a location and dispersion assessment before it happens, not just an assumption that open air is safe.
  4. This incident led directly to the CSB's recommendation to prohibit gas blows using flammable gas, which was subsequently incorporated into the National Fuel Gas Code and adopted into law in several states, including Connecticut.
