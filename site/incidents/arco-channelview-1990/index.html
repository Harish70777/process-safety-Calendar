Anniversary: July 5, 1990

ARCO Chemical Channelview Wastewater Tank Explosion

Channelview, TX, USA · 17 fatalities, 5 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityManagement Of ChangeProcess Hazard Analysis

What Happened

At approximately 11:21 p.m. on July 5, 1990, a wastewater storage tank exploded at the ARCO Chemical petrochemical plant in Channelview, Texas, roughly 15 miles east of Houston. The tank had been taken out of normal service so that a nitrogen compressor supplying its purge gas system could be repaired, and during this period the tank received only minimal nitrogen purging. Organic peroxides present in the wastewater decomposed inside the tank, generating oxygen. Because the air in the tank's headspace was largely stagnant, this oxygen accumulated in a "dead zone" rather than dispersing, and a temporary oxygen analyzer installed between two roof beams failed to detect the dangerous buildup in time. Contract workers from Austin Industrial were performing cleaning and maintenance work at the tank when the explosion occurred, most likely ignited by a gasoline engine on or near a truck at the site. The blast leveled an area the size of a city block, was heard as far as 10 to 20 miles away, and produced a fireball that rose roughly 200 feet into the air. Seventeen people were killed, five ARCO employees, eleven Austin Industrial contractors, and one truck driver for a separate contractor, and five others were injured. The explosion shut down roughly 15 percent of total U.S. styrene production capacity. In the aftermath, ARCO spent approximately $36 million to rebuild and upgrade the wastewater treatment area, including $20 million specifically to add redundancy to its safety systems.

Root Causes

  • Nitrogen purge gas flow was reduced during maintenance: taking the tank's nitrogen compressor out of service for repair meant the tank received only minimal purging during the period when oxygen was actively accumulating inside it.
  • An oxygen analyzer was positioned where it couldn't detect the actual hazard: the temporary oxygen analyzer installed between two roof beams sat outside the stagnant "dead zone" where oxygen was building up, so operators believed conditions were safe when they were not.
  • Organic peroxides in the wastewater decomposed and generated oxygen: this decomposition reaction was the direct source of the oxygen accumulation that created explosive conditions inside the tank.
  • An ignition source was present during active maintenance work: with contractors performing cleaning and maintenance at the tank, an ignition source, most likely a gasoline engine associated with a truck at the site, was close enough to trigger the explosion once explosive conditions had developed.

Lessons Learned

  1. Taking safety-critical purge or inerting systems out of service for maintenance needs a documented management of change process evaluating the resulting hazard during the outage, not just a plan to repair the system as quickly as possible.
  2. Gas detection and monitoring equipment needs to be positioned based on where a hazardous condition would actually develop, not simply somewhere convenient. An analyzer outside the stagnant zone where oxygen was accumulating provided false reassurance rather than real protection.
  3. Vessels containing organic peroxides or other materials capable of generating oxygen through decomposition need ongoing hazard evaluation for exactly this failure mode, especially whenever normal purging or inerting systems are compromised for any reason.
  4. Ignition sources like gasoline engines need to be excluded from the immediate vicinity of tanks handling flammable or hazardous materials during maintenance work, particularly when any process upset, like a disabled purge system, could be actively creating explosive conditions without visible warning signs.
