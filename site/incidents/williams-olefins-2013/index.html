Anniversary: June 13, 2013

Williams Olefins Plant Reboiler Explosion

Geismar, LA, USA · 2 fatalities, 167 injuries
Management Of ChangeMechanical IntegrityIncident Investigation

What Happened

The plant's propylene fractionator was originally designed in 1967 to run with two reboilers (heat exchangers) operating simultaneously, with no isolation valves between the reboilers and the fractionator, meaning both were protected from overpressure by a single relief device mounted on the fractionator itself. Years later, Williams changed operating practice to run the fractionator with only one reboiler in service at a time, which let the offline reboiler be isolated and cleaned without a full shutdown. This operational change, made without adequately addressing the resulting loss of overpressure protection, went unresolved for roughly 12 years. On the day of the incident, the reboiler in service was believed to be fouled, so the operations supervisor began the process of switching to the second reboiler, which had been isolated by a single closed block valve since February 2012 maintenance, but had liquid propane trapped inside despite that isolation. When hot water was directed into it to bring it into service, the trapped liquid flashed to vapor faster than the isolated, unprotected vessel could handle. The reboiler's shell catastrophically ruptured in a boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion (BLEVE), releasing roughly 30,000 pounds of hydrocarbons that ignited and burned for three and a half hours. Two workers died, one immediately, the operations supervisor from burns the next day, and 167 others were injured, most of them contractors working on a nearby expansion project.

Root Causes

  • A 1990s/2000s operational change removed overpressure protection without fixing it: switching to single-reboiler operation meant the offline reboiler could be isolated by a block valve with no relief device protecting it directly, a real reduction in the layer of protection the original design relied on.
  • The gap went unaddressed for 12 years: CSB found this was a known condition that persisted across more than a decade without the company implementing a fix, such as installing a dedicated relief device on each reboiler.
  • Poor process safety culture: the investigation concluded that broader deficiencies in how the site identified, tracked, and closed out process safety hazards allowed this specific, serious gap to remain unresolved for so long.
  • Liquid propane trapped inside an "isolated" vessel: despite the block valve being closed, propane had entered and remained in the offline reboiler, meaning the vessel wasn't actually empty or safe when hot water was reintroduced.

Lessons Learned

  1. Any operational change that removes or bypasses an existing protective layer, even one made years ago, even one that seems purely procedural, needs formal management of change review, including verifying the original design basis for overpressure protection still holds under the new mode of operation.
  2. A known, unresolved safety gap doesn't become less dangerous the longer it goes unaddressed, treat "we've operated this way for years without incident" as evidence of latent risk, not evidence of safety.
  3. Before reintroducing heat or reactivating an "isolated" vessel, verify it's actually empty or safe to heat, a closed valve upstream doesn't guarantee a vessel has no trapped hazardous material inside.
  4. Process safety culture failures often show up not as a single dramatic decision but as years of a known issue simply not getting fixed, audits and corrective action tracking need teeth, not just documentation.
