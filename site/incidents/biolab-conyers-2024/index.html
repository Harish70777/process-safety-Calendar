BioLab Conyers Warehouse Fire and Toxic Smoke Plume

Conyers, GA, USA · 0 fatalities, 0 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityProcess Hazard AnalysisIncident InvestigationManagement Of Change

What Happened

At approximately 5:10 a.m., a corroded component in the sprinkler system of Bio-Lab's Plant 12 warehouse failed, allowing water to leak onto stored chlorinated isocyanurate pool and spa treatment chemicals. Water contact with these reactive chemicals triggered an exothermic decomposition reaction that generated intense heat, activating more sprinklers and wetting additional stock, which spread the reaction further. Employees on fire watch, a precaution already in place due to strong chemical odors detected months earlier, heard a popping sound and recognized the product had gotten wet, but were unable to isolate the reacting material before flames became visible by 6:30 a.m. A second, larger fire broke out around noon, ultimately destroying the warehouse entirely. The reaction produced a massive plume of toxic smoke containing chlorine, hydrogen chloride, bromine, and other hazardous substances that spread across the greater Atlanta metropolitan area. Approximately 17,000 nearby residents were evacuated, and shelter-in-place advisories were issued for roughly 90,000 people, with warnings in the immediate area continuing nightly for 17 days. No injuries were reported at the site during or after the incident. CSB's investigation found the warehouse held nearly 14 million pounds of reactive chemicals, more than double the roughly 6.2 million pounds the company had originally disclosed to local authorities when the facility opened in 2019. KIK Consumer Products, the facility's operator, permanently ceased operations at the Conyers site in May 2025.

Root Causes

  • A corroded sprinkler component failed, introducing water to reactive chemicals: the direct trigger was a failed sprinkler part allowing water into direct contact with chlorinated isocyanurates, a combination known to produce a dangerous decomposition reaction.
  • Known corrosion had gone uncorrected for at least nine months: an inspection roughly nine months before the incident had already identified more than 1,100 corroded sprinkler heads elsewhere in the same facility, a warning sign that wasn't acted on in time to prevent this specific failure.
  • Actual chemical inventory was more than double what was declared: the facility stored nearly 14 million pounds of reactive material against a roughly 6.2 million pound figure disclosed when the warehouse first opened, a gap CSB found had never been re-reviewed as inventory grew.
  • A "run to failure" maintenance culture: CSB's final report specifically identified a pattern of allowing equipment, including corrosion-prone components like these sprinkler parts, to remain in service until they failed rather than being proactively replaced.
  • No organizational learning from prior incidents: KIK had experienced earlier fires and corrosion-related component failures at this and other facilities, but CSB found the company had not effectively institutionalized lessons from those events to prevent a repeat.
  • A regulatory gap left this hazard largely uncovered: chlorinated isocyanurates are not regulated under OSHA's Process Safety Management standard or EPA's Risk Management Program rule, despite their documented potential to release toxic gases when triggered.

Lessons Learned

  1. Corrosion identified during a routine inspection needs a tracked, timely repair, not an entry in a report that sits unaddressed. More than 1,100 corroded sprinkler heads had already been found in this facility months before the failure that triggered this incident.
  2. Fire suppression systems installed to protect a facility can themselves become the ignition mechanism when the stored material is water-reactive. Sprinkler system design and maintenance for warehouses holding reactive chemicals needs to account for this specific risk, not just generic fire protection standards.
  3. Declared chemical inventory needs periodic reconciliation against actual on-site quantities, especially as storage volume grows over time. A facility operating at more than double its originally disclosed inventory represents a significant, unreviewed change in hazard profile.
  4. A pattern of prior incidents at the same company, even at different locations, needs genuine organizational learning and cross-site corrective action, not just local, incident-specific fixes. CSB explicitly found this company's history of similar failures had not translated into systemic prevention.
  5. Reactive chemical hazards that fall outside OSHA PSM and EPA RMP coverage remain genuinely dangerous regardless of their regulatory status. This is a recurring theme across this database (Bayer CropScience, Optima Belle, Airgas), and this incident's scale, a chemical release affecting an entire metropolitan area, underscores how significant the consequences of that regulatory gap can be.
