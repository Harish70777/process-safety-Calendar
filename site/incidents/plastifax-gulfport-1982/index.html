Anniversary: June 2, 1982

Plastifax Company Nitration Explosion

Gulfport, MS, USA · 3 fatalities, 9 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisMechanical IntegrityEmergency Planning

What Happened

At approximately 1:45 p.m. on June 2, 1982, an explosion occurred at the Plastifax Company in Gulfport, Mississippi, during the nitration of 2-ethyl-hexanol to produce 2-ethyl-hexyl-nitrate, a process that used nitric acid. The explosion and resulting fire leveled the plant, described by a responding firefighter as looking "like a big Tinkertoy set," with only a few walls left standing. Metal debris from the blast was scattered nearly a mile away, and the fire and explosion quickly spread to Chemfax, a welding company operating next door. Three workers were killed; two died at the scene, and a third, found alive amid the wreckage, died at the hospital from trauma sustained in the explosion. Nine other people were injured according to NIOSH's official health hazard evaluation, though broader community reports described dozens more residents and responders affected by chemical exposure. Approximately 5,000 people were evacuated from the surrounding area for four hours as chemical smoke was visible up to 40 miles from the scene. Caustic liquids used in the manufacturing process spilled onto the ground at the site, and firefighting water mixed with released chemicals required nearby seaway canal inlets to be dammed off to prevent additional pollution. A subsequent NIOSH investigation found that some rescue workers' respiratory protection equipment ran out of air during the response, and recommended both an adequate supply of fresh air for future rescue operations and better supervision of potentially explosive chemical processes. The site was later designated a hazardous waste site under federal environmental law.

Root Causes

  • The nitration reaction became uncontrolled: the process of converting 2-ethyl-hexanol to 2-ethyl-hexyl-nitrate using nitric acid escalated beyond safe control, triggering the explosion, though the specific mechanical or procedural trigger wasn't detailed in available public records.
  • Insufficient supervision of a potentially explosive chemical process: NIOSH's investigation specifically recommended better supervision of processes with explosive potential, implying oversight of this nitration reaction had been inadequate to catch a developing hazard before it escalated.
  • Rescue workers' respiratory protection equipment ran out of air during the response: the emergency response itself was compromised when some responders' air supply was exhausted before their work at the scene was complete, extending their exposure to hazardous conditions.
  • Chemical plant operations sited near other businesses and residential areas: the fire's spread to the neighboring Chemfax welding company, along with the evacuation of 5,000 nearby residents, reflects how closely this hazardous process operated to other occupied areas.

Lessons Learned

  1. Nitration reactions and other processes with well-documented explosive potential need active, verified supervision throughout, not periodic oversight. NIOSH's explicit recommendation for better process supervision here points to a real gap between the hazard's known potential and the actual monitoring in place.
  2. Emergency responders need a verified, sufficient air supply plan for extended operations at a hazardous materials incident, including a resupply protocol, since running out of breathing air mid-response defeats the purpose of the protective equipment entirely.
  3. Chemical processes carrying explosion risk need physical separation from neighboring businesses sufficient to prevent a single incident from cascading into adjacent facilities, as happened here when the fire spread directly into the neighboring welding company.
  4. Firefighting and containment water needs a plan for managing chemical contamination before it can reach nearby waterways, given how quickly this incident required emergency damming of canal inlets to prevent broader environmental contamination.
