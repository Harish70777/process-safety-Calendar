Anniversary: November 17, 2003

DPC Enterprises Glendale Chlorine Release

Glendale, AZ, USA · 0 fatalities, 16 injuries
Operating ProceduresMechanical IntegrityIncident Investigation

What Happened

DPC Enterprises repackaged chlorine from railcars into smaller containers at its Glendale, Arizona facility, capturing excess chlorine vapor from the transfer process in a caustic scrubber that also produced household bleach as a saleable byproduct. On November 17, 2003, operators were transferring liquid chlorine from a railroad tank car to a tanker truck when the scrubber's caustic soda concentration, already routinely run down to less than 0.5 percent, a level CSB found left only a limited safety margin, dropped all the way to zero. With the active scrubbing chemical fully depleted, the scrubber could no longer absorb incoming chlorine vapor, which began venting directly into the atmosphere. Safety alarms sounded to warn operators the scrubber was nearing this exact condition, but CSB's investigation found it was common practice at the facility to allow chlorine transfer to continue even after the alarms activated, a direct violation of DPC's own written procedures. Even after the transfer was eventually shut off, the release didn't stop, since the now over-chlorinated bleach solution itself began breaking down through a separate chemical reaction, continuing to generate and release chlorine gas for approximately six hours. Up to 3,500 pounds of chlorine escaped in total. Local authorities ordered more than 4,000 residents across a 1.5 square mile area of Glendale and neighboring Phoenix to evacuate. Sixteen people, including 11 police officers and 5 residents, were treated for chlorine exposure symptoms including nausea, throat irritation, and headaches; no one was critically injured.

Root Causes

  • Alarms indicating an unsafe condition were commonly ignored: CSB found that letting chlorine continue flowing after the scrubber's low-caustic alarm sounded was routine practice at the facility, despite being a direct violation of DPC's own written procedures.
  • The scrubber operated with an insufficient safety margin by design: routinely running the caustic concentration down to below 0.5 percent left very little buffer between normal operation and complete depletion, meaning a single missed response to an alarm could exhaust the safeguard entirely.
  • No engineering safeguard automatically stopped the transfer: the facility relied on operators to manually respond to the alarm and halt the chlorine transfer, rather than having an automated system that would stop the process once the scrubber's capacity was exceeded.
  • A similar incident had already occurred at a sister facility: DPC's plant in Festus, Missouri, had released 48,000 pounds of chlorine in a separate, also CSB-investigated incident the year before, indicating a pattern that extended beyond this single Glendale facility.

Lessons Learned

  1. An alarm that operators have grown accustomed to overriding is providing no real protection, regardless of what the written procedure says should happen. If ignoring a specific alarm has become routine practice, that gap between procedure and reality needs to be closed before it causes an incident, not after.
  2. Safety-critical process parameters, like a scrubber's active chemical concentration, need a genuine safety margin built into normal operation, not a routine practice of running right up to the edge of failure.
  3. Safeguards this consequential, capable of releasing thousands of pounds of toxic gas if they fail, deserve engineering controls that don't depend entirely on a human operator correctly responding to an alarm in the moment. An automated shutoff tied directly to the safety parameter removes that dependency.
  4. A serious incident at one facility within a company needs to trigger a genuine safety review at every other facility handling the same process. DPC's Festus, Missouri release a year earlier involved the same fundamental hazard, chlorine scrubber failure, that recurred here.
