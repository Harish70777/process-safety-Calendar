Anniversary: July 17, 2001

Motiva Enterprises Delaware City Refinery Sulfuric Acid Tank Explosion

Delaware City, DE, USA · 1 fatalities, 8 injuries
Hot Work PermitMechanical Integrity

What Happened

A crew of contract workers was repairing grating on a catwalk above a spent sulfuric acid storage tank farm when a spark from their hot work ignited flammable vapors escaping from holes in the roof and shell of one of the tanks, holes caused by corrosion that had gone unaddressed. The tank, structurally weakened, separated from its floor and instantaneously released its contents; other tanks in the farm also failed and released theirs. Approximately 1.1 million gallons of spent sulfuric acid were released in total, with roughly 99,000 gallons reaching the Delaware River and killing thousands of fish and crabs. A fire burned for about half an hour. One contractor, a boilermaker performing the catwalk repair, was killed; eight others were injured.

Root Causes

  • Inadequate hot work program: CSB found Motiva's hot work permitting and control process did not adequately assess or mitigate the risk of performing spark-generating work directly above a tank farm holding large volumes of flammable and hazardous material.
  • Corroded storage tank with undetected structural damage: the failed tank had multiple holes in its roof and shell from corrosion, damage serious enough to compromise structural integrity, but which had not been caught or addressed before the incident.
  • Inspection schedule not followed: an internal inspection of the tank had been scheduled for 1999 under industry guidance calling for internal inspection every five years, but this inspection either did not occur as planned or did not catch the developing corrosion.
  • No emergency venting adequate to the hazard: the tank's venting and safety systems were not sufficient to safely relieve the conditions that developed once flammable vapors began accumulating and finding an ignition source.

Lessons Learned

  1. Hot work permits need to specifically evaluate what's underneath, above, or adjacent to the work area, not just the immediate work surface. A spark falling toward a tank farm is a fundamentally different risk than the same spark falling on bare concrete.
  2. A missed or overdue tank inspection isn't a paperwork lapse, it's a direct loss of your ability to know whether a vessel holding hazardous material is still structurally sound. Treat inspection schedule slippage as an operational risk escalation, not an administrative backlog.
  3. Corrosion-related holes in a tank shell or roof are themselves an active hazard signal, independent of any ignition source, any detected breach in a hazardous material tank's containment should trigger an immediate hazard evaluation, not wait for the next scheduled inspection cycle.
  4. Contractor safety and site hazard awareness need to flow both ways: the contract crew doing the catwalk work needed to understand exactly what hazard sat beneath them, and the site needed a hot work control process rigorous enough to catch that mismatch before work began.
