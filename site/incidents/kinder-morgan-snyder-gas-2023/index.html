Kinder Morgan Snyder CO2 Vessel Overpressure Rupture

Snyder, TX, USA · 0 fatalities, 0 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisMechanical IntegrityProcess Safety Information

What Happened

At 6:40 a.m. on January 31, 2023, a vessel catastrophically ruptured after overpressurizing at a Kinder Morgan carbon dioxide (CO2) gas plant in Snyder, Texas, propelling fragments up to 1,200 feet and heavily damaging a control room. Kinder Morgan estimated the property damage at more than $14 million. In the site's CO2 compression system, a liquid level control valve, known as a "dump valve," on a high-pressure compressor scrubber became stuck open, a malfunction the company found was common at the site during cold weather; the outside temperature at the time was 22 degrees Fahrenheit. Liquid drained through the stuck-open valve, and then high-pressure CO2 gas itself began flowing through it into the facility's low-pressure drain system. As the gas expanded into the lower-pressure system, it cooled dramatically through the Joule-Thomson effect, reaching temperatures as low as negative 30 degrees Fahrenheit. This extreme cold caused ice or hydrate to form in the drain system vessel's outlet piping, including the line leading to its own emergency pressure relief valve, blocking the one path that could have safely vented the rising pressure. Pressure in the drain system vessel, rated to a maximum of 125 pounds per square inch gauge, continued building until it reached an estimated 550 to 700 psig and ruptured in a brittle failure mode, scattering fragments across the site. The vessel had no instrumentation to allow operators to monitor its pressure, temperature, or liquid level, and the scrubber's liquid level and dump valve position were not being recorded, leaving personnel with no way to detect the developing abnormal condition before it became catastrophic. Kinder Morgan reported that approximately 5.6 million pounds of carbon dioxide were released, along with smaller quantities of natural gas, hydrogen sulfide, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides.

Root Causes

  • A dump valve became stuck open, a known cold-weather malfunction: this valve failure, which the company acknowledged was common at the site during cold weather, was the initiating event that allowed high-pressure gas to enter a system not designed to handle it.
  • Ice or hydrate formed and blocked the vessel's emergency relief valve: the extreme cooling from expanding CO2 gas froze the outlet piping leading to the very safety device meant to prevent overpressure, disabling it exactly when it was needed.
  • The vessel was rated for a small fraction of the pressure it eventually reached: designed for a maximum of 125 psig, the vessel ultimately failed at an estimated 550 to 700 psig, more than four times its rated capacity.
  • Prior hazard analyses had classified this scenario as operational, not a safety issue: two previous process hazard analyses had identified the potential for CO2 to blow through a scrubber dump valve, but treated it as an operational nuisance rather than a safety hazard, and never evaluated the potential for ice or hydrate formation in the downstream drain system as a result.
  • The failed vessel had no instrumentation to detect the developing condition: without pressure, temperature, or level monitoring, and without dump valve position being recorded, operators had no way to notice the abnormal condition building before the vessel ruptured.

Lessons Learned

  1. A malfunction acknowledged as "common" during certain conditions, like this dump valve sticking open in cold weather, is itself a standing hazard that needs engineering correction, not acceptance as a routine operational quirk to work around.
  2. Process hazard analyses need to fully trace the consequences of an identified failure mode through connected downstream systems, not stop at classifying the immediate event as operational. This facility's own PHA identified the valve failure but never followed it through to the ice-blocking and overpressure scenario that actually occurred.
  3. Low-pressure utility or drain systems that could receive an unplanned high-pressure gas input need pressure relief and instrumentation sized for that realistic worst-case scenario, not just their intended normal, low-pressure service.
  4. Any vessel handling process material needs basic instrumentation, pressure, temperature, and level monitoring, sufficient for operators to detect a developing abnormal condition before it escalates to failure, especially when that vessel sits downstream of equipment with a known history of malfunction.
