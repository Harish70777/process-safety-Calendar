Horsehead Zinc Refinery Explosion

Monaca, PA, USA · 2 fatalities, 1 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityManagement Of ChangeIncident InvestigationTraining

What Happened

At the Horsehead Corporation zinc refinery, molten zinc was purified in tall, ceramic distillation columns using a decades-old technology called the New Jersey process. One column, rebuilt about a month before the incident to separate zinc from cadmium, reused a sump at its base that had originally been designed for a different column configuration with a much lower liquid flow rate — the rebuild increased flow into that sump by four to five times. The sump became partially blocked, and because it couldn't drain properly, liquid zinc began accumulating and getting trapped inside the column rather than evaporating normally. Trapped and unable to release pressure through normal vapor flow, the liquid zinc became superheated by heat from the combustion chamber. Eventually the buildup ruptured the column, causing an "explosive decompression" that released a large volume of zinc vapor, which combusts spontaneously on contact with air. The rupture and resulting flash fire killed two operators, James Taylor and Corey Keller, who were performing unrelated maintenance work on a nearby column at the time; a third worker was seriously injured. Investigators later found symptoms of the developing blockage had been visible in the days before the explosion but were not recognized as a serious warning sign.

Root Causes

  • A design flaw carried over into a new configuration: the sump reused for the rebuilt column had never been designed for the much higher liquid flow rate that column would actually produce, creating the conditions for a blockage from the moment of the rebuild.
  • No effective management of change process: CSB's investigation found the facility did not follow proper management of change protocol when constructing the new cadmium-separation column, meaning the mismatch between the reused sump and the new flow conditions was never caught before startup.
  • A history of blockages had been normalized: the facility had experienced prior non-fatal explosions and column blockages, and rather than treating this pattern as an urgent unresolved hazard, the investigation found these conditions had become accepted as a normal, if unwelcome, part of operations.
  • Warning signs in the days before the explosion went unrecognized: the investigation concluded the facility missed several real opportunities to catch the developing blockage before it escalated, because staff weren't trained to recognize or evaluate the hazard signals accurately.

Lessons Learned

  1. Reusing existing equipment (like a sump) in a new configuration requires verifying it was actually designed for the new operating conditions — a component that worked safely under one flow rate can become a serious hazard under a significantly higher one without any visible change to the equipment itself.
  2. Any construction or equipment change during a rebuild needs a real management of change review, not just an assumption that "if it worked before, it'll work now" — this incident is a direct case of MOC being skipped specifically during the step that introduced the fatal flaw.
  3. A history of near-identical incidents at the same facility, even non-fatal ones, is a serious warning sign that deserves genuine root-cause investigation — CSB's investigators found this facility's own history contained real precedent for exactly the failure that eventually killed two people.
  4. Workers need to be specifically trained to recognize the early warning signs of developing hazards in their specific process, not just react to catastrophic failures after they occur — the investigation's finding that warning signs were visible "days before" the explosion but not understood as significant points to a training and hazard-awareness gap as much as a mechanical one.
