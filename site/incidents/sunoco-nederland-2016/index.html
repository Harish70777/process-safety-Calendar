Sunoco Nederland Crude Oil Terminal Flash Fire and Explosion

Nederland, TX, USA · 0 fatalities, 7 injuries
Hot Work PermitContractors

What Happened

A Sunoco contractor, L-Con, was conducting hot work, welding on the inside surface of a flange, on a section of pipe that still contained residual crude oil. The pipe segment had been plugged on both ends using isolation devices installed by a separate contractor, CARBER, hired by L-Con. During the welding operation, flammable vapor from the residual crude oil gathered in the space between the two installed isolation tools and ignited, causing a pressure buildup that led to an explosion at both ends of the isolated pipe section. Seven workers suffered burn injuries; four were transported to hospitals by ambulance and three by helicopter, with no fatalities. CSB's investigation, released more than six years after the incident as the oldest of its then-open cases, found that both Sunoco's and L-Con's hot work procedures had similar, significant gaps.

Root Causes

  • Hot work performed on equipment containing residual flammable material: the pipe had not been adequately cleaned or inerted to remove crude oil residue before welding began, a direct violation of the basic principle that hot work should never occur on equipment that currently or previously contained flammable material without proper preparation.
  • Sunoco's hot work procedure had a critical gap: CSB found the procedure did not adequately state that hot work on equipment that currently or previously contained flammable material was not permitted under OSHA or NFPA 51B requirements.
  • No clear guidance on cleaning or inerting equipment: even where the procedure gestured at hazard controls, it failed to clearly explain how workers were supposed to verify equipment was properly cleaned or inerted before hot work could safely proceed.
  • Contractor procedures mirrored the same deficiencies: L-Con's own hot work guidance had similar gaps to Sunoco's, meaning neither the facility owner's nor the contractor's procedures caught the hazard before work began.

Lessons Learned

  1. Hot work permits must explicitly prohibit welding, cutting, or grinding on any equipment that currently holds or has ever held flammable material, unless verified clean and inerted, this is a well-established industry principle, and its absence or ambiguity in a written procedure is a real, correctable gap.
  2. A hot work procedure needs to specify exactly how cleaning or inerting is verified, not just state that it should happen. Vague guidance that doesn't define the verification method leaves the actual determination to individual judgment under time pressure.
  3. When work involves multiple contractors (here, Sunoco, L-Con, and CARBER), each party's procedures need to be checked against each other for consistency, not just individually reviewed, gaps that exist in more than one party's guidance can compound rather than cancel out.
  4. CSB explicitly noted hot work incidents remain a common, recurring problem across U.S. facilities despite being "well-understood events and avoidable", this incident, alongside Packaging Corporation of America's two separate hot work fatalities in this database, underscores how a well-known hazard category continues to cause serious harm when procedural rigor lapses.
