Anniversary: July 11, 1978

Los Alfaques Propylene Tanker BLEVE

Alcanar, Spain · 215 fatalities, 200 injuries
Facility SitingEmergency Planning

What Happened

At approximately 2:36 p.m. on July 11, 1978, a road tanker truck carrying roughly 23 tons of liquefied propylene, en route from a refinery near Tarragona to an industrial site in central Spain, experienced a catastrophic tank failure directly alongside the Los Alfaques holiday campsite in Alcanar, Spain, a popular seaside destination for French, West German, and Belgian tourists. Accounts differ on the precise sequence: some witnesses reported the tank was already leaking before a loud bang, possibly a blown tire, caused the truck to swerve and crash into a retaining wall separating the campsite from the road; other analysis concluded there was no preceding mechanical failure or collision, and that the overstressed pressure vessel simply failed at a weak point. In either account, propylene began flashing from the ruptured tank within seconds, forming a white vapor cloud that drifted into the crowded campsite and toward a nearby discotheque. Campers, curious about the unusual cloud, approached rather than fled. The cloud reached an ignition source, flashed back to the tanker, and triggered a massive boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion (BLEVE) that sent towering flames hundreds of feet into the air, engulfing tents, trailers, and sunbathers on the adjacent beach almost instantly and setting off secondary explosions from butane cookers and vehicle fuel tanks. Parts of the tanker were found nearly half a mile away. Two hundred people were killed within minutes, most burned beyond recognition; fifteen more later died of their injuries, bringing the total to 215 deaths, including the truck driver, with 178 of the victims French nationals. More than 200 others suffered severe burns. Several individuals from the tanker's owning company were later prosecuted for criminal negligence, and Spain subsequently enacted legislation restricting the transit of vehicles carrying dangerous cargo through populated areas to nighttime hours only.

Root Causes

  • The tanker experienced a catastrophic loss of containment: whether triggered by a collision following tire failure or by the pressure vessel failing independently at a structural weak point, the tank's integrity was lost while directly adjacent to a densely populated area.
  • The campsite was sited immediately alongside a route used for hazardous material transport: placing a crowded holiday campground directly against a highway carrying tankers of liquefied flammable gas created the conditions for exactly this kind of consequence if a transport failure ever occurred nearby.
  • Onlookers approached the developing vapor cloud out of curiosity: rather than immediately evacuating on seeing an unusual white cloud, campers moved toward it to investigate, placing themselves directly in its path when it eventually ignited.
  • No restriction existed at the time on hazardous cargo transit through populated areas: the absence of any time-of-day or routing restriction meant a tanker carrying 23 tons of flammable liquefied gas could pass immediately alongside a crowded holiday site in broad daylight.

Lessons Learned

  1. Any unusual, unexplained vapor cloud near a hazardous transport route should be treated as an immediate evacuation trigger, not an object of curiosity, given how quickly a flammable gas cloud can find an ignition source and how catastrophic the consequence can be once it does.
  2. Land use planning near major transport corridors used for hazardous cargo needs to account for the realistic worst-case consequence of a transport failure, similar to how fixed-facility siting is now regulated, rather than treating transport routes as inherently lower-risk than stationary process equipment.
  3. This disaster directly led to regulatory change, restricting hazardous cargo transit through populated areas to nighttime hours in Spain, illustrating how transportation safety regulation, much like fixed-facility process safety regulation, often develops in direct response to catastrophic failure rather than proactive risk assessment.
  4. Public awareness and training about the danger of approaching visible gas releases, rather than treating them as a curiosity, is a low-cost, high-value safety measure that could plausibly have reduced casualties in this specific incident, given how many victims were positioned near the cloud by the time it ignited.
