Loy-Lange Box Company Pressure Vessel Explosion

St. Louis, MO, USA · 4 fatalities, 1 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityIncident InvestigationCompliance Audits

What Happened

The bottom head of a pressure vessel called a Semi-Closed Receiver (SCR), part of Loy-Lange's steam system, catastrophically failed, triggering a boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion (BLEVE). The failure fatally injured a Loy-Lange employee working nearby and launched the roughly 2,000-pound vessel through the air and through the roof of a neighboring business, Faultless Healthcare Linen, roughly 500 feet away, fatally injuring three employees there, including two on their very first day of work. CSB's investigation found the vessel had been corroding for years: oxygen pitting corrosion, driven by Loy-Lange's own startup practices introducing oxygenated water into the system and inadequate water treatment that failed to remove dissolved oxygen, had steadily thinned the metal. The company had been aware of corrosion problems as far back as 2004. A 2012 repair attempt removed only part of the corroded metal, and a subsequent inspection failed to catch that the original, still-compromised steel remained in place. That same original steel is what ultimately failed five years later. In the days before the explosion, employees had discovered the vessel was actively leaking; a contacted welder was unable to visit in time, and Loy-Lange continued operating the equipment anyway.

Root Causes

  • Known corrosion mechanism, poorly controlled for over a decade: Loy-Lange's own operating practices contributed directly to the corrosion that ultimately caused the failure, and the company was aware corrosion was a problem as early as 2004.
  • An incomplete repair left the original defect in place: the 2012 repair addressed only part of the corroded area, and a follow-up inspection did not detect that some of the original compromised steel remained, that steel is what failed five years later.
  • No regular inspection program despite known risk: Loy-Lange did not implement a program for regularly inspecting the pressure vessel, despite industry guidance calling for exactly that given the known corrosion history.
  • The vessel was never permitted or registered with the city: Loy-Lange never applied for an installation or repair permit, or registered the pressure vessel with St. Louis, meaning it fell outside the city's inspection process entirely, even though annual inspections were technically required for boilers and pressure vessels in the city.
  • Operated despite a known active leak in the days before failure: when employees discovered the vessel was leaking just before the explosion, the company continued operating it rather than taking it out of service, after being unable to promptly arrange a repair visit.

Lessons Learned

  1. A known corrosion mechanism, once identified, needs an ongoing, documented management program, not a one-time repair years later that may not even fully address the original problem.
  2. Any repair to a pressure-containing vessel needs verification that it actually removed all compromised material, a repair that leaves defective steel in place provides a false sense of resolution while the real hazard persists.
  3. Equipment that falls outside formal regulatory registration doesn't become safer for being unregistered, if anything, it means the facility carries sole responsibility for a rigor that regulatory inspection would otherwise help enforce.
  4. An actively leaking pressure vessel is a stop-operating signal, not a schedule-a-repair signal, this incident is a direct, tragic case of a company continuing to run known-compromised equipment because a repair couldn't be arranged quickly enough, with catastrophic consequences reaching well beyond the facility's own walls.
