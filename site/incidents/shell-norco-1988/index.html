Anniversary: May 5, 1988

Shell Norco Catalytic Cracking Unit Explosion

Norco, LA, USA · 7 fatalities, 18 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityFacility SitingProcess Hazard Analysis

What Happened

At 3:37 a.m. on May 5, 1988, an explosion occurred in the catalytic cracking unit (CCU) of the Shell Oil refinery in Norco, Louisiana. The explosion resulted from corrosion of an 8-inch vapor line running from a 10-inch header that carried the main overhead vapor from the unit's depropanizer column, operating at approximately 270 pounds per square inch of pressure. The corroded line failed catastrophically, releasing an estimated 17,000 pounds of hydrocarbon vapor in roughly 30 seconds. A possible ignition source was the unit's superheater furnace. The resulting explosion was likely an aerial vapor cloud explosion centered in the area between the depropanizer and the CCU control room. Five employees were found fatally injured inside the control room itself; a sixth was found roughly 30 feet outside the building; a seventh was fatally injured while exiting a separate nearby control room. Damage radiated a full mile from the explosion's center, with debris found as far as five miles away; the blast rattled windows as far as 45 miles away in Slidell, on the other side of Lake Pontchartrain. Eighteen additional people were injured according to OSHA's official investigation record, though other contemporary reports described a broader count of residents and workers affected by the blast and evacuation. According to accounts from a former plant employee, the unit had a known corrosion problem, evidenced by an ammonia injection system installed specifically at the pipe elbows where the failure ultimately occurred, and had been pushed to process significantly more crude oil per day than its original design capacity.

Root Causes

  • A corroded vapor line ruptured under normal operating pressure: the direct cause was corrosion in the 8-inch line, which had thinned enough over time to fail catastrophically at the unit's standard 270 psi operating pressure.
  • The unit was operated well beyond its original design capacity: according to a former employee's account, the catalytic cracker had been progressively pushed to process roughly three times its original designed throughput, a change that would have altered stress and flow conditions throughout the unit's piping.
  • A known corrosion problem already had a mitigation measure installed: the presence of an ammonia injection system specifically at the pipe elbows that failed indicates the facility was aware corrosion was a concern at this exact location, yet the mitigation in place wasn't sufficient to prevent the eventual failure.
  • The control room was sited close to the hazardous process area: most of the fatalities occurred because personnel were inside or near the control room when the vapor cloud exploded, placing continuously occupied space close to equipment carrying high-pressure hydrocarbon vapor.

Lessons Learned

  1. Progressively increasing a process unit's throughput well beyond its original design basis needs a full re-evaluation of piping, equipment, and corrosion allowances for the new operating conditions, not just continued operation under the assumption that existing equipment can absorb the change.
  2. An existing corrosion mitigation measure, like the ammonia injection system present here, needs verification that it's actually adequate for the corrosion rate the equipment is experiencing, not just installed once and assumed effective indefinitely.
  3. Control rooms and other continuously staffed spaces need siting decisions that account for the realistic consequence of a vapor cloud explosion from nearby high-pressure hydrocarbon piping, not just routine operational proximity needs.
  4. A facility's own internal safety measures, like a targeted corrosion mitigation system, can be a strong indicator that a known hazard exists at a specific location; that existing awareness deserves escalation and independent verification rather than being treated as a solved problem once a mitigation is in place.
