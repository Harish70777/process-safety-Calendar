Shell Deer Park Olefins Unit Heat Exchanger Fire

Deer Park, TX, USA · 0 fatalities, 9 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityOperating ProceduresEmergency Planning

What Happened

At approximately 2:56 to 3:00 p.m. on May 5, 2023, a fire broke out in the olefins unit at Shell's Deer Park chemical plant in Texas, while workers were conducting routine maintenance on a heat exchanger. A leak developed during a heat exchange between two heavy gas oils, and the escaping material, which included cracked heavy gas oil, cracked light gas oil, and gasoline, ignited. Emergency responders initially believed an explosion had occurred based on early reports, though officials later clarified it was a fire without an explosion. Nine contract workers were taken to the hospital as a precaution, most for heat exhaustion and proximity to the fire rather than burns; all were evaluated and released without serious injury. No shelter-in-place order was issued, and air monitoring by Shell and the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality did not detect harmful chemical levels in the surrounding community. Firefighting efforts continued for hours the first day, but the fire reignited on both Saturday and Sunday after Shell had reported it extinguished. By the second day, the large volume of water used to fight the fire exceeded the capacity of the facility's wastewater storage, requiring a controlled discharge of runoff water into the Houston Ship Channel, with containment boom deployed as a precaution against product entering the waterway.

Root Causes

  • A leak developed during a heat exchange between two gas oils: the direct cause of the fire was a leak that occurred during this heat transfer process, releasing flammable hydrocarbon material that subsequently ignited.
  • The incident occurred during routine maintenance: workers were actively performing maintenance on the heat exchanger when the leak developed, indicating the maintenance activity itself was connected to the failure.
  • The fire reignited multiple times over several days: despite being reported extinguished, the fire restarted on both the following Saturday and Sunday, indicating the initial firefighting effort hadn't fully addressed the underlying fuel source or hot spots.
  • Wastewater containment capacity was exceeded by extended firefighting: the volume of water required to control a multi-day fire surpassed the facility's on-site storage capacity, forcing an emergency controlled discharge into a nearby waterway.

Lessons Learned

  1. Heat exchanger maintenance involving hydrocarbon process fluids needs procedures that specifically account for the possibility of a leak during the maintenance activity itself, not just during normal operation.
  2. Firefighting success needs verification that underlying hot spots or residual fuel sources are actually extinguished before being reported as controlled, given how this fire reignited twice after being declared out.
  3. Facilities need wastewater containment capacity planning that accounts for extended, multi-day firefighting scenarios, not just routine operational wastewater volumes, since this incident's water usage exceeded on-site storage and required emergency discharge planning.
  4. Clear, accurate initial incident communication matters for community trust, given the early confusion here between reports of an explosion versus what officials ultimately clarified was a fire without an explosion.
