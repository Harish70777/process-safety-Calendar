Anniversary: February 16, 2007

Valero McKee Refinery Propane Fire (Frozen Dead-Leg Piping)

Sunray, TX, USA · 0 fatalities, 4 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityProcess Hazard Analysis

What Happened

A valve on a capped, out-of-service section of piping, a "dead-leg" that had been disconnected from active use since the early 1990s, was accidentally left partially open, allowing small amounts of propane and entrained water to seep into the idle pipe. Being denser than propane, water accumulated at the low point of the piping elbow over time. A period of unusually cold weather in early February 2007 froze that trapped water, which expanded and cracked the pipe. When temperatures rose again on February 16, the ice that had been sealing the crack melted, and high-pressure liquid propane began escaping at an estimated rate of 4,500 pounds per minute through the fractured elbow. The resulting vapor cloud drifted toward a boiler house, found an ignition source, and erupted into a fire that grew rapidly, threatening a nearby LPG storage area and collapsing a major pipe rack. Three employees and one contractor were injured, three of them with serious burns. The refinery was completely shut down for nearly two months and operated at reduced capacity for close to a year afterward, with direct losses exceeding $50 million.

Root Causes

  • A frozen, previously undetected leak into out-of-service piping: propane and water had been slowly entering a supposedly idle "dead-leg" through a valve that wasn't fully closed, a leak path that existed for years without being identified.
  • No formal freeze protection program: CSB found Valero did not have corporate-wide requirements for formal, written freeze protection practices at refineries subject to freezing temperatures, despite this being a foreseeable seasonal risk in the region.
  • Idle piping left connected to an active, high-pressure process: the dead-leg remained physically connected to the live propane system rather than being properly isolated or removed, creating an ongoing pathway for exactly this kind of failure.
  • No remotely operated shutoff for this section: once the leak began, there was no way to quickly isolate the affected piping remotely, extending how long the fire could be fed and complicating firefighting efforts under high, shifting winds.

Lessons Learned

  1. Out-of-service or infrequently used piping ("dead-legs") connected to live processes need a formal identification and freeze-protection program, being idle doesn't make a line exempt from process safety risk if it's still physically connected to an active system.
  2. A valve that isn't fully closed on an idle line is functionally an active leak path, periodic verification of isolation valve integrity on dead-legs deserves the same attention as valves in continuous active service.
  3. Facilities operating in climates with freezing temperatures need explicit, written freeze protection requirements as a standing corporate policy, not an ad hoc response to a specific cold snap, the risk is seasonal and recurring, and the program needs to be too.
  4. Remote emergency isolation capability matters even for equipment normally considered "idle", this incident is a direct example of how a supposedly inactive piece of piping can become the source of a major, hard-to-control fire once conditions change.
