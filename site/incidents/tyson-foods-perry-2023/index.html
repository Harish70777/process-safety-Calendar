Tyson Foods Perry Ammonia Compressor Release

Perry, IA, USA · 0 fatalities, 1 injuries
Operating ProceduresMechanical IntegrityTraining

What Happened

At approximately 4:55 p.m. on January 7, 2023, about one pound of anhydrous ammonia was released at the Tyson Fresh Meats facility in Perry, Iowa, seriously injuring one employee. The worker was performing maintenance on an out-of-service ammonia compressor used in the facility's refrigeration system; the compressor had been isolated from the broader system, and the ammonia inside was believed to have been removed entirely. While removing bolts on the flange connecting the outlet piping to the compressor, a burst of ammonia vapor was released directly into the employee's chest and face. Because the compressor was understood to be empty, the employee was not wearing respiratory protection at the time. He was taken to a hospital for treatment of his ammonia exposure injuries. Tyson's subsequent investigation found that the company's ammonia removal procedure had allowed some ammonia to remain trapped between the compressor's discharge check valve, which only permits flow in one direction, and an isolation valve. A separate valve needed to be opened to fully remove ammonia from this isolated section of piping, but the removal procedure did not include instructions to open that specific valve, so it remained closed throughout the process meant to empty the equipment.

Root Causes

  • Ammonia remained trapped between a check valve and an isolation valve: the specific piping configuration created a pocket where residual ammonia could remain even after the broader removal process was believed complete.
  • The ammonia removal procedure did not include the valve needed to clear this trapped section: the written procedure was missing a step, opening a specific valve, that would have allowed this residual ammonia to actually be removed rather than remaining hidden in the piping.
  • The worker was not wearing respiratory protection because the equipment was believed empty: the false assumption that the compressor had been fully cleared of ammonia directly led to a protective equipment decision that left the employee exposed once trapped ammonia was released.

Lessons Learned

  1. Written removal or draining procedures for hazardous material equipment need to be verified against the actual complete piping configuration, not just the main flow path, since a check valve or similar component can create an isolated pocket that a general procedure fails to address.
  2. Respiratory and other protective equipment decisions should be based on verified confirmation that equipment is actually empty, not the expectation that a removal procedure was sufficient, particularly for any equipment that has held a toxic substance like ammonia.
  3. Check valves, by their one-directional design, can trap material in ways that aren't always obvious when planning a draining or removal sequence; procedures for equipment containing these components need specific attention to whether residual material could remain on either side of the valve.
  4. When a procedure is found to have missed a specific valve or step after an incident, as happened here, the same gap likely exists in any other equipment using a similar piping configuration, and correcting the procedure for the equipment involved in the incident isn't sufficient without checking for the same vulnerability elsewhere in the facility.
