Anniversary: April 8, 2021

Yenkin-Majestic Resin Plant Vapor Cloud Explosion and Fire

Columbus, OH, USA · 1 fatalities, 8 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityOperating Procedures

What Happened

During a batch resin-production operation, an operator added liquid solvent to a kettle of hot resin while the kettle's agitator was turned off, contrary to established procedure. When the agitator was later activated, the sudden mixing caused the solvent to rapidly vaporize, building pressure inside the kettle. That pressure forced naphtha solvent vapor and resin liquid out through the seal of a manway — an access port that had been newly installed on the kettle only three months earlier. The escaping vapor formed a flammable cloud that spread quickly through the facility and found an ignition source, triggering an explosion that was seen, heard, and felt throughout parts of Columbus, followed by a fire that burned for roughly 11 hours. One employee was killed; eight others were injured, one seriously enough to require a leg amputation after being crushed under collapsed debris. CSB's investigation found the newly installed manway "was not designed, constructed, or pressure tested to a design pressure appropriate for the process" — a mechanical integrity failure baked in from the moment it was installed.

Root Causes

  • A newly installed manway inadequate for the actual process pressure: the component that ultimately released the flammable vapor had never been properly designed, built, or pressure tested for the conditions it needed to withstand, despite being installed just three months before the incident.
  • No engineering control preventing solvent addition with the agitator off: the plant relied on a computer panel indicator to show agitator status to operators rather than a physical interlock preventing solvent addition when the agitator wasn't running — leaving the safeguard dependent on an operator correctly checking and acting on a screen reading.
  • No flammable gas detection or evacuation alarms: once the vapor cloud began forming and spreading through the facility, there was no instrumented system to detect it or alert personnel to evacuate before it found an ignition source.
  • No flame-resistant PPE requirement: workers in operating areas processing flammable chemicals were not required to wear flame-resistant clothing, a gap CSB specifically flagged in its recommendations.
  • A broader industry guidance gap: CSB found a general lack of clear industry guidance for low-pressure vessels used in hazardous chemical service — this wasn't purely a facility-level failure but reflected a genuine gap in the standards available to guide facilities like this one.

Lessons Learned

  1. Any newly installed component in hazardous chemical service — even something as seemingly routine as an access manway — needs verified pressure testing matched to actual process conditions before being put into service, not just standard installation practice.
  2. Safety-critical process states (like whether an agitator is running) that affect whether it's safe to add material should be enforced by physical engineering controls, not just displayed to operators as information they're expected to check and correctly interpret every time.
  3. Flammable gas detection and automatic evacuation alarms are essential wherever flammable solvents are processed in enclosed or semi-enclosed spaces — a spreading vapor cloud needs to be caught by instrumentation, not first noticed by workers as it reaches an ignition source.
  4. When industry-wide guidance for a category of equipment (like low-pressure vessels in hazardous service) is genuinely thin, individual facilities need to compensate with more conservative internal engineering standards rather than assuming compliance with general practice is sufficient — this incident is part of why CSB specifically recommended API develop dedicated guidance for this equipment class.
