Anniversary: May 3, 2019

AB Specialty Silicones Chemical Explosion

Waukegan, IL, USA · 4 fatalities, 1 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisIncident InvestigationMechanical Integrity

What Happened

During production of a silicon hydride emulsion product, an operator was adding potassium hydroxide (KOH) to adjust the batch's pH. Drums of KOH solution being brought to the area looked identical to drums of a different chemical, Andisil XL10, used elsewhere in the same process, and KOH is highly reactive with XL10. When the two ended up mixed, the reaction rapidly generated explosive quantities of flammable hydrogen gas. Workers noticed something was wrong and attempted to open the emulsion area to outside air and start ventilation fans, but couldn't complete those efforts before the accumulating hydrogen found an ignition source and the building exploded. Four workers were killed, including the company's owner and a second co-owner, and a fifth was seriously injured. The facility was destroyed and nearby businesses sustained extensive damage.

Root Causes

  • Two chemically incompatible materials mixed due to visually identical containers: the drums holding KOH and XL10 looked the same, creating a real risk of mix-up that materialized with fatal consequences.
  • No effective hazard analysis program: CSB found AB Specialty lacked a comprehensive hazard analysis process that could have identified the incompatibility risk and the container confusion hazard before it caused an incident.
  • A near-identical 2014 incident had already occurred and gone uncorrected: a smaller chemical explosion in 2014 caused temporary hearing loss to a worker, and the company's own investigation at the time concluded a "lack of comprehensive hazard analysis" contributed, but no meaningful improvements followed.
  • No functioning hydrogen or flammable gas detection in the building: despite handling chemistry capable of generating explosive hydrogen, the production area had no working gas detection system to provide early warning as the hazard developed.
  • The facility fell below regulatory oversight thresholds: the quantities of hazardous material used and stored did not trigger EPA Risk Management Program or OSHA Process Safety Management standard requirements, meaning the company wasn't legally required to implement the kind of formal safety program that might have caught this hazard.

Lessons Learned

  1. Visually distinguish containers of chemically incompatible materials as a baseline control, color-coding, labeling, and physical separation of storage areas are inexpensive safeguards against exactly this kind of mix-up, and their absence is a real, addressable gap.
  2. A company's own prior investigation finding, "lack of comprehensive hazard analysis contributed to this incident", is a direct, self-issued warning. Treat your own past near-miss conclusions as binding commitments to fix the identified gap, not filed paperwork.
  3. Facilities that fall below regulatory thresholds for formal process safety programs are not thereby exempt from the underlying hazards, the chemistry doesn't know or care what size threshold triggers PSM or RMP coverage. Smaller facilities handling reactive chemistry need genuine hazard analysis regardless of regulatory obligation.
  4. Flammable gas detection is a foundational safeguard for any process capable of generating hydrogen or similar gases as a byproduct, this incident is a stark example of workers recognizing a developing hazard by smell or sight, with no instrumentation giving them the earlier warning that could have meant the difference between evacuation and explosion.
