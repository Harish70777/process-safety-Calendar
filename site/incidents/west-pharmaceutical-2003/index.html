Anniversary: January 29, 2003

West Pharmaceutical Services Dust Explosion

Kinston, NC, USA · 6 fatalities, 38 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisIncident InvestigationMechanical Integrity

What Happened

A fine polyethylene powder, used as an anti-tack agent in the plant's rubber compounding process for medical products like syringe plungers and vial seals, had accumulated above a suspended ceiling over the manufacturing area, a space that was never designed or maintained to prevent dust buildup. An explosion and fire destroyed the plant, killing six workers and injuring 38 others including two firefighters; the blast was felt 25 miles away, and burning debris ignited wooded areas up to two miles from the site. Because the explosion caused such extensive structural damage, investigators were never able to definitively determine what dispersed the accumulated dust into an explosive concentration or what ultimately ignited it, CSB identified four plausible ignition theories without being able to confirm which one occurred. What investigators could establish clearly was the underlying condition that made an explosion possible at all: years of dust accumulating, undetected and unaddressed, in an inaccessible space above the ceiling.

Root Causes

  • No adequate engineering assessment of the dust hazard: the company never properly evaluated the combustible dust risk created by using powdered zinc stearate and polyethylene as anti-tack agents in its process.
  • Fire safety standards were never consulted: CSB found the company's engineering management systems did not ensure relevant industry fire codes, specifically NFPA 654, which requires sealing inaccessible spaces prone to dust accumulation, were referenced or applied.
  • Inadequate hazard communication: the plant's hazard communication program did not identify combustible dust as a hazard, so the workforce had no awareness of the risk building up above their heads.
  • A prior warning sign was ignored: the company never conducted a documented investigation of an earlier incident in which dust had ignited during welding, a clear precursor that, properly investigated, could have revealed the hazard before it became fatal.
  • Non-rated electrical equipment in a dust-prone space: electrical fixtures above the suspended ceiling were not rated for use in combustible dust environments as the National Electric Code requires for such spaces.

Lessons Learned

  1. An inaccessible space (like above a suspended ceiling) is not a space that's exempt from dust control, it needs to be sealed or otherwise engineered to prevent accumulation precisely because it can't be easily inspected or cleaned.
  2. Any prior ignition event involving a material later found to be explosive, however minor it seemed at the time, deserves a full documented investigation, not a shrug. This incident is one of several in this database where an unexamined near-miss preceded a fatal event.
  3. Material Safety Data Sheet review needs to specifically screen for combustible dust hazards, not just the known hazards of a material in its bulk or liquid form, a powder that seems inert can carry an entirely separate hazard profile than the same substance handled as a solid or slurry.
  4. This incident was one of three fatal U.S. dust explosions investigated by CSB in 2003 alone (alongside CTA Acoustics and Hayes Lemmerz), and directly triggered CSB's national combustible dust hazard study, a strong example of how a pattern across several incidents in a short window can reveal an industry-wide gap that no single investigation would surface alone.
