Anniversary: February 18, 2008

Alon USA Big Spring Refinery Propylene Splitter Explosion

Big Spring, TX, USA · 0 fatalities, 5 injuries
Facility SitingMechanical IntegrityEmergency Planning

What Happened

At approximately 8:15 to 8:20 a.m. on February 18, 2008, Presidents Day, an explosion occurred at the Alon USA Energy refinery in Big Spring, Texas, centered around the facility's propylene splitter unit. The blast was strong enough to be felt for miles, reportedly as far away as Andrews and Colorado City, Texas, shattering or cracking windows, walls, and doors and popping open doors at buildings well beyond the refinery's boundary. A large black smoke plume rose to roughly 6,500 feet and drifted away from town. The fire that followed the initial explosion threatened the refinery's adjacent alkylation unit, where hydrofluoric acid, a highly toxic and corrosive chemical, is used and stored, though the fire did not reach that unit or trigger an HF release. Five people were injured: one employee was hospitalized for burns, three contractors were treated and released, and a fifth person, a bystander, was injured when her car was struck by debris on the adjacent interstate highway. Local schools and Interstate 20 were closed, and a limited evacuation of the immediate area, including a nearby industrial plant, was conducted. Because the explosion occurred on a holiday, only about 40 workers were on site compared to the roughly 170 typically present, a staffing difference company officials suggested may have reduced the number of people injured. The refinery, built in 1928, remained shut down for approximately two months following the incident.

Root Causes

  • The explosion originated in the propylene splitter unit: company officials identified this unit, located on the south side of the plant, as the point of origin, though publicly available sources do not detail the specific mechanical or process failure that triggered the explosion.
  • The resulting fire threatened an adjacent unit handling a far more hazardous chemical: the alkylation unit's hydrofluoric acid inventory sat close enough to the fire that its safety became an active concern during the emergency response, even though it was ultimately not compromised.
  • Reduced holiday staffing may have limited the human toll: with roughly a quarter of the normal workforce present due to the Presidents Day holiday, fewer people were in the affected areas than would typically be the case, a circumstance of timing rather than a safety measure.

Lessons Learned

  1. Facility layout needs to account for the possibility that a fire or explosion in one unit could threaten a much more hazardous inventory in an adjacent unit, as happened here with the fire's proximity to hydrofluoric acid storage. Physical separation and fire barriers between units handling different hazard classes matter precisely for scenarios like this.
  2. A close call involving a highly toxic chemical inventory, even when the chemical itself is never released, deserves serious follow-up investigation into why the fire came as close as it did, not just relief that the worst-case outcome didn't occur.
  3. Staffing levels during holidays or off-hours affect the realistic consequence of an incident in ways worth incorporating into hazard and risk planning, since a similar explosion during full staffing could plausibly have resulted in significantly more injuries.
  4. Full public transparency about the specific technical cause of refinery incidents, even non-fatal ones, supports broader industry learning. This incident's underlying mechanical or process trigger doesn't appear to have been detailed in public reporting to the same degree as its consequences.
