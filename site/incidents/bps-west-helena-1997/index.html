Anniversary: May 8, 1997

BPS West Helena Pesticide Warehouse Explosion

West Helena, AR, USA · 3 fatalities, 17 injuries
Process Safety InformationEmergency PlanningFacility Siting

What Happened

BPS operated a bulk storage and distribution facility in West Helena, Arkansas, repackaging the agricultural pesticide azinphos-methyl (AZM). As a bulk sack of the pesticide was being offloaded into a warehouse, employees noticed smoke coming from the building and notified the West Helena Fire Department. A team of four firefighters entered to locate the source of the smoke; company representatives reportedly told them there was no risk of an explosion. While they searched, an explosion occurred, and a collapsing cinderblock wall killed three of the firefighters, Stewart Warren, Ed Hudson, and Reginald Robinson, and injured the fourth. The joint EPA/OSHA investigation determined the most likely cause was decomposition of the AZM pesticide while a bulk sack of it sat in contact with a hot compressor discharge pipe, generating heat and eventually triggering the explosion. The fire released a large cloud of toxic gas and burned for nearly two weeks before being fully extinguished. Hundreds of residents, including 44 patients and 150 employees evacuated from the nearby Helena Regional Medical Center, were moved out of the area, and a stretch of the Mississippi River was closed to traffic for roughly 12 hours. An additional 17 firefighters required treatment for heat exhaustion and other injuries during the extended firefighting and recovery operation.

Root Causes

  • Pesticide decomposed in contact with a hot compressor discharge pipe: the investigation found a bulk sack of azinphos-methyl generated heat through decomposition after being placed in contact with heated equipment, initiating the sequence that led to the explosion.
  • Firefighters were misinformed about the explosion risk: company representatives reportedly told the responding fire department there was no chance of explosive harm, information that directly shaped how the firefighters approached searching for the smoke's source.
  • Responders were sent to locate a smoke source without adequate hazard information: the fire department team entered the building actively looking for the origin of the smoke, a task that placed them in the building precisely when the explosive failure occurred.
  • No effective process safety information was provided to guide the emergency response: the facility did not equip responding firefighters with an accurate understanding of the specific chemical hazard they were dealing with, undermining their ability to make an informed risk decision before entering.

Lessons Learned

  1. Facilities storing reactive or thermally unstable chemicals need to provide emergency responders with accurate, specific hazard information immediately, not reassurances that may not reflect a genuine understanding of the chemical's actual risk profile.
  2. Pesticide and other reactive chemical storage needs physical separation from heat sources like compressor discharge piping. Direct or indirect contact with unrelated hot equipment can be enough to initiate a hazardous decomposition reaction.
  3. When a company represents that a hazardous material poses no explosion risk, emergency responders and the company itself both carry responsibility for that representation being accurate. This incident's fatal outcome followed directly from firefighters acting on information that proved wrong.
  4. Sending personnel to actively search for a source of smoke or an unclear hazard, rather than establishing a safe standoff distance until the situation is better understood, carries real risk when the material involved has any decomposition or explosion potential that hasn't been fully ruled out.
