Suncor Commerce City Dead-Leg Freeze and Vapor Cloud Explosion

Commerce City, CO, USA · 0 fatalities, 2 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityOperating ProceduresEmergency Planning

What Happened

At approximately 10:55 a.m. on December 24, 2022, an accidental release of roughly 1,400 pounds of flammable light straight-run gasoline occurred within a hydrotreating unit at the Suncor Energy refinery in Commerce City, Colorado. From June 17, 2022 until the day of the incident, a steam turbine-driven pump and its surrounding equipment had been locked out for maintenance, closing the inlet and outlet valves and creating a temporary "dead leg," a section of piping with no flow. The process fluid inside, a mixture of hydrocarbon and water, sat stagnant for 190 days. During a severe cold snap from December 21 to 24, ambient temperatures dropped below freezing, and the water within the isolated piping froze. When daytime temperatures rose on December 24 and the ice began to melt, flammable hydrocarbons escaped through the flange of the ice-damaged valve at 10:40 a.m. The release quickly formed a flammable vapor cloud that drifted toward a nearby fired heater, where it most likely ignited. Two operators, wearing standard flame-resistant coveralls, were simultaneously performing emergency response tasks in the fired heater area and became engulfed in the colorless, odorless portion of the vapor cloud; both were injured when it exploded. Suncor estimated $40 million in property damage, and the incident contributed to a refinery-wide shutdown for inspection and repairs that lasted until the end of March 2023.

Root Causes

  • A temporary dead leg was never identified and protected against freezing: Suncor's dead-leg identification and management program failed to catch this specific piece of isolated piping, leaving water inside it exposed to freeze-thaw conditions for 190 days without anyone flagging the risk.
  • Water froze inside the isolated piping during extreme cold: with no flow to keep it moving and no protective measures in place, standing water in the dead leg froze solid as ambient temperatures dropped below freezing over several days.
  • The ice damaged a valve flange, and thawing released hydrocarbons: as temperatures rose and the ice began melting, the previously frozen and expanded piping had already been damaged enough that flammable hydrocarbons escaped through the compromised flange.
  • Suncor's emergency response procedure relied on workers' senses to judge safety: the company's policy told operators to "eliminate any source of ignition if it can be done safely," which in practice meant two operators tried to judge, using sight and smell alone, when it was safe to approach a partly invisible, odorless vapor cloud, and they were engulfed when it ignited.

Lessons Learned

  1. Dead-leg identification programs need to actively catch every instance of isolated, stagnant piping created during maintenance, not just the intentional, permanent dead legs a facility already tracks. This one existed for 190 days without being flagged.
  2. Isolated piping containing water needs freeze protection during cold weather regardless of how temporary the isolation is expected to be, since a "temporary" maintenance lockout provided no actual time limit on how long the pipe sat exposed to winter conditions.
  3. Emergency response procedures that ask workers to personally judge when it's safe to approach a flammable vapor cloud, especially one that may be partially invisible and odorless, are asking people to do something no human sense can reliably verify. Clear, absolute stand-off criteria protect responders in a way that relying on sight or smell cannot.
  4. A vapor cloud drifting toward an ignition source like a fired heater needs to be treated as an active, developing emergency requiring evacuation, not a condition operators should approach to try to eliminate the ignition source themselves.
