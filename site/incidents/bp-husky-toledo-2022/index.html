BP-Husky Toledo Refinery Naphtha Fire

Oregon, OH, USA · 2 fatalities, 0 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisOperating ProceduresIncident InvestigationTraining

What Happened

Throughout the day before the incident, the BP-Husky Toledo Refinery experienced a cascading series of process upsets. Water accumulated in a crude unit's overhead accumulator drum, forcing an overflow of liquid into other processing equipment and requiring naphtha to be redirected to alternate storage. During one of these transfers, naphtha leaked into a fuel gas mix drum, a vessel designed to hold only vapor. As the drum's level rose, operators responding to a high-level alarm began draining the liquid, first to the flare and sewer systems, then directly to the ground once those routes proved insufficient. Two brothers, both refinery employees, Ben and Max Morrissey, were draining naphtha from the drum when an approaching rainstorm shifted wind direction, pushing the resulting vapor cloud toward a nearby crude oil furnace. The cloud ignited, causing a flash fire that fatally injured both men. In the twelve hours before the incident, board operators had been managing an "alarm flood" of more than 3,700 alarms, a volume CSB found overwhelmed their ability to respond effectively. The incident caused approximately $597 million in property damage, making it the largest fatal incident at a BP-operated U.S. refinery since the 2005 Texas City disaster.

Root Causes

  • Liquid naphtha overflowed into a vessel designed to hold only vapor: the refinery's existing safeguards, including safety instrumented systems and emergency pressure-relief valves, did not prevent naphtha from filling the fuel gas mix drum during the day's cascading process upsets.
  • An alarm flood overwhelmed operators: more than 3,700 alarms sounded in the twelve hours before the fire, a volume CSB found made it extremely difficult for board operators to identify and respond to the alarms that actually mattered most.
  • The refinery never implemented a shutdown in time: despite a day of escalating process problems, operators did not initiate a unit shutdown before the situation progressed to the point of a fatal release.
  • A nearly identical 2019 incident had already provided a warning: naphtha had previously filled the same vessel following an earlier refinery-wide process upset. BP's internal investigation of that event identified warning signs but did not develop corrective actions that would have prevented this recurrence.

Lessons Learned

  1. Alarm management systems need to actively prevent alarm floods, or at minimum help operators triage which alarms demand immediate action, rather than presenting thousands of undifferentiated alerts during exactly the high-stress period when a clear signal matters most.
  2. A vessel designed to hold only vapor needs safeguards specifically verified to prevent liquid overflow under realistic upset conditions, not just under normal operation. This incident's root cause was exactly the scenario those safeguards were meant to catch.
  3. When a facility experiences a non-catastrophic near-miss, like the 2019 naphtha overflow here, the resulting internal investigation needs to produce real corrective action, not just documentation that a warning sign existed. CSB explicitly called this a missed opportunity to prevent the fatal 2022 event.
  4. Extended, cascading process upsets over many hours deserve an explicit decision point for shutting down rather than continuing to manage an escalating situation. The refinery's failure to shut down in time, despite a full day of worsening conditions, is this incident's clearest single lesson.
