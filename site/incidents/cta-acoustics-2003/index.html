Anniversary: February 20, 2003

CTA Acoustics Dust Explosion and Fire

Corbin, KY, USA · 7 fatalities, 37 injuries
Process Safety InformationProcess Hazard AnalysisCompliance Audits

What Happened

At an acoustic insulation plant that impregnated fiberglass mat with phenolic resin using heated ovens, a curing oven had been left open due to an unresolved temperature control problem. Workers were cleaning the area near the oven, stirring up a cloud of combustible phenolic resin dust, which the open oven's heat most likely ignited. A series of dust explosions and fire tore through the plant, killing seven workers, injuring 37, and destroying the facility. The building itself was never designed to limit dust explosion severity, it lacked features like minimized flat surfaces where dust could settle, or fire walls to separate production lines and contain a blast to one area.

Root Causes

  • The dust hazard was known but never communicated to workers: CSB found the company was aware phenolic resin dust could explode, but never communicated this to the workforce or adjusted operating procedures or facility design accordingly.
  • The chemical supplier withheld a directly relevant prior warning: Borden Chemical, which supplied the resin, was aware of a nearly identical 1999 dust explosion at a different facility (Jahn Foundry) involving a similar resin, but never passed that safety lesson on to CTA, despite documentation showing Borden knew about it.
  • Facility design did not account for dust explosion severity: the plant's design predated modern combustible dust practice and lacked basic mitigation features that limit how far and how badly a dust explosion can propagate once started.
  • No regulatory inspection ever caught the hazard: state OSHA inspectors had visited the facility multiple times without citing the combustible dust risk, the state fire marshal's office had never inspected the 32-year-old plant at all, and years of insurance inspections also failed to flag phenolic resin dust as an explosion hazard.

Lessons Learned

  1. Chemical and material suppliers carry real responsibility for passing along safety-relevant incident history, if you supply a hazardous material, a documented explosion involving that material at another customer's site is information the next customer needs, not proprietary knowledge to sit on.
  2. A known internal hazard that never reaches the workforce is functionally the same as an unknown hazard, hazard communication isn't complete until it's actually reached the people doing the work.
  3. Facility and equipment design should proactively limit dust explosion propagation (minimizing dust-collecting surfaces, using fire walls between production areas) rather than relying entirely on preventing the initial ignition, a single ignition source will eventually occur; design should assume that and limit the consequence.
  4. Regulatory inspection gaps compound quietly: this plant went 32 years without a single fire marshal inspection, and multiple OSHA visits missed the hazard entirely, don't assume "we've been inspected and passed" means every relevant hazard was actually evaluated.
