Wacker Polysilicon Hydrogen Chloride Release

Charleston, TN, USA · 1 fatalities, 4 injuries
Operating ProceduresContractorsIncident Investigation

What Happened

Seven contract workers from two different firms were performing two unrelated maintenance tasks simultaneously on the fifth floor of a five-story equipment access platform. Three workers were assigned to torque flange bolts on a heat exchanger outlet pipe carrying hydrogen chloride (HCl) gas, wearing full-body chemical-resistant suits; four others from a separate company were repairing insulation nearby, wearing only flame-resistant clothing, not chemical protection. One of the bolt-torquing workers applied excessive force to a flange bolt, cracking the pipe and releasing a rapidly expanding cloud of HCl gas across the platform. The platform had only one way down, a staircase, and as the gas cloud spread, workers lost visibility of their surroundings and couldn't reach it. Three of the workers not wearing chemical-resistant suits, desperate to escape the gas, began climbing down the outside of the structure instead. All three fell approximately 70 feet; one died from the fall, and two sustained serious injuries. CSB found that three months before the incident, Wacker's own employees had identified the need for a second means of egress from the platform, but no action had been taken on that recommendation before the fatal event.

Root Causes

  • No written procedure for the torquing task: the contract pipefitters were tasked with torquing bolts on a hazardous-service pipe with multiple bolts carrying different torque requirements, without documented, verified instructions, a gap that directly enabled the over-torquing that cracked the pipe.
  • Ineffective control of hazardous energy: CSB found the broader hazardous energy control process around this maintenance activity was inadequate to prevent the kind of accidental overpressure/over-torque event that occurred.
  • Uncontrolled simultaneous operations: two separate contractor crews, from two different firms, performing two unrelated tasks in the same elevated, confined area were not adequately coordinated, meaning workers without chemical protection ended up directly exposed to a hazard from a task they weren't even involved in.
  • A single means of egress from an elevated platform handling hazardous chemicals: the platform's only exit route was a staircase that became unusable once the gas cloud obscured visibility, leaving workers with no safe way down.
  • A known corrective action was never implemented: Wacker employees had specifically flagged the need for a second egress point three months before the incident, this recommendation sat unaddressed until it became directly relevant to a fatality.

Lessons Learned

  1. Any task involving mechanical work on hazardous-service piping (like bolt torquing) needs a written, verified procedure, especially when different bolts on the same assembly may require different specifications, which is exactly the kind of detail informal instruction is prone to missing.
  2. Simultaneous operations by different crews doing unrelated work in the same physical area need active coordination and hazard communication, a crew working on an insulation project has every right to know a nearby crew is handling hazardous chemical piping, and to have matching protective equipment or physical separation.
  3. Elevated platforms or confined work areas handling hazardous materials need more than one means of emergency egress, a single exit route that can be blocked by the very hazard it's meant to help people escape is a critical design gap.
  4. An internally identified safety recommendation, like the need for a second egress point, needs a tracked, resourced path to implementation, not indefinite deferral. This incident is a direct, tragic case of a known fix sitting unaddressed until it became the difference between survival and a fatal fall.
