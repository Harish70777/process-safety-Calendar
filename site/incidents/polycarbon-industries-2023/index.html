Polycarbon Industries Pressure Filter Explosion

Newburyport, MA, USA · 1 fatalities, 0 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisMechanical IntegrityProcess Safety Information

What Happened

At approximately 12:40 a.m. on May 4, 2023, a pressure Nutsche filter vessel exploded at the Polycarbon Industries facility in Newburyport, Massachusetts, during production of a substance called Dekon 139. The material had already been synthesized, and excess liquid had been removed from the solid product, or "cake," inside a nitrogen-inerted vessel; an agitator was being used to smooth the cake and break up lumps that formed during drying. Unknown to the company at the time, the agitator had loosened and was rubbing against a plate at the bottom of the vessel, generating heat through friction. This friction created a localized hot spot in the Dekon that reached the temperature needed to trigger decomposition, a reaction the company later learned, through post-incident testing, could become self-accelerating once Dekon was heated to 280 degrees Fahrenheit, releasing flammable gases including hydrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide. The decomposition reaction released energy that raised the surrounding material's temperature further, causing more Dekon to decompose in a runaway sequence. The resulting gases rapidly increased pressure inside the vessel, first forcing open its discharge door and igniting a weak initial explosion when the released gas and dust mixed with atmospheric oxygen, then triggering a larger second explosion when the vessel's rupture disc opened and the vessel itself failed. One Polycarbon operator was unable to escape the area and was fatally injured. The incident caused approximately $48 million in property damage and led to the permanent closure of the facility.

Root Causes

  • The agitator loosened and generated friction heat against the vessel's base plate: this mechanical degradation went undetected before the incident and created the localized hot spot that ultimately triggered the reaction.
  • Polycarbon had incomplete knowledge of Dekon's decomposition hazard: the company only learned through post-incident testing that Dekon could undergo self-accelerating decomposition at 280 degrees Fahrenheit, meaning this specific reactive hazard wasn't understood or controlled for during normal operation.
  • The decomposition reaction was self-accelerating once initiated: the reaction's own heat output drove further decomposition, creating a runaway sequence that generated pressure and flammable gas far beyond what could be controlled once it began.
  • The affected operator was unable to escape the immediate area: whatever combination of location, timing, and the explosion's rapid escalation prevented the fatally injured worker from reaching safety before the vessel failed.

Lessons Learned

  1. Agitator and mechanical component condition inside process vessels needs monitoring or inspection sufficient to catch developing looseness or misalignment before it generates enough friction heat to become an ignition source for reactive material.
  2. Reactive chemical hazard testing needs to happen before a compound is put into full production use, not discovered afterward through post-incident investigation. Polycarbon's understanding of Dekon's decomposition temperature only came after the fatal explosion had already occurred.
  3. Self-accelerating decomposition reactions can escalate from a localized hot spot to a vessel-destroying event extremely quickly, meaning prevention (avoiding the initiating condition entirely) matters far more than any response once the reaction has begun.
  4. Vessels handling materials with any reactive decomposition potential need engineering controls, monitoring, and emergency venting specifically sized for a worst-case runaway scenario, and facility layout needs to ensure workers in the area have a genuinely viable escape path if such a reaction does begin.
