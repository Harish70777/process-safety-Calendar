Anniversary: December 7, 2009

NDK Crystal Pressure Vessel Explosion with Offsite Fatality

Belvidere, IL, USA · 1 fatalities, 1 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityIncident InvestigationProcess Hazard Analysis

What Happened

NDK Crystal manufactured synthetic quartz crystals inside eight high-pressure vessels using caustic sodium hydroxide solution at extreme temperature and pressure. In January 2007, the company discovered stress corrosion cracking in four of the eight vessel lids; a consultant retained by NDK's own insurance company examined the vessels and expressed "serious reservations" about returning them to service, explicitly warning of possible danger to the public. NDK did not verify the integrity of the vessels' protective coating and returned them to service anyway without independently testing them. Nearly three years later, Vessel #2 catastrophically ruptured. A piece of the building structure was blown 650 feet and struck a truck driver walking back to his vehicle at a highway rest stop, killing him. A separate vessel fragment weighing over 8,000 pounds tore through a wall, crossed a neighboring parking lot, and struck an automotive supply company where 70 people were working, one of whom was injured. Roughly 800–850 gallons of sodium hydroxide solution were released in the explosion. The facility never reopened.

Root Causes

  • Stress corrosion cracking, identified years in advance and not resolved: the specific failure mechanism that ultimately caused the rupture had already been detected in 2007, in this exact vessel type, with an explicit third-party warning about the danger.
  • A direct, documented warning was ignored: NDK's own insurance consultant recommended against returning the vessels to service without further verification, the company proceeded anyway.
  • No vessel testing before returning to service: having been warned, NDK still did not independently test or verify the vessels' condition or the effectiveness of the protective coating meant to guard against exactly this corrosion mechanism.
  • Years without proper internal inspection: state inspections that did occur (in 2003, 2006, and just months before the explosion in 2009) focused only on externally accessible surfaces and never examined the vessel interiors where the corrosion was actually occurring, partly because the vessels had been incorrectly classified as non-corrosive service.
  • Inadequate facility siting review during permitting: CSB found no documentation that the city's zoning and permitting process ever considered the offsite consequences of operating eight high-pressure vessels this close to a highway rest stop and neighboring businesses, the pressure vessels weren't even included in the permit application details.

Lessons Learned

  1. A documented, explicit third-party warning about vessel safety is about as unambiguous a signal as an organization will ever receive, treat it as an operational stop-and-verify trigger, not one input among many to weigh against production pressure.
  2. Returning known-compromised equipment to service without independent testing, after already being warned, converts a known risk into an accepted one, the accountability for what happens next shifts entirely onto that decision.
  3. Vessel classification (corrosive vs. non-corrosive service) needs to be verified against actual operating chemistry, not assumed, a misclassification that exempts a vessel from meaningful internal inspection removes the exact safeguard most likely to catch a developing failure.
  4. Facility siting reviews need to explicitly account for high-hazard equipment even when it isn't the headline feature of a permit application, this incident's fatality occurred entirely outside the facility's own fence line, a stark reminder that consequence distance for high-pressure equipment failures can be very large and needs proactive land-use consideration.
