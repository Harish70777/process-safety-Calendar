Anniversary: January 19, 2004

Skikda LNG Complex Explosion

Skikda, Algeria · 27 fatalities, 74 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityIncident InvestigationProcess Hazard Analysis

What Happened

At approximately 6:40 p.m. on January 19, 2004, a massive explosion occurred at a liquefied natural gas (LNG) production train at the Sonatrach-owned Skikda complex on Algeria's Mediterranean coast, one of the world's largest LNG export facilities. A large hydrocarbon leak was ingested into the facility's main boiler firebox, where it ignited and breached the boiler. The resulting boiler explosion ignited an extensive vapor cloud that had formed around the LNG train, triggering a much larger secondary explosion. The blast destroyed three of the complex's six LNG production trains, badly damaged a nearby berth used for loading LNG tankers, overturned parked security vehicles and ambulances with heat intense enough to melt their metal structures, and blew out windows in nearby homes. Twenty-seven workers were killed, and 74 others were injured, most with facial injuries; foreign nationals from France and Turkey were among the injured. The explosion disabled roughly 2 percent of the world's total LNG liquefaction capacity and pushed global oil prices to a 10-month high. The head of security at the plant told state radio that he had heard vibrations and irregular noise at the boiler just before the blast, and workers said they had warned a year earlier that the boiler was faulty and that an accident would happen, but the boiler had only received a superficial repair rather than a genuine fix. An official Algerian government inquiry later determined a failed gas pipeline, rather than the boiler itself, was the root cause, though the boiler served as the ignition point that turned the leak into a catastrophic explosion.

Root Causes

  • A large hydrocarbon leak was ingested into the boiler firebox: the leak, originating from a failed gas pipeline according to the official investigation, found its way into the boiler's combustion chamber, where it ignited.
  • The boiler had been known as defective for roughly a year before the explosion: workers and plant security personnel had already flagged the boiler's faulty condition, warning specifically that an accident could result.
  • Only a superficial repair had been made to the known defect: despite the warning, whatever repair was performed on the boiler didn't address the underlying problem thoroughly enough to prevent the eventual failure.
  • The boiler explosion ignited a much larger vapor cloud: once the boiler failed, the resulting explosion ignited an extensive hydrocarbon vapor cloud that had accumulated around the LNG train, escalating a contained equipment failure into a catastrophic, wide-area explosion.

Lessons Learned

  1. Worker reports of a known defective condition, especially one specifically flagged as likely to cause an accident, need a genuine root-cause repair, not a superficial fix that leaves the underlying problem in place. This warning existed a full year before the explosion occurred.
  2. Boilers and other combustion equipment located near hydrocarbon-handling processes need protection against ingesting leaked hydrocarbon vapor, since this ingestion was the specific mechanism that turned a leak into an ignition source.
  3. LNG facility design needs engineering controls that prevent a single equipment failure, like this boiler explosion, from being able to ignite a much larger vapor cloud accumulation nearby. The scale of this disaster came specifically from that escalation.
  4. This incident remains one of the most significant accidents in the history of the LNG industry, and its causal chain, a known defect, an inadequate repair, and a catastrophic consequence, is a pattern that recurs across many of the largest industrial disasters in this database, regardless of the specific industry or country involved.
