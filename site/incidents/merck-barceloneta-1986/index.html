Anniversary: June 12, 1986

Merck Sharp and Dohme Barceloneta Tank Explosion

Barceloneta, PR, USA · 3 fatalities, 5 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityTrainingProcess Safety Information

What Happened

At approximately 6:00 a.m. on June 12, 1986, a chemical storage tank exploded at the Merck Sharp and Dohme Química de Puerto Rico plant in Barceloneta, part of a four-plant pharmaceutical manufacturing complex roughly 40 miles west of San Juan. The plant produced methyldopa, an ingredient in the blood pressure medication Aldomet. The explosion ignited a fire that spread quickly through the building. Three workers were killed, and at least five others were seriously injured, several with severe burns. Residents as far as 15 miles away reported smelling fumes from the explosion, and government officials expressed concern that the local water supply might be contaminated with cyanide released during the incident. The damaged plant, one of four at the complex, sustained an estimated $10 to $15 million in damage and was shut down for approximately two years for reconstruction; the complex's other plants continued normal operations. Merck sent an investigation team from its Rahway, New Jersey headquarters to examine the cause. Subsequent civil litigation alleged that a carbon disk component, manufactured by a separate company and used as part of the tank's design, contributed to the explosion, and that Merck had negligently designed, installed, modified, operated, and maintained the tank, in addition to failing to adequately train employees in its proper use and maintenance.

Root Causes

  • A tank component failure contributed directly to the explosion: civil litigation following the incident specifically alleged that a carbon disk installed as part of the tank assembly contributed to the failure that triggered the explosion.
  • The tank's design, installation, and maintenance were called into question: plaintiffs in subsequent litigation alleged the tank had been negligently designed, installed, modified, operated, and maintained, pointing to systemic gaps rather than a single isolated failure.
  • Employee training on the tank's proper use and maintenance was alleged to be inadequate: the same litigation specifically claimed Química failed to adequately train its employees in how to properly use and maintain the tank involved in the explosion.
  • Cyanide-containing byproducts created a secondary community hazard: beyond the immediate explosion and fire, officials had to address concern that cyanide compounds released during the incident could contaminate the local water supply, extending the incident's risk profile beyond the plant boundary.

Lessons Learned

  1. Individual tank components, like the carbon disk alleged to have contributed here, need verification that they're rated and suited for the specific service conditions of the vessel they're installed in, since a single component failure can be the direct trigger for a catastrophic tank failure.
  2. Tank design, installation, and ongoing maintenance need periodic, documented review, especially for vessels handling chemicals with hazardous byproduct potential like cyanide-generating processes.
  3. Employee training on the specific equipment they operate and maintain needs to be thorough and verifiable, not generic. Litigation following this incident specifically alleged the training gap was a contributing factor.
  4. Chemical processes capable of generating cyanide or other water-contaminating byproducts need incident response plans that address community water supply protection specifically, not just the immediate fire and explosion hazard at the facility itself.
