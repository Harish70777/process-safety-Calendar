Anniversary: January 24, 2020

Watson Grinding and Manufacturing Propylene Explosion

Houston, TX, USA · 3 fatalities, 18 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityTrainingProcess Hazard Analysis

What Happened

Overnight, a degraded and poorly crimped rubber welding hose came off its fitting at a propylene storage tank used for the facility's thermal spray coating operations. A manual shutoff valve at the tank had also not been closed at the end of the previous workday. Propylene, a flammable gas, leaked steadily through the night and accumulated inside the building. When employees arrived shortly before 4:30 a.m. and one turned on a light switch, the gas ignited, causing a massive explosion that destroyed the building and scattered debris up to half a mile away. Two employees were killed immediately; a nearby resident whose home was struck by debris died from his injuries about two weeks later. 18 people sought emergency room treatment for injuries, and roughly 450 nearby homes and businesses were damaged. The facility had experienced a strikingly similar propylene leak and explosion in 2008, which injured two workers and damaged the coating building at the time, leading the company to construct an entirely new coating building, though the underlying gaps in detection, training, and emergency response evidently persisted.

Root Causes

  • Degraded equipment left in service: the rubber welding hose that failed had deteriorated to the point of poor crimping and eventual failure, without being caught and replaced through adequate mechanical integrity inspection.
  • A manual shutoff valve was left open overnight: the propylene supply was not isolated at the end of the workday, meaning any downstream failure, like the hose disconnection, had an open, uninterrupted path to release gas for hours.
  • Gas detection and shutdown systems were not functioning: CSB found that a gas detection alarm, exhaust fan startup system, and automatic gas shutoff system were all not working properly, removing multiple layers of protection that should have caught the leak well before ignition.
  • Inadequate worker training on the specific hazard: employees had not been effectively trained on the hazards of propylene, and on the morning of the incident were not instructed to evacuate, prevent others from entering, or contact emergency responders when a release was suspected.
  • The facility fell below regulatory coverage thresholds: the quantity of propylene stored on-site did not meet the 10,000-pound threshold that would have triggered EPA Risk Management Program or OSHA Process Safety Management coverage, leaving the company without a legal requirement to implement a comprehensive safety management program.
  • Facility siting placed a hazardous process near residential homes: the coating facility operated in an area without zoning separation from nearby residences, meaning a process failure had a direct path to affect the surrounding community, as it tragically did.

Lessons Learned

  1. End-of-shift procedures for hazardous gas systems need a verified, positive isolation step, leaving a manual valve's position to habit or assumption is a direct invitation for any downstream failure to become an uncontrolled overnight release.
  2. Gas detection, ventilation, and automatic shutoff systems are only safeguards if they're verified functional, a non-working detection system provides zero actual protection while creating false confidence that the hazard would be caught.
  3. A near-identical prior incident at the same facility (2008) should trigger a fundamental review of detection, training, and procedural gaps, rebuilding the physical structure without addressing the underlying safety management gaps left the same vulnerability in place for over a decade.
  4. Regulatory threshold exemptions don't reduce the actual hazard, a facility handling flammable gas near occupied structures needs genuine hazard analysis and emergency planning regardless of whether it legally triggers PSM or RMP coverage; the consequence to a nearby resident in this incident makes that point starkly.
