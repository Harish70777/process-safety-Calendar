Anniversary: September 10, 1997

Georgia-Pacific Resins Columbus Runaway Reaction Explosion

Columbus, OH, USA · 1 fatalities, 4 injuries
Process Hazard AnalysisFacility SitingOperating Procedures

What Happened

On September 10, 1997, an explosion occurred in a phenol-formaldehyde resin reactor at the Georgia-Pacific Resins plant on the south side of Columbus, Ohio, a facility located in a residential neighborhood. The explosion resulted from a runaway exothermic reaction, where heat generation in the reactor exceeded the system's ability to remove it, causing temperature and pressure to increase uncontrollably until the vessel failed. One worker was killed and four others were injured. The explosion spread toxins across the adjacent residential area, and local residents were evacuated for approximately five hours as a precaution. The manufacture of phenolic resins using this chemistry had already produced documented incidents dating back to 1957, meaning the specific reactive hazard behind this explosion was well established in the industry literature long before this event occurred. Residents in the surrounding South Side neighborhood had already experienced years of hazardous releases from the facility prior to the explosion, reporting recurring symptoms including burning eyes and nostrils, shortness of breath, and fatigue, along with community concern about elevated local rates of lung and liver cancer. Following the explosion, community organizing efforts and subsequent litigation resulted in a $22 million settlement.

Root Causes

  • A runaway exothermic reaction developed in the resin reactor: heat generated by the phenol-formaldehyde reaction exceeded what the reactor's cooling system could remove, driving temperature and pressure beyond the vessel's safe limits until it failed.
  • The specific reactive hazard was well documented in industry literature decades before this incident: phenol-formaldehyde runaway reactions had already caused recorded incidents dating back to 1957, meaning this wasn't a previously unknown or unanticipated chemistry.
  • The facility was sited directly within a residential neighborhood: placing a reactive chemical process this close to homes meant that any failure would directly and immediately affect residents, as happened when toxins spread across the adjacent area.
  • A pattern of hazardous releases had already affected the community for years: residents reported ongoing exposure symptoms well before this explosion, indicating the facility's process safety and emissions control had already been a recognized concern in the years leading up to the incident.

Lessons Learned

  1. A reactive hazard documented in industry literature for decades, as phenol-formaldehyde runaway reactions were here, needs to be treated as an established, known risk requiring specific engineering controls, not something each facility discovers independently through its own incident.
  2. Facility siting decisions for processes with reactive hazard potential need to account for the realistic worst-case consequence to nearby residential communities, not just the facility's own property line.
  3. A pattern of recurring hazardous releases affecting a community, even short of a catastrophic incident, is itself a strong signal that underlying process safety and containment issues exist and deserve remediation before a more severe event occurs.
  4. Emergency venting and reactor protection systems for exothermic reactions need sizing and design specifically verified against the actual worst-case runaway scenario for that chemistry, given the well-established literature on how quickly a phenol-formaldehyde reaction can escalate once cooling capacity is exceeded.
