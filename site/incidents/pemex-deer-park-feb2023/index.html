Pemex Deer Park Heat Exchanger Startup Bleed Valve Fire

Deer Park, TX, USA · 0 fatalities, 0 injuries
Operating ProceduresMechanical IntegrityProcess Safety Information

What Happened

At 8:15 p.m. on February 23, 2023, approximately 164 pounds of hydrocarbons, mostly pentane and heavier compounds, were accidentally released from an open bleed valve on a crude unit heat exchanger during startup at the PEMEX Deer Park Refinery in Texas. The hot hydrocarbons autoignited, causing a fire that damaged nearby equipment and cost an estimated $2.3 million in property damage. Ten days earlier, the affected heat exchangers had been shut down for cleaning. Day shift operators purged air from the exchangers in preparation for restart, and night shift operators continued readying the equipment for startup, using an energy isolation drawing documenting which valves had been locked open or closed for the cleaning work. The operators walked down the equipment, removed the locks specified on the drawing, and believed they had returned every valve to its correct startup position. Unknown to them, however, a bleed valve on top of the heat exchangers remained locked open when it should have been closed, and the lock should have been removed and the valve shut. The valve was not easy to locate visually, and darkness likely further impaired the operators' ability to see it during the walkdown. As the startup sequence progressed, hot hydrocarbons flowing to the heat exchangers passed through this open bleed valve into the atmosphere, where they ignited at their operating temperature of 562 degrees Fahrenheit, well above their autoignition temperature of 482 degrees. The energy isolation drawing itself was destroyed in the fire, so investigators could not determine whether the open bleed valve had even been correctly documented on it in the first place.

Root Causes

  • A bleed valve remained locked open when it should have been closed for startup: this specific valve was missed during the process of restoring the heat exchangers to their normal operating configuration after cleaning.
  • The bleed valve was not easily visible during the walkdown: its location made visual verification difficult, a problem compounded by darkness during the night shift startup preparation.
  • It's unknown whether the energy isolation drawing correctly documented this valve: because the drawing was destroyed in the fire, investigators could not determine whether the gap originated in the documentation itself or in how operators executed it.
  • No leak testing was performed to verify all valves were correctly positioned before startup: the facility had no independent verification step, like a pressure or leak test, that could have caught the incorrectly positioned valve before hot hydrocarbons were introduced to the system.

Lessons Learned

  1. Energy isolation drawings and valve lineups for startup need to be clear and specific enough that every valve position can be unambiguously verified, especially for valves that are physically difficult to see or access.
  2. Startup preparation performed in low-light or nighttime conditions needs additional verification measures, like flashlights, physical tags, or a second-person check, given the specific way reduced visibility contributed to this valve being missed.
  3. A physical walkdown alone, while valuable, isn't a complete verification method for confirming correct valve positions before startup. A leak test or other independent check provides a second layer of assurance that doesn't depend solely on visual inspection.
  4. Critical safety documents like energy isolation drawings need backup copies or a system ensuring they survive to support post-incident investigation, since this drawing's destruction in the fire it may have helped explain left investigators unable to determine a key fact about the incident's root cause.
