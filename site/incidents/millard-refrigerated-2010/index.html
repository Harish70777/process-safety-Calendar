Anniversary: August 23, 2010

Millard Refrigerated Services Ammonia Release

Theodore, AL, USA · 0 fatalities, 152 injuries
Mechanical IntegrityProcess Hazard AnalysisIncident Investigation

What Happened

The evening before the incident, Millard's cold storage facility lost power for more than seven hours, shutting down its anhydrous ammonia refrigeration system. When power returned the next morning, an operator troubleshooting the restart cleared alarms in the control system, which reset a group of freezer evaporators mid-cycle — switching them directly from a step in the defrost cycle into refrigeration mode while hot, high-pressure gas still remained in the evaporator coil. That combination of hot gas and suddenly reintroduced liquid ammonia in the same line produced "hydraulic shock" — a sudden, localized pressure surge caused by a rapid change in flow velocity — which the piping and equipment weren't built to survive. A suction header cracked on the roof and an evaporator coil failed inside one freezer, releasing roughly 32,000 pounds of anhydrous ammonia into the atmosphere. Three employees went onto the roof and took about four hours to manually close the valves supplying ammonia to the affected 24,000-square-foot blast freezer. The ammonia cloud drifted directly over a nearby site where more than 800 people were working on decontaminating ships that had responded to the Deepwater Horizon oil spill cleanup. A one-mile shelter-in-place and evacuation was ordered; 152 people were treated for ammonia exposure symptoms at Mobile-area hospitals, 32 of them hospitalized and 4 requiring intensive care.

Root Causes

  • Hydraulic shock from an improper restart sequence: resetting the evaporator directly from defrost into refrigeration mode, while hot gas remained trapped in the coil, created exactly the flow-velocity conditions known to cause a destructive pressure surge in ammonia piping.
  • No procedure guarding the post-outage restart: the operator's troubleshooting decisions during a non-routine power restoration weren't governed by a documented, hazard-reviewed restart procedure that could have prevented resetting equipment into an unsafe state.
  • This was the facility's third ammonia release, not its first: the company had experienced two earlier releases (2007 and January 2010) at the same site from related causes, and corrective actions taken after those incidents didn't prevent this larger one.
  • No remote emergency isolation: stopping the release required employees to physically climb onto the roof and manually close valves, a process that took roughly four hours — far longer than a remotely operated isolation system would have taken.

Lessons Learned

  1. Restarting refrigeration or process equipment after a power outage is a distinct hazard scenario that deserves its own documented, verified procedure — the sequence of coming back online safely is not the same as normal steady-state operation, and treating it casually is exactly how this release happened.
  2. Repeated smaller incidents at the same facility (two prior ammonia releases here) are a direct signal that underlying corrective actions haven't actually closed the gap — a third, larger release at the same site is the clearest possible evidence that "we fixed it" wasn't true.
  3. Remote emergency isolation capability matters especially for facilities near populated areas or active operations — a four-hour manual valve-closing effort, while ammonia continued releasing the entire time, is a long window for consequences to escalate.
  4. Hazardous material releases don't respect what else happens to be nearby — this incident's exposure to responders working an unrelated environmental disaster is a reminder that facility risk assessments should account for whatever populations could realistically be in range, not just routine neighboring land use.
