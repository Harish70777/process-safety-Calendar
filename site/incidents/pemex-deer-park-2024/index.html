PEMEX Deer Park Refinery Hydrogen Sulfide Release

Deer Park, TX, USA · 2 fatalities, 13 injuries
Operating ProceduresProcess Safety InformationContractorsTraining

What Happened

During maintenance activities in the refinery's Amine Unit, contract workers from Repcon, Inc. mistakenly opened a flange on piping that contained pressurized hydrogen sulfide, a highly toxic gas, when they had actually been assigned to open a different flange located about five feet away. One Repcon worker was fatally injured by the immediate release. The hydrogen sulfide vapor then traveled downwind into an adjacent unit, where a worker employed by a separate contractor (ISC) inhaled the gas and was also fatally injured. The release continued for nearly an hour until refinery emergency responders were able to reassemble the leaking flange and stop the discharge. Over 27,000 pounds of hydrogen sulfide were released in total; 13 workers were transported to local medical facilities and dozens more were treated at the scene. Local officials in the neighboring cities of Deer Park and Pasadena issued shelter-in-place orders that remained in effect for several hours.

Root Causes

  • No reliable way to identify the correct equipment: CSB found the refinery lacked an effective method to clearly distinguish the correct flange before work began — drawings and flange lists weren't precise enough to tell nearly identical piping segments apart, and the identification tag for the correct flange was placed out of view.
  • An overly broad work permit: the permit covering the job spanned multiple tasks with varying hazards and no clear hold points, and failed to address the specific hazard of opening piping in an operational unit immediately upwind of other contractors.
  • Contractors reassigned without a hazard briefing: on the day of the incident, workers were abruptly moved from a fully shut-down unit to a partially operational one containing hydrogen sulfide — the proximity of the two areas led them to believe they were still in the safe, shut-down environment, and they weren't specifically told otherwise.
  • A gap between written policy and actual practice: CSB found the refinery's procedures generally aligned with industry standards on paper, but management and operations personnel routinely misunderstood or deviated from them in practice.

Lessons Learned

  1. Positive equipment identification — a clear, verified way to confirm you're opening the *correct* piping before you do it — isn't optional for hazardous service lines. CSB explicitly noted that opening the wrong equipment is a common failure mode across the chemical and refining industry, and that no industry-wide standard currently addresses it directly.
  2. Work permits need to be scoped tightly enough to actually name the specific hazards of the specific task — a broad permit covering "multiple jobs with varying hazards" gives workers less protection than several narrower, clearly-scoped ones.
  3. Reassigning contractors between areas with meaningfully different hazard profiles requires an explicit, deliberate briefing every time — proximity between a shut-down area and an active one is exactly the condition that lets workers carry a false sense of safety with them into a genuinely hazardous space.
  4. A safety program that looks good on paper isn't the same as one that's actually followed — CSB's finding of a gap between written procedure and real practice is a reminder that policies need active verification (audits, direct observation), not just documentation, to actually function as safeguards.
